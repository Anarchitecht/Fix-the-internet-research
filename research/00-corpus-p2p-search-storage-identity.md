# Peer-to-peer search, storage, and identity: measured overhead of each mechanism, and an integrated design

## Distributed hash table lookup takes seconds in deployed networks

A distributed hash table — abbreviated DHT — lets any participant determine which computer stores a given piece of data, with no directory server. Each participant and each piece of data receives an identifier drawn from one range of numbers. The participant whose identifier is closest to a data item's identifier stores the location record for that item. To find an item, a participant sends the request to whichever contact in its routing table has an identifier closest to the item's identifier. Each recipient repeats that step, so the request reaches the responsible participant after a count of forwarding steps that grows as the logarithm of the participant count.

Kademlia, published by Petar Maymounkov and David Mazières at the International Workshop on Peer-to-Peer Systems in 2002, computes the distance between two 160-bit identifiers as the bitwise exclusive-or of those identifiers. Its routing table stores, for each distance range, a list of up to k contacts, and it retains long-lived contacts in preference to newly discovered ones. Kademlia runs in BitTorrent's trackerless mode, in the InterPlanetary File System through libp2p, and in Ethereum's discv5 node-discovery protocol.

Earlier and adjacent designs vary the routing-table structure. Chord (Stoica, Morris, Karger, Kaashoek, Balakrishnan, ACM SIGCOMM 2001) arranges identifiers on a ring and stores contacts at exponentially increasing distances, giving O(log n) forwarding steps with O(log n) contacts. Pastry (Rowstron and Druschel, IFIP/ACM Middleware 2001) and Tapestry (Zhao, Huang, Stribling, Rhea, Joseph, Kubiatowicz, IEEE JSAC 2004) route by matching successively longer identifier prefixes. The Content Addressable Network (Ratnasamy, Francis, Handley, Karp, Shenker, ACM SIGCOMM 2001) partitions a coordinate space of d dimensions and routes along the axes. Koorde (Kaashoek and Karger, IPTPS 2003) combines Chord with de Bruijn graphs and achieves O(log n) forwarding steps with only 2 contacts per participant, or O(log n / log log n) forwarding steps with O(log n) contacts. Viceroy (Malkhi, Naor, Ratajczak, ACM PODC 2002) emulates a butterfly network with a constant degree of 7 contacts per participant and O(log n) forwarding steps, and adding or removing a participant requires a constant count of link changes in expectation. Symphony (Manku, Bawa, Raghavan, USENIX USITS 2003) arranges identifiers on a ring and adds a small count of long-range links per participant, chosen from a harmonic distribution based on Kleinberg's small-world model, giving O(log²n / k) expected forwarding steps with k long-range links. Accordion (Li, Stribling, Morris, Kaashoek, Gil) learns contacts as a side effect of lookups and lets the participant set a bandwidth budget that controls how many contacts to maintain; the density of contacts is inversely proportional to their distance in identifier space, giving O(log n) worst-case forwarding steps at any table size and maintaining an efficient latency-versus-bandwidth tradeoff across varying churn rates. Bamboo (Rhea, Geels, Roscoe, Kubiatowicz, USENIX ATC 2004) uses Pastry-style prefix routing and focuses on churn resilience; the paper showed that periodic rather than reactive failure recovery, TCP-style timeout calculation, and proximity neighbor selection together produce consistent lookup latency under churn rates as high as those measured in Kazaa.

Under iterative routing the requesting participant contacts each successive forwarding step itself and observes every reply. Under recursive routing each step forwards the request on the requester's behalf, and only the final result returns. Iterative routing exposes every step to the requester, which lets the requester detect a step that returns a false result. Recursive routing completes in fewer round trips and therefore in less time. Proximity neighbor selection fills each routing-table entry with the network-closest of several eligible contacts, which reduces total lookup time without changing the count of forwarding steps.

In the identity-forgery attack — also called the Sybil attack, after John Douceur's paper at the International Workshop on Peer-to-Peer Systems in 2002 — one operator creates many identities and thereby controls a disproportionate share of the identifier range. In the eclipse attack, an adversary fills one victim's routing table with computers the adversary controls, so every request the victim issues reaches only the adversary. In routing and storage attacks, a participant on the path discards the request, returns a false location record, or refuses to serve stored data. Guido Urdaneta, Guillaume Pierre, and Maarten van Steen classified the defenses against all of these in "A Survey of DHT Security Techniques," ACM Computing Surveys 43(2), Article 8, January 2011, and concluded that protecting such systems against attack is notoriously difficult. Emil Sit and Robert Morris stated the attack classes in "Security Considerations for Peer-to-Peer Distributed Hash Tables." Miguel Castro and colleagues proposed certified identifier assignment, redundant routing along independent paths, and a routing failure test that detects when a lookup returned a wrong result, in "Secure routing for structured peer-to-peer overlay networks."

Trautwein and colleagues measured publication latency in the InterPlanetary File System in "IPFS in the Fast Lane" (2024). Writing one location record into the Kademlia table took 6.3 seconds at the median, 20.6 seconds at the 90th percentile, and 49.8 seconds at the 95th percentile. The iterative sequence of forwarding steps accounted for more than 90% of that time. An independent measurement of about 4 million content identifiers (arXiv 2212.07375) recorded an average retrieval latency for location records of about 6 seconds and a maximum of 1.5 hours. A user notices delay and loses attention above roughly one second, per the response-time thresholds in Google's RAIL model. An interactive application must therefore not require the user to wait for a DHT lookup. The client must answer from a cache, from an already-connected peer, or from a delegated lookup service whose answer the client verifies against the content hash.

**Selection: Kademlia with iterative routing, Accordion-style bandwidth budgeting, and Bamboo's churn findings applied.** Kademlia has three advantages over the alternatives. First, its XOR distance metric is symmetric — the distance from A to B equals the distance from B to A — which means every lookup reinforces routing-table entries in both directions, and every participant along the path learns about every other. Chord, Pastry, and Tapestry lack that symmetry. Second, Kademlia's k-bucket preference for long-lived contacts is a passive defense against churn: a routing table that favors stable contacts contains fewer stale entries without active probing. Third, Kademlia is the only DHT with deployment-scale measurement data (IPFS, BitTorrent, Ethereum), so its failure modes are observed rather than theoretical. Koorde's 2-contact minimum gives the lowest routing-table memory, but losing either contact isolates the participant, making Koorde fragile under real churn. Viceroy's constant degree of 7 is attractive, but its butterfly topology is harder to repair than Kademlia's k-buckets. Symphony's small-world links add latency variance because the harmonic distribution sometimes produces poor long-range contacts. Accordion's self-tuning bandwidth budget should be adopted into the Kademlia implementation: let each participant set its routing-table maintenance bandwidth, and fill the table with contacts at density inversely proportional to distance. Bamboo's three findings — periodic recovery, TCP-style timeouts, and proximity selection — should also be adopted, because those three changes together produced consistent lookup performance under Kazaa-level churn in Bamboo's experiments. Use iterative routing, not recursive, because iterative routing lets the querying participant detect and route around a malicious forwarding step.

## Unstructured query methods find widely held items at low cost and miss rare items

An unstructured overlay answers a query that states words rather than an exact identifier. Under query broadcast, a participant sends the query to every neighbor, and each neighbor forwards the query to every one of its own neighbors, up to a limit on forwarding depth. Under random walk, a participant sends the query to one randomly chosen neighbor, which forwards the query to one of its own neighbors, and so on. Neither method ensures that an item present in the network is found. Measurement of the Gnutella network established that query broadcast consumes bandwidth proportional to the participant count, and that a small fraction of participants carried most traffic. The response was the two-tier design, in which high-capacity participants store the indexes of many low-capacity participants and answer on their behalf.

Random walk encounters items held by many participants quickly, because many participants store a copy. Locating an item held by one participant requires contacting a large fraction of all participants. An architecture therefore cannot rely on unstructured search for rare content, and must retain exact-identifier lookup for rare content.

Plumtree builds a delivery tree for messages and retains the underlying random links to repair the tree when a participant departs. HyParView maintains a partial list of the membership at each participant and restores connectivity under high churn. SWIM — short for Scalable Weakly-consistent Infection-style process group Membership — separates failure detection from membership dissemination, so detecting a failed participant requires sending a constant count of messages regardless of network size. Secure Scuttlebutt, described by Dominic Tarr, Erick Lavoie, Aljoscha Meyer, and Christian Tschudin at the ACM Conference on Information-Centric Networking in 2019, replicates one append-only log per identity and delivers each log to participants within two social connections of its author.

**Selection: HyParView for membership, Plumtree for message dissemination, built on top of the Kademlia DHT.** HyParView gives each participant a small active view of live contacts and a larger passive view of candidates, and it recovers from partitions faster than SWIM because it actively replaces failed contacts from the passive view rather than waiting for infection-style propagation. Plumtree builds an efficient delivery tree from HyParView's active view and uses the passive links as gossip backup, so a tree failure causes temporary duplicate delivery rather than message loss. SWIM's failure-detection protocol should be adopted within HyParView for its constant-message-count property. Secure Scuttlebutt's friend-of-friend replication is too restrictive for discovery across a large network, but its append-only-log-per-identity data model is the right structure for user feeds and is adopted in the application layer.

## Peer-to-peer ranked full-text search exceeds participants' upload bandwidth at web scale

Jinyang Li, Boon Thau Loo, Joseph Hellerstein, Frans Kaashoek, David Karger, and Robert Morris analyzed the feasibility in "On the Feasibility of Peer-to-Peer Web Indexing and Search," International Workshop on Peer-to-Peer Systems, 2003. They analyzed query broadcast and DHT intersection of posting lists — a posting list being the list of documents that contain a given word — and concluded the peer network lacks the capacity for web-scale search. Applying caching of posting lists, precomputation of frequent word pairs, Bloom-filter intersection — a Bloom filter being a compact representation of a set that answers membership queries with a controlled rate of false matches — and compression together brings the cost within one order of magnitude of feasibility. A later analysis (International Conference on the Theory of Information Retrieval, 2011) measured about 6 megabytes of traffic per query for an index of 3 billion documents partitioned by word. Caching posting lists reduced average query cost by 38% in the MIT query trace, because most queries in that trace occurred once.

Under word partitioning, one participant stores the entire posting list for one word, so a query stating two words requires transmitting one full posting list across the network for intersection, and participants storing common words receive disproportionate load. Under document partitioning, each participant stores all words for a slice of the documents, so no posting list crosses the network, but every query must reach every participant that stores a slice. Neither partitioning alone produces results fast enough for an interactive ranked search, so a deployed system must combine local indexes with a smaller count of high-capacity indexing participants.

YaCy operates a peer crawler and index that runs today. Minerva selects which peer collections to query for a given request. Alvis, also called OverCite, distributed an academic citation index over a DHT. Seeks shared queries and results between users rather than distributing the index. Threshold algorithms of the type published by Ronald Fagin return the top k results after reading a bounded prefix of each posting list.

Retrieval by learned vector representation replaces word matching with proximity in a numeric space. Hierarchical Navigable Small World — abbreviated HNSW — builds a multi-level graph in which each search step moves to the neighbor closest to the query vector. Inverted File with Product Quantization — abbreviated IVF-PQ — partitions vectors into clusters and compresses each vector into a short code. No published scheme distributes either index across untrusted participants while preserving retrieval accuracy and rejecting deliberately inserted misleading vectors. That gap is an open problem stated below.

## Content addressing verifies data without trusting the sender

Content addressing gives any recipient the ability to verify that received data matches what was requested, without trusting the sender. A content identifier — abbreviated CID — is the cryptographic hash of the data. The recipient hashes what arrived and compares the result to the identifier requested. The InterPlanetary File System — abbreviated IPFS — combines libp2p for transport and connection management, Kademlia for locating which participant stores a block, Bitswap for requesting and exchanging blocks, and InterPlanetary Linked Data — abbreviated IPLD — for structures whose links are content identifiers. Trautwein and colleagues described and evaluated that design in "Design and Evaluation of IPFS: A Storage Layer for the Decentralized Web" (2022).

Wei and colleagues measured concentration in "Exploring the Role of Centralization in IPFS," USENIX Symposium on Networked Systems Design and Implementation, 2024. One centralized indexing service stored more than 100 times as many location records as the entire DHT. Content addressing by itself therefore does not prevent one company from becoming the sole practical route to the data, because users send requests to whichever service answers fastest.

Proof of Replication requires a paid storage provider to run a deliberately slow encoding over the data, so producing a second copy on demand requires more work than retaining one. Proof of Spacetime requires the provider to repeat a proof at intervals, so a provider that deletes the data fails the next proof. Filecoin uses both. The academic primitives underneath are Proofs of Retrievability, published by Hovav Shacham and Brent Waters, and Provable Data Possession. Arweave pays for storage once and replicates widely. Storj and Sia distribute erasure-coded pieces under contract. Swarm, Tahoe-LAFS, OceanStore, PAST, CFS, Hyphanet (formerly Freenet), Hypercore (formerly Dat), Iroh, and Codex make different combinations of these choices.

Replication stores whole copies, so recovering one lost copy requires transferring one full copy. Erasure coding splits data into n pieces such that any k of them reconstruct the original, which tolerates n−k losses at storage overhead n/k. Plain erasure coding requires downloading k pieces to regenerate one lost piece. Regenerating codes, published by Alexandros Dimakis and colleagues, reduce that repair traffic. Minimum-Storage Regenerating codes — abbreviated MSR — keep storage overhead at the erasure-coding minimum and reduce repair traffic as far as that constraint permits. Minimum-Bandwidth Regenerating codes — abbreviated MBR — accept higher storage overhead and reduce repair traffic further. Local reconstruction codes reduce the count of participants contacted per repair.

The reconstruction threshold k must fall between the donated storage capacity and the rate of simultaneous departures. Below the lower bound, overhead n/k grows and the system stores more redundant pieces than participants have space for. Above the upper bound, simultaneous departures destroy data before repair completes. Measured overheads from "Monotone Erasure Codes" (arXiv 2605.22426): Storj achieves overhead 1.75 but must reconstruct an entire file to recover any part of it, and Walrus reconstructs efficiently and tolerates Byzantine faults — faults in which a participant sends contradictory messages to different recipients — at overhead 3.5.

Editable shared data converges through Conflict-free Replicated Data Types — abbreviated CRDTs — which are data structures whose merge operation produces the same result regardless of the order in which concurrent edits arrive. Operational transformation achieves the same convergence by rewriting each operation against concurrent operations, and requires a server in its common implementations. Martin Kleppmann, Adam Wiggins, Peter van Hardenberg, and Mark McGranaghan stated seven requirements for software that stores data on the user's own device in "Local-first software: you own your data, in spite of the cloud" (Onward! 2019): fast response, use across several devices, operation without a network connection, collaboration with others, readability of the data years later, privacy of the contents, and user control over the copy. Automerge implements a JSON CRDT, and its version 3.0 (2025) reduced memory consumption by approximately a factor of ten by reimplementing the core in Rust. Yjs implements the same class of structure with a different internal representation. Secondary indexes and range queries over content-addressed stores remain immature, which is an open problem stated below. Kleppmann's work on Byzantine-tolerant CRDTs and Byzantine causal broadcast extends convergence to the case where some participants send contradictory messages to different recipients.

**Selection: IPLD content addressing for immutable data, MSR erasure codes for cold data, replication for frequently requested data, Automerge CRDTs for editable state, and Filecoin-style PoRep/PoSt for paid storage verification.** Content addressing (CID = hash of data) is the clear choice for immutable data because verification requires no trust and deduplication is automatic. Among erasure-coding schemes, MSR codes give the minimum storage overhead for a given fault tolerance while reducing repair traffic below plain Reed-Solomon, so MSR is the right default for data that is stored but rarely retrieved. Frequently requested data should be replicated in whole copies rather than erasure-coded, because serving a whole copy requires no reconstruction step and any peer that has the data can serve it immediately. Storj's overhead of 1.75 is lower than Walrus's 3.5, but Storj requires reconstructing the full file to repair any part, which Walrus does not. For this architecture, use MSR codes at an overhead between 1.75 and 3.5 with per-piece repair. Operational transformation requires a server, so it is excluded. Between Automerge and Yjs, Automerge 3.0's Rust core fits a Rust-based system and Kleppmann's Byzantine-tolerant CRDT extensions apply directly to Automerge's data model, so Automerge is the better fit. Among storage verification methods, Filecoin's PoRep/PoSt is the only one with a deployed verification pipeline, and it is the only mechanism that lets a third party confirm storage without trusting the provider or downloading the data.

## Self-certifying names provide security and decentralization but not human readability

A naming system provides at most two of these three properties: names a human reads and remembers, names that resolve to the correct key despite an adversary, and assignment of names without a central authority. Zooko Wilcox-O'Hearn formulated that bound. A self-certifying name is the hash of a public key, which provides the second and third properties and lacks the first. The holder of the matching private key signs a record stating the current content identifier, so any reader verifies that record against the name itself. The InterPlanetary Name System — abbreviated IPNS — implements that construction. DNSLink places a content identifier in a DNS text record, which supplies a readable name at the cost of depending on the domain name system.

Systems that add a readable global namespace obtain the first property through a consensus mechanism or through a private name scope. Namecoin, the Ethereum Name Service — abbreviated ENS — and Handshake record name-to-key bindings on a ledger that all participants agree on. The GNU Name System gives each user a private mapping from readable names to keys, so one name refers to one key for one user and a different key for another user.

Loss of the private key ends control of the name, because no other party can sign a replacement record. An editable naming layer therefore requires a defined recovery procedure before deployment.

**Selection: self-certifying names as the primary identity, audited by a CONIKS-style key-transparency log, with an optional ENS-style ledger only for the human-readable namespace layer.** Self-certifying names are the right default because they require no consensus mechanism, no registration authority, and no ongoing ledger fees — a participant generates a key pair and the public-key hash is the name. The cost is that the name is not human-readable, but human-readable names are only needed for the small fraction of identities that strangers need to find by name (public profiles, businesses, publications). For that fraction, ENS-style ledger registration adds the readable layer at the cost of a consensus dependency and a registration fee, but the readable name is an alias that resolves to the self-certifying name, so the identity survives even if the readable-name ledger fails. Namecoin and Handshake offer the same function as ENS. ENS has the largest deployed user base among naming ledgers, but the choice among them is less important than the principle that the readable name must be an optional layer above the self-certifying name, never the primary identifier. The GNU Name System's private-name-scope approach avoids the consensus dependency entirely but sacrifices global uniqueness — two users can map the same readable name to different keys — so it is better suited for personal contact books than for public discovery. DNSLink depends on the existing domain name system, which reintroduces the registrar as a point of capture. A CONIKS-style transparency log (Melara, Blankstein, Bonneau, Felten, Freedman, USENIX Security 2015) audits the key-transparency directory by publishing signed, chained Merkle-tree snapshots so that any user who monitors their own entry detects a substituted key. Parakeet (Malvai et al., NDSS 2023) extends CONIKS with bounded-size compaction and threshold-witness signing, benchmarked at 10 million updates per day, which is sufficient for this architecture's scale.

## Every social-graph identity-forgery defense performs community detection and is defeated by the same adversary construction

The identity-forgery attack, also called the Sybil attack — stated by John Douceur at the International Workshop on Peer-to-Peer Systems in 2002 — is one entity creating many identities to obtain influence proportional to identity count rather than to that entity's own resources.

Social-graph defenses assume the honest participants form a region in which a random walk mixes quickly, connected to the adversary's region by a small count of edges between an honest identity and an adversary identity. SybilGuard and SybilLimit, by Haifeng Yu and colleagues, bound the count of adversary identities accepted per such edge by intersecting random routes. SybilRank, by Qiang Cao and colleagues, ranks accounts by the probability that a short random walk started from trusted accounts ends at that account. SybilInfer applies Bayesian inference over the graph. SybilBelief applies loopy belief propagation over labeled and unlabeled accounts. SybilSCAR and SybilFuse extend that inference. Ostra applies the same edge limit to message delivery. Bazaar applies the edge limit to marketplace transactions. Canal and Integro reduce the computation cost for large graphs. Whanau, by Chris Lesniewski-Laas and Frans Kaashoek (USENIX Symposium on Networked Systems Design and Implementation, 2010), builds a DHT in which the count of adversary identities does not affect performance and only the count of edges to honest users does. Whanau tolerates up to O(n/log n) such edges using O(√n log n) routing entries per participant and completes lookups in constant time.

Bimal Viswanath, Ansley Post, Krishna Gummadi, and Alan Mislove showed in "An Analysis of Social Network-Based Sybil Defenses" (SIGCOMM 2010) that these schemes all detect the local community around a trusted participant, that a network whose honest users form distinct communities is therefore more vulnerable, and that an adversary who selects which honest users to connect to is more effective than one who connects at random. Lorenzo Alvisi, Allen Clement, Alessandro Epasto, Silvio Lattanzi, and Alessandro Panconesi showed in "SoK: The Evolution of Sybil Defense via Social Networks" (IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy, 2013) that the choice of community-detection algorithm changes the result substantially, and that a simple feature classifier is sufficient against an adversary who connects at random while no published method is sufficient against one who selects targets.

Proof of personhood issues at most one credential per human being. Bryan Ford and colleagues published the pseudonym-party construction in "Proof-of-Personhood: Redemocratizing Permissionless Cryptocurrencies" (2017): people gather in one physical place at one announced time, and each attendee receives one anonymous token without stating who they are. BrightID verifies through video sessions. Idena requires all participants to solve tests simultaneously that no automated system solves reliably. Worldcoin issues a credential after a device scans the iris. Vitalik Buterin's analysis of these designs states that the biometric method requires trusting the manufacturer of the scanning device, which BrightID and Proof of Humanity do not require, that every method permits coercion of the credential holder, and that party organizers can issue tokens to identities that do not exist. These claims come from project documentation and published critique, not from independent verification.

Require computational work per write from the start, add social-graph ranking once a graph exists, and add proof of personhood only where one vote per human is the requirement — because each mechanism alone is defeated by the adversary construction Viswanath and Alvisi described.

Reputation systems rank participants by the ratings other participants assign. EigenTrust, by Sepandar Kamvar, Mario Schlosser, and Hector Garcia-Molina (World Wide Web Conference, 2003), computes each participant's global score as the principal left eigenvector of the matrix of normalized local ratings. EigenTrust requires a set of participants trusted in advance, and requests concentrate on those participants. HonestPeer and EERP were published to reduce that concentration. PowerTrust (Runfang Zhou and Kai Hwang, IEEE Transactions on Parallel and Distributed Systems, Vol. 18, No. 4, 2007) uses the observation that peer feedback frequencies follow a power-law distribution; it identifies the most highly rated participants and uses them as seeds for a distributed random-walk computation of global trust, converging faster than EigenTrust on their benchmarks. PeerTrust (Li Xiong and Ling Liu, IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, Vol. 16, No. 7, 2004) computes trust from three parameters — the feedback a participant receives, the total count of transactions the participant performed, and the credibility of the feedback sources — plus two adaptive weighting factors for transaction context and community context. The Advogato trust metric (Raph Levien, PhD thesis, UC Berkeley, 2004) computes group trust as a maximum-flow problem: trust spreads from a seed set through the trust graph, and each edge carries a capacity equal to its trust rating, so a single adversary identity cannot inject trust beyond the capacity of its incoming edges. Appleseed (Cai-Nicolas Ziegler and Georg Lausen, Information Systems Frontiers, Vol. 7, No. 4-5, 2005) computes local group trust by injecting an initial energy at a source participant and propagating the energy along outgoing trust edges in proportion to their weights, with a spreading factor that controls how much energy each step retains versus passes on. The Beta reputation system (Audun Jøsang and Roslan Ismail, 15th Bled Electronic Commerce Conference, 2002) represents trust as a beta probability density function parameterized by the count of positive and negative ratings, so the expected probability of a positive outcome in the next interaction is (positive + 1) / (positive + negative + 2). TrustRank (Zoltán Gyöngyi, Hector Garcia-Molina, Jan Pedersen, VLDB 2004) was designed for web spam detection rather than peer reputation: it assigns trust by a biased PageRank walk seeded from a manually verified set of trusted pages, and was later adapted for peer-to-peer contexts. All reputation systems are vulnerable to discarding an identity with poor ratings and creating a new one, to coordinated mutual rating between colluding identities, to rating oneself through controlled identities, to rating a competitor falsely low, and to multiplying influence through forged identities.

**Selection: Advogato-style flow metric for trust propagation, with Beta reputation for bilateral transaction ratings.** EigenTrust, PowerTrust, and TrustRank all compute a single global score per participant, which requires either a pre-trusted seed set (EigenTrust, TrustRank) or a globally agreed set of "power" participants (PowerTrust). In each case, the seed set becomes a point of centralization: whoever chooses the seeds controls the trust rankings. The Advogato flow metric avoids this because each participant computes trust from their own position in the graph, using their own direct ratings as the source of the flow computation. An adversary can inject trust only up to the capacity of the edges connecting the adversary to honest participants, which bounds the damage per attack edge. Appleseed shares the local-computation property but uses an energy-propagation model rather than a max-flow model; max-flow has a tighter bound on adversary influence per edge, so Advogato is preferred. For bilateral transactions (marketplace, file exchange), the Beta reputation system is the simplest correct choice: it represents the probability of a good outcome directly from the count of past outcomes, it updates in constant time per rating, and it requires no global computation. PeerTrust's multi-factor model adds context-dependent weighting, which is useful but adds complexity that is not justified until the system has enough transaction volume to calibrate the weights. Use Beta reputation as the default and add PeerTrust-style weighting factors once calibration data exists.

## A strategic client extracts 70% more download throughput from BitTorrent's reciprocity mechanism

BitTorrent allocates upload capacity to the peers that upload the most in return. Michael Piatek and colleagues built a client that exploits that allocation in "Do Incentives Build Robustness in BitTorrent?" (USENIX Symposium on Networked Systems Design and Implementation, 2007). The client measured what each peer actually returned, connected to more peers at once, and reduced its upload to each peer to the minimum that still earned a return, obtaining a median 70% improvement for a 1 Mbit/s connection on live swarms. When every client behaves that way, average performance in each swarm falls.

The cause is that contributed bandwidth cannot be verified cheaply by a third party, so any accounting of bandwidth accepts the contributor's own claim. Measurements of early Gnutella recorded that most participants shared no files at all. KARMA proposed a distributed credit balance per participant. Dandelion released decryption keys only after payment. PropShare allocated upload in proportion to received data. Storage contribution differs, because Proof of Replication and Proof of Spacetime verify storage without trusting the provider. Pay for storage, which is verifiable, and treat bandwidth contribution as voluntary rather than as an enforced obligation.

## Privacy mechanisms increase latency, bandwidth, or computation by measured factors

Onion routing hides which destination a user contacts from anyone observing the user's connection. The client encrypts each message in nested layers, one per relay, and each relay removes one layer and forwards the result, so no relay has both the origin and the destination. Tor implements that construction over three relays. Overlay delay exceeded 500 milliseconds in about 10% of circuits in the New York University study "Waiting for Anonymity." That 500-millisecond figure describes a tail of the distribution; the Tor Project publishes distributions rather than one summary figure, and no single primary source states one number for Tor's added delay. Payap Sirinam, Mohsen Imani, Marc Juarez, and Matthew Wright showed in "Deep Fingerprinting" (ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security, 2018) that a classifier identifies which website a user loaded from packet timing and direction alone, with over 98% accuracy against undefended Tor traffic and over 90% against traffic protected by WTF-PAD, and that Walkie-Talkie reduces the same classifier to 49.7%.

Walkie-Talkie, by Tao Wang and Ian Goldberg (USENIX Security, 2017), transmits in one direction at a time and pads each transmission to match one from a decoy page, at 31% additional bandwidth and 34% additional time. WTF-PAD inserts padding only during gaps and therefore adds no delay, at additional bandwidth reported between 32% and 54% depending on configuration.

A mix network prevents an adversary who observes every link simultaneously from determining who communicates with whom. Loopix, by Ania Piotrowska, Jamie Hayes, Tariq Elahi, and George Danezis (USENIX Security, 2017), delays each message independently at each relay by an exponentially distributed interval, and sends messages containing no content at a constant rate to conceal when real messages pass through. Loopix relays handle upward of 300 messages per second at under 1.5 milliseconds of processing delay per relay, and total message latency is on the order of seconds. Nym runs a production network on that design. Debajyoti Das and colleagues proved (IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy, 2018) that any anonymous communication system provides at most two of: strong anonymity, low latency overhead, and low bandwidth overhead. That bound applies to any system, present or future.

Private information retrieval — abbreviated PIR — lets a client fetch record i without the server learning which record was fetched, by having the server compute over an encrypted query. Measured single-thread throughput, expressed as database bytes processed per second: SealPIR 102 MB/s, FastPIR 189 MB/s, OnionPIR 122 MB/s, and Spiral 310 MB/s. Spiral, by Samir Menon and David Wu, retrieves from a 7.9 GB database using a 14 kB query and an 84 kB response, at about 24.5 seconds of server computation. SimplePIR reaches higher throughput and requires the client to download a large precomputed hint first. Schemes using several non-colluding servers, including those built on distributed point functions — abbreviated DPFs — require far less computation.

Searchable symmetric encryption — abbreviated SSE — permits keyword search over encrypted documents, and reveals which encrypted entries each query touched. Attacks by Islam, Kuzu, and Kantarcioglu, by Cash and colleagues, and by Grubbs and colleagues reconstruct the plaintext queries from what SSE reveals, given partial knowledge of the document set. Encrypted search is therefore not equivalent to private search: a design that requires query privacy needs PIR rather than SSE.

Dissent, Herbivore, and Riposte hide which member of a group sent a message, at a coordination cost that grows with group size. Vuvuzela, Stadium, Yodel, Karaoke, Talek, and Pung hide who communicates with whom, by adding noise drawn from a differential-privacy distribution or by retrieving messages through PIR. Oblivious RAM — abbreviated ORAM — hides which storage location a client accessed, at a bandwidth multiple of the data actually read.

**Selection: Tor for tier 1 with Walkie-Talkie padding as an option, Loopix/Nym for tier 2, Spiral PIR for tier 3.** Tor is the right tier-1 mechanism because it has the largest deployed relay network, the most studied attack surface, and acceptable latency for most interactive use. WTF-PAD adds less bandwidth overhead than Walkie-Talkie (32–54% versus 31%) but fails against the Deep Fingerprinting classifier at over 90% accuracy, while Walkie-Talkie reduces that classifier to 49.7%. Walkie-Talkie is therefore the better padding defense despite its 34% time overhead. Among mix networks, Loopix is preferred over Mixminion because Loopix's Poisson mixing provides a formal privacy guarantee (sender-receiver unlinkability against a global passive adversary) with message latency on the order of seconds rather than minutes, and Nym operates a production deployment on the Loopix design. Among PIR schemes, Spiral achieves the highest single-thread throughput at 310 MB/s and uses the smallest query and response sizes (14 kB and 84 kB), making it the best choice for tier 3 when the client needs to fetch a record without the server learning which record. Multi-server DPF-based PIR is preferable when two or more non-colluding servers are available, because it reduces server computation by orders of magnitude, but it requires the non-collusion assumption, which is a trust dependency. Dissent/Herbivore/Riposte are excluded because their coordination cost grows with group size and none has been deployed at more than experimental scale. SSE is excluded because the leakage-abuse attacks by Islam-Kuzu-Kantarcioglu, Cash, and Grubbs reconstruct queries from the access patterns SSE reveals. ORAM is excluded from the default tiers because its bandwidth multiplier (typically 10–100×) makes interactive use impractical, but ORAM may be added as a tier 4 for storage-access-pattern hiding if conditions require it.

## Shared data converges without global consensus when participants use conflict-free replicated data types

Byzantine fault tolerant consensus — abbreviated BFT — makes all correct participants agree on one total order of operations even when some participants send contradictory messages to different recipients. PBFT, HotStuff, and Tendermint achieve that agreement among a known set of participants. Nakamoto consensus achieves the agreement among an open set by requiring computational work per block. Directed-acyclic-graph orderings relax the total order to a partial one. Threshold signatures let any quorum of a group produce one signature that verifies against a single group key, with no member having the whole private key.

CRDTs converge without any agreement on order, and the Byzantine-tolerant variants converge even when participants send contradictory messages. Collaborative documents, message threads, and content feeds have that property. Global consensus is required only for a resource that must be unique across all users at once — one readable global namespace, or a transferable token balance — so apply consensus to the naming ledger if a readable global namespace is required, and to nothing else, because consensus adds per-operation latency and concentrates validation on the parties that afford to run validators.

## A content identifier cannot be changed after publication, so moderation labels content at retrieval rather than deleting it at storage

A CID is the hash of the data, so the data cannot be edited in place and cannot be withdrawn from a participant who continues to serve the data. Removal therefore occurs at the point of retrieval and display, not at the point of storage.

Bluesky separates hosting from judgment: independent labeling services publish assertions about content, and each client subscribes to the labelers its user selects and applies the labelers' assertions. Ozone is the reference labeling service. Mastodon blocks at the level of whole instances, and each instance operator sets the instance's own rules. Matrix distributes shared block lists as policy rooms that servers subscribe to. For content that is illegal to store — including child sexual abuse material — the only available mechanism is propagating identifier block lists that participants and gateways honor by refusing to serve the matching data.

Aravindh Raman and colleagues measured concentration in "Challenges in the Decentralised Web: The Mastodon Case" (Internet Measurement Conference, 2019): a simulated outage in 5 autonomous systems reduced the largest connected component from 92% to 46% of users, and outages at 10 instances removed 62.69% of all posts. Distributing content across independent operators is therefore a moderation requirement as well as a durability requirement, because few operators concentrated together have the power to remove most content at once.

**Selection: Bluesky-style composable labelers, not Mastodon-style instance blocking or Matrix-style shared block lists.** Mastodon's instance-level blocking forces a binary choice — every user on a blocked instance becomes unreachable — which punishes users on a mixed-quality instance and gives instance operators disproportionate power. Matrix's shared block lists (policy rooms) converge toward a single shared policy, which re-centralizes the moderation decision. Bluesky's composable labelers let each client subscribe to independently operated labeling services and combine their assertions, so a user who disagrees with one labeler's judgment can switch labelers without leaving the network. The labeling service publishes assertions ("this content is spam," "this content is NSFW") and the client decides what to do with each label. For illegal content (CSAM), propagate CID-based deny lists that participants and gateways honor by refusing to serve the matching data; this is the only mechanism available given that a CID is immutable.

## iOS and Android suspend background applications, so phones cannot participate as always-connected peers

iOS and Android suspend an application shortly after the user leaves it and close its network sockets, so a phone does not answer lookups or serve blocks while the screen is off. Continuous relaying also consumes battery charge and cellular data allowance. Browsers restrict peer connections to WebRTC and WebTransport and cap the count of simultaneous connections. Phones and browser tabs are therefore clients that depend on relays, not participants that carry network load. Supply store-and-forward relays that hold messages for a recipient whose device is suspended.

Connection establishment through network address translation — abbreviated NAT — is the other requirement. Hole punching coordinates two peers to send packets simultaneously so each opens a path through its own translator. A 2025 measurement of Direct Connection Upgrade through Relay — abbreviated DCUtR — in the IPFS network, covering more than 4.4 million attempts across more than 85,000 networks in 167 countries, recorded a conditional success rate of 70% ± 7.1%. TCP and QUIC were statistically indistinguishable, and the result did not depend on which relay coordinated the attempt. A symmetric translator — one that assigns a different external port per destination — defeats hole punching entirely, and requires relaying every packet through a third party.

Caching retrieved content at the requesting client, prefetching content the user is likely to request next, volunteer caches placed near users, falling back to a server when the peer path fails, and delegated routing — in which a well-connected participant performs the lookup and returns a result the client verifies against the content hash — each reduce the latency difference from centralized services.

## Privacy as user-selected tiers, each with a stated adversary and a measured increase in latency or bandwidth

Each tier listed below is combined with the tiers above it, and the client applies tiers per action rather than per session. A user raises the tier as conditions require without changing systems.

|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Direct connection with transport encryption | Baseline latency | An observer reading message contents |

## Open problems that have no published solution and require original work

| Problem | Why no published solution exists |
|---|---|
| Ranked full-text search across peers at web scale | The measured cost remains about 6 MB per query, and posting-list caching reduces the cost by only 38% because most queries occur once |
| Distributed vector search that rejects deliberately inserted misleading vectors | No published scheme distributes HNSW or IVF-PQ across untrusted participants while preserving retrieval accuracy |
| Forgery resistance without trusted starting participants | Every social-graph defense performs community detection and is defeated when honest participants form distinct communities |
| Private search at interactive latency | Single-server PIR requires seconds of computation per query on multi-gigabyte databases |
| Repair economics for volunteer erasure-coded storage | Regenerating codes minimize repair traffic, and no measurement establishes that volunteers supply that traffic under real churn |
| Continuous participation from phones | iOS and Android suspend applications and close sockets when the screen is off |
| Removing illegal material from content-addressed storage | A CID is immutable, so removal depends on every participant honoring a block list |
| Verifiable bandwidth accounting | No cheap third-party verification of contributed bandwidth exists, so reciprocity remains exploitable |
| Honest bandwidth reporting in a capacity-ordered overlay | HSkip+ orders participants by self-reported bandwidth and does not address a participant that misreports to gain position |
| Forward secrecy under long partitions | BeeKEM's authors conjecture the tradeoff between cross-branch recovery and forward secrecy is inherent to decentralized group key agreement |
| Secondary indexes and range queries over content-addressed stores | Published content-addressed stores retrieve by identifier only, and no distributed index over content-addressed stores has been measured at scale |

## Design sequence and thresholds

Build storage and naming first, using content-addressed blocks with self-certifying signed editable records audited by a key-transparency log, because that component has the most primary-source measurement behind it. Measure median retrieval latency before adding anything that depends on it. If delegated lookup and volunteer caching do not bring median retrieval below one second, redesign retrieval before building applications that depend on it.

Separate the user's data store, the portable identifier, and the indexing parties. Require every indexing party to be replaceable and its results verifiable. If any single indexing party answers more than half of all queries, the system has reproduced the intermediary that decentralization was intended to eliminate.

Require computational work on every write from the first release. Add social-graph ranking once a graph exists. Add proof of personhood only for one-vote-per-human decisions. Do not release with a single forgery defense, because Viswanath and Alvisi demonstrated that each defense alone is defeated by one adversary construction.

Set tier 0 as the default. Implement all four privacy tiers before release. Display each tier's measured cost in the interface at the point of the toggle, so the user selects with the numbers visible.

Treat phones and browser tabs as clients that depend on relays. Build store-and-forward relays and delegated lookup before building any application that assumes a recipient is reachable.

Do not attempt ranked full-text search across peers alone. Plan for volunteer high-capacity indexes whose results the client verifies, because the six-megabyte-per-query measurement has not been overturned.

## SPLiNTERNET already implements several of these components, and three open defects map directly to the research

SPLiNTERNET is a Rust peer-to-peer marketplace and publishing protocol with two crates: splinternet-core (protocol kernel: objects, canonical encoding, governance, attestation, search, web-of-trust, geo indexing, storage) and splinternet-net (daemon, transport, Kademlia DHT). It mandates Tor/I2P transport, has no native cryptocurrency token, and has no central server. The client is an HTML/CSS/JS webview that communicates with the Rust core over IPC. The protocol uses a content DAG (§19) where every object is typed, signed, and content-addressed. 294 tests pass. The following maps what the protocol already has to what the research recommends, and states what needs to change.

**DHT: already Kademlia (splinternet-net), which the research selects.** Adopt Accordion-style bandwidth budgeting into the existing Kademlia implementation so each participant controls routing-table maintenance cost. Adopt Bamboo's three churn findings (periodic recovery, TCP-style timeouts, proximity neighbor selection) if not already present. The existing DHT should be checked for iterative-versus-recursive routing; the research selects iterative because the querying participant detects malicious forwarding steps.

**Content DAG (§19) is the content-addressed storage layer.** Every object — marketplace listing, event, fundraiser, commons inventory entry, stringer curation, broadsheet composition — is a signed, content-addressed entry in the DAG, propagated by gossip with HEAD pointers. This is the IPLD-equivalent layer. The research recommends MSR erasure coding for cold data and replication for frequently requested data; the current protocol should be checked for which redundancy scheme it uses and whether repair is implemented.

**Search (§36) has a critical confidentiality defect that the research addresses.** The private search channel uses salted-hash private set intersection. A code review (prior conversation, synthesized findings report) established that this scheme reveals any predicate the answering peer holds or can guess: on a match, the answerer already has the cleartext value that produced the matching hash; on a no-match, the answerer enumerates candidates under the received salt and confirms or excludes each. The per-query salt prevents offline precomputed tables and cross-query linkage but does not prevent online enumeration. The §36.3 confidentiality claim — that the answerer learns only match/no-match, never the predicates — is false as written. The fix path from the research: replace the salted-hash PSI with an Oblivious Pseudo-Random Function (OPRF) based PSI, where the answerer evaluates a blinded function over the query so the answerer never sees the query value even on a match. If hiding the match pattern itself is required (whether a match occurred at all), use PIR (the research selects Spiral at 310 MB/s throughput, 14 kB query, 84 kB response). The OPRF path is the right next step; PIR is the tier-3 privacy escalation.

**Search (§36) is also missing proof-of-work rate limiting, and synchronous recursive forwarding creates an amplification vector.** The research recommends computational work on every write from the first release. Extend that to every query: require a small PoW stamp on each search request, verified before the answering peer evaluates it. The synchronous recursive RPC in the current search path means one query pins a thread at each forwarding step and stacks per-step latency, so a single query generates work proportional to the graph diameter. Replace with asynchronous message-passing: the querying participant sends the query, each step forwards asynchronously and returns results as they arrive.

**Geo search (§35) has inconsistent behavior that should be fixed before shipping.** The prior code review found four inconsistent behaviors: silent empty result on a positive geo query, wrong answer on a negated geo query, and two other edge cases. Add a compile-time guard that rejects geo queries until geo search is fully implemented, collapsing all four inconsistencies into one loud error.

**Broadsheet composition (§41) proves the content-DAG approach covers the application patterns.** The broadsheet spec, written against the actual codebase, defines a chain-5 object type ("sheet") in the §19 content DAG: a signed manifest of content-addressed references to typed objects (listings, events, fundraisers, commons entries, published writing, video, stringer curations) plus narrative text and layout intent. The broadsheet inherits all existing machinery — gossip propagation, HEAD pointers, §36-EXT tag search, §35 geo indexing, §18 hash moderation, §8.3 degradation — with no new transport or DHT primitives. Live references update via recompute-from-held-set (the same pattern as §31 societies and §35 geo), not server push. Milestone conditions (fundraiser progress, volunteer slots filled) are deterministic threshold predicates that any participant recomputes from the held set. Provenance is unforgeable via §19.1 parent-echo. This one object type covers the forum, blog, events, fundraising, mutual aid, and news aggregation rows in the application patterns table, because each row's data model is a composition of the typed objects the protocol already defines.

**Stringer tab implements the feed-generation function without a central index.** Strings are signed subscribable curation feeds. A stringer selects and annotates existing protocol objects; subscribers receive updates via the same DAG gossip. Discovery of stringers uses Burt's structural-hole metric over the web-of-trust graph: a participant positioned between otherwise-disconnected groups has high structural-hole score, which identifies participants who curate across communities. Compensation ranges from signed appreciation (a public attestation of value) through bounties (a payment offer for a specific curation). This is the SPLiNTERNET equivalent of the Bluesky feed-generator architecture, with two differences: feeds are authored by individuals rather than by server-side algorithms, and discovery is a graph metric rather than a directory listing.

**Pol.is-style consensus ranking was evaluated and partially fits.** Pol.is uses PCA (Principal Component Analysis — a method that finds the axes of greatest variation in a matrix) and k-means clustering to group participants, then scores each item by the product of per-group agreement probabilities (Group-Informed Consensus), which favors items that have support across groups rather than items that are popular in one group. The three centralization dependencies — full vote matrix access, batch recomputation, and voter-linked vote visibility — were mapped against SPLiNTERNET's constraints. The best fit is per-node local recomputation from gossiped votes using the existing DAG gossip, tolerating temporary ranking divergence across nodes. The privacy tradeoff: this spreads the vote matrix across all hosting nodes rather than concentrating it on one server, which distributes the exposure rather than eliminating it.

**DAG-based causal ordering replaces clock oracles.** The §19 content DAG orders events by causal hash references: each new entry includes the hash of the entry it depends on, so the ordering is verifiable from the data itself without contacting a time server. This eliminates three failure modes that a centralized clock introduces: a single point of control over ordering, metadata leakage from timestamp-request traffic, and a requirement for live connectivity to the clock at the time of writing. The research section on consensus confirms this: CRDTs and causal ordering converge without global consensus, and global consensus is needed only for a scarce unique resource (a readable namespace or a transferable balance).

**Identity uses §7 Monero-decoupled identity, which needs upgrading to social recovery.** The research selects social-recovery key management to prevent the Nostr/SSB unrecoverable-key failure. SPLiNTERNET should add a recovery quorum: the user designates a set of guardians, and any quorum of those guardians can authorize a key rotation. Record each rotation in a CONIKS-style transparency log so a substituted key is detectable by any party monitoring the log. The existing §37 tiered security framework provides the authorization structure for the recovery operation.

**Moderation uses §18 hash moderation, which should be extended with composable labeling.** The research selects Bluesky-style composable labelers over Mastodon-style instance blocking. §18 hash moderation already lets participants refuse to serve data matching a hash; extend this with a labeler subscription mechanism where independent services publish signed label assertions (spam, NSFW, illegal-content CID) and each client subscribes to the labelers it selects. The client applies the intersection of its subscribed labelers' assertions when displaying content.

**Sybil resistance is not yet implemented; the research provides the layered design.** Require computational work (a PoW stamp) on every write and every search query immediately. Use the existing web-of-trust graph for SybilRank-style ranking once the graph has enough edges. Add proof-of-personhood (pseudonym parties or BrightID-style video verification) only for one-vote-per-human decisions such as governance votes within §31 societies.

**Privacy tiers map to the mandatory Tor/I2P transport.** The current architecture operates at tier 1 (onion routing) by default, which is stronger than the research's tier-0 default. Adding Walkie-Talkie padding (31% bandwidth, 34% time) to the Tor transport defends against the Deep Fingerprinting classifier. Tier 2 (Loopix/Nym mix network) and tier 3 (Spiral PIR) are opt-in escalations above the Tor baseline. Because SPLiNTERNET already mandates Tor/I2P, the tier-0-to-tier-1 transition that other architectures must offer is unnecessary; the relevant toggles are tier-1-with-padding, tier-2, and tier-3.

## Under onion routing, round trips are the scarce resource — not bytes

The SPIP Research Integration document (SPLiNTERNET's adjudication of the research corpus against its own design) establishes a constraint that changes most of the recommendations in the research literature. Every cell on the onion transport is 4,096 bytes with random padding, so a 200-byte DHT query and a 4,000-byte response occupy the same space on the wire. A circuit round trip is 200 ms to 1 s, with published onion time-to-first-byte medians of 270 to 760 ms. At the optimistic end, removing one round trip saves as much as transferring 100 to 500 KB. The consequence: most space-efficiency results in the academic literature are inert when the transport is onion-routed, because a structure that shrinks from 3,100 bytes to 2,400 bytes still occupies one cell. The useful unit of accounting is cells and round trips, not bytes.

This constraint invalidates or reduces the benefit of several mechanisms the general architecture recommends:

**Bloom/Cuckoo/Xor/Ribbon filter comparisons are irrelevant at 4,096-byte cell granularity.** Cuckoo's measured 3.1% space advantage over Bloom saves one cell out of 40 at 100,000 objects. Xor at matched false-positive rates saves zero cells at 10,000 objects. Ribbon's 30% saving is zero cells below about 3,400 objects. The choice among filter formats, which occupies much of the literature, does not affect performance on a padded transport.

**Epidemic push (Plumtree/GossipSub) saves nothing for messages under 4 KB.** Under fixed 4 KB cells, eager send and Plumtree both cost four cells for any payload up to 4 KB — and a group-chat message and a feed head fetch both sit at exactly that size. GossipSub's heartbeat consumes 48 KB/s of uplink for its control plane alone, which is roughly half of a single circuit's planning throughput. Plumtree only helps above 4 KB, and the one payload large enough (a 4 MiB erasure share) is distributed by DHT placement rather than broadcast.

**Gossiped inventory filters cannot be probed on a salted search channel.** Every search predicate crosses the wire as BLAKE3(salt ‖ field_tag ‖ value) under a salt drawn fresh for each query. A filter is committed before the salt exists, so nothing in the filter can be tested against a salted query. Building the filter over unsalted keys requires the searcher to probe with the plaintext key, which is the disclosure the salted channel exists to prevent. The pruning benefit described in the literature is therefore not available on a privacy-preserving search channel.

**Mixnets cost more than the link budget.** Nym's default cover traffic is 125 KB/s, continuous, which is larger than the entire per-circuit planning budget and 10.8 GB per day per client. On the Tor tier, the marginal protection does not justify what it consumes. SPLiNTERNET's S.P.I.P. 3 addresses the residual channel (cell count over time) with cover traffic at a stated operating point: one cover cell per minute with about 15 seconds of added latency costs 11.8 MB/day, and one cover cell per round with under 1 second of added latency costs 708 MB/day.

## Four tests determine whether a mechanism can be offered as an opt-in setting

The SPIP document states four tests. A mechanism that fails any one cannot be offered as a user toggle.

**Who bears the consequence when one peer enables it?** If the consequence lands on the peer that chose, the choice is real. If it lands on peers that did not choose, the setting decides for other people. A peer's disk under placement and reading-keeps-a-copy contains other authors' erasure shares, so publishing a summary of what a machine holds discloses strangers who never agreed to be listed. A summary of what a person authored discloses only that person.

**Does the mechanism work when only some peers run it?** Bilateral mechanisms (a wire verb two peers both speak, a reconciliation session) need no crowd. Mechanisms that need a crowd — an anonymity set, a uniform padding regime, a global ordering — deliver a weaker property to everyone rather than the same property to a subset.

**Can anyone see which choice a peer made?** A visible setting sorts the network into two populations, and which population a peer belongs to is information. Optional privacy narrows the group it exists to hide people in.

**Does enabling it help enough that it becomes mandatory in practice?** If the setting materially improves the service a peer receives, non-adopters receive slower answers and less reach, and the pressure to adopt is as strong as the benefit. Evaluate the equilibrium, not the nominal freedom.

These tests produce three categories: offerable per peer (the cost lands on the chooser, bilateral, works with partial adoption), offerable only at collective scope (a society or room whose members all joined), and not offerable at any scope.

## Mechanisms the research corpus offers, with their measured exposure and performance tradeoffs

Each mechanism below delivers a stated benefit and creates a stated exposure. The architecture selects among them based on what tradeoff fits the deployment.

**Gossiped inventory filters (Bloom, Cuckoo, Xor, Ribbon over a peer's held content).** A peer builds a compact set summary of the tags or content it indexes and sends the summary to its neighbors. A searcher checks the summary before forwarding and skips peers whose summary excludes the query. The benefit is large: pruning half the candidate forwards at each hop halves aggregate search traffic from about 1,024 cells to about 512 per query. The exposure: a filter is a standing query interface to a peer's disk — any neighbor can test any key offline, for as long as the neighbor keeps the bytes. Bitcoin's BIP37 shipped client-to-server Bloom filters in 2012; Gervais et al. (ACSAC 2014) recovered much of a wallet's address set from one filter; Bitcoin Core 0.19 disabled the feature by default. A filter over self-authored public content exposes only what the author already published. A filter over everything a peer holds — including other authors' erasure shares placed there by the storage layer — exposes content the peer did not author and whose authors did not consent to being cataloged. On a salted search channel (where each query is fingerprinted under a fresh per-query salt), a pre-committed filter cannot be tested against the salted query, so the pruning benefit requires either an unsalted probe (which discloses the query to every peer holding the filter) or a separate channel.

**Network-wide popularity and cardinality aggregation (HyperLogLog, Count-Min sketches merged across peers).** Produces global counts no single peer could compute. As a ranking input, a popularity figure lets a searcher order results by something beyond their own local graph. As telemetry, it gives each operator a picture of the network beyond their own horizon. The exposure: Count-Min merges by addition, so a single crafted sketch moves the merged result by an unbounded amount. HLL merges by maximum, so one peer using a different hash seed permanently inflates the merged estimate with no way to identify or subtract the contribution. If adoption is partial, the merged value is a correct count of the participating subset and is indistinguishable from a count of the whole — the denominator is unknown. Results ordered by a shared global number are ordered identically for everyone, which makes the ranking a target for gaming and a surface for subpoena. Society-scoped counts (within a named group whose members all joined) avoid the unknown-denominator and gaming problems because the population is defined and consent is collective.

**Published per-identity behavior scores (EigenTrust, SybilRank, image scoring, bilateral bandwidth channels).** The benefit: assessment of strangers without a direct connection. A peer far away in the graph is not assessed at all without shared scores — not badly assessed, simply unranked. The exposure: a score is an assertion about a person, made by parties that person did not choose, traveling to parties that person will never meet. The scored person cannot opt out of being scored. GossipSub's local peer scoring is distinct from this category because its scores are computed locally and never transmitted — the wire format is unchanged whether a peer scores or not.

**Content-derived similarity signatures (MinHash, SimHash, LSH bucket publication).** A peer publishes a compact representation from which its content or interest profile can be estimated, so queries route toward peers likely to hold matches. The benefit: routing that improves as the network grows. The exposure: a published similarity signature gives every neighbor a durable profile of the peer's content. An author-declared topic set (coarse, chosen by the author rather than derived from content) exposes only what the author chose to declare. Whether the routing improvement justifies the profile exposure depends on whether content clusters among connected peers, which has never been measured for a consent-gated graph.

**Epidemic push (Plumtree, GossipSub) as a default dissemination mode.** Every relay stores and forwards content it never requested. The benefit: one message reaches all subscribers without polling. The exposure: involuntary hosting of content the relay did not choose. Under 4,096-byte padded cells, epidemic push saves zero cells for messages under 4 KB (both eager and Plumtree cost four cells), and GossipSub's heartbeat consumes 48 KB/s for its control plane. An opt-in mesh scoped to a topic or room — where joining is the hosting decision — retains the push benefit at a scope where consent is explicit.

## The four adopted SPIPs and what each changes

**S.P.I.P. 9: Range-fingerprint reconciliation for large shared sets.** When two peers want to determine which objects they hold in common and which they differ on, range-fingerprint reconciliation (Meyer, IEEE SRDS 2023; Nostr NIP-77 Negentropy) computes the difference in logarithmically many rounds. Its benefit exceeds the cost only above roughly 1,500 shared objects; below that, sending the ID list outright is cheaper. Scoped to one author's chain (which keeps it clear of the inventory refusal), most sets are too small. Scoped to everything a peer holds (where the benefit is clear), the fingerprinted set is an inventory of that peer's disk. The scope question must be settled before building.

**S.P.I.P. 10: Disjoint-path lookups at the existing α=3.** Making the existing three concurrent Kademlia lookup paths visit non-overlapping sets of peers takes no extra messages and raises lookup success against a 20% routing adversary from 41% to 79% (Baumgart & Mies, ICPADS 2007, applying S/Kademlia's own formula). A defect found while checking: the address-group cap (MAX_PER_ADDR_GROUP = 2) returns the host portion verbatim, so on the Tor tier every v3 onion address is its own group and the cap never fires. Disjoint paths stand alone; the cap needs prefix-derived grouping for clearnet and a different bound for onion contacts.

**S.P.I.P. 11: Origin ambiguity on the search tier.** Adopted from 4P's IEP/FEP forwarding idea (Zeilemaker, Pouwelse, Sips, IEEE P2P 2014). The adoption rests on the mechanism being sound; the primary PDF could not be retrieved, so no measurement from the paper is quoted.

**S.P.I.P. 12: Group keying over pairwise ratchets, with DCGKA as the fallback.** The current pairwise fan-out costs (N−1) × 4 KB per message, which at measured throughput is about 0.12 s at 30 members and 4 s at 1,000 members. DCGKA (Weidner et al., CCS 2021) provides one ciphertext per message with forward secrecy and post-compromise security, requires only the causal order the content DAG already supplies, and its two-party channels are the built Double Ratchet. MLS was reserved for lack of a decentralized alternative and requires a delivery service imposing total order.

## Three improvements need no protocol change and are larger than anything in the research corpus

**The compact and streaming cell profiles are built, tested, and never advertised.** The framing layer defines three cell geometries: 4,096 B padded (privacy, the default for everything), 512 B padded (compact, for DHT/search/trust/slot), and 16,393 B zero-padded (streaming, for fetch/replicate). Nothing in production advertises the compact or streaming profiles, so every session runs the 4 KB default. Advertising the compact profile reduces DHT and search traffic by approximately 8 times. The streaming profile was derived so a 256 KiB erasure block is exactly 16 cells instead of 64. Messaging is pinned to the padded default regardless of advertisement, so the anonymity tier cannot be weakened.

**The feed refresh issues 200 sequential DHT walks instead of concurrent ones.** `refresh_feed` iterates the follow list in a serial for-loop, each iteration performing a full DHT walk. A follower of 200 authors performs 200 DHT walks one after another — on the order of 600 to 1,000 sequential round trips. The walks are independent. Running them concurrently at a bound of 8 divides refresh latency to 75–125 round trips. Nothing new crosses the wire. This single change removes most of the latency that S.P.I.P. 9 was proposed to reduce.

**Three disjoint lookup paths cost zero extra messages.** Described under S.P.I.P. 10 above. The change is making the existing α=3 concurrent lookup paths non-overlapping, which is a constraint on peer selection, not an additional message.

## Social-graph routing (Hyphanet/Freenet) is catastrophically worse than Kademlia on measured evidence

The Pitch Black attack (published measurements on up to 800 nodes running real Freenet code): two malicious nodes out of 800 — a quarter of one percent, each with an ordinary user's resources — cost the network between 15% and 60% of its stored content within about three hours. The production system in that class reports 0.08% darknet request success and 4.4% overall. A node's location is a shared coordinate; corrupting it corrupts the keyspace partition every other node routes and stores against, so the cost of one peer defecting lands on the whole network. Routing over the linkage graph also makes reachability depend on social connectivity: a brand-new identity with zero linkages could not route at all, which contradicts the principle that a new identity is a functioning peer.

## Regenerating codes need 9 of 10 holders live, which is a durability objection

At SPLiNTERNET's parameters (n=10, k=4, d=9 over a 16 MiB object), minimum-storage regenerating codes reduce repair traffic from 16 MiB to 6 MiB — a 2.67× reduction. The objection is not bandwidth but liveness: nine live, cooperative holders instead of any four. The system already treats staying above ten reachable hosts as the marginal case, and a repair mechanism that needs nine of ten available is weakest exactly when repair is most needed. Exact-repair product-matrix constructions avoid the content-addressing breakage of functional repair and require d ≥ 2k−1 = 7, satisfied at d=9. The verdict: refuse today on the liveness requirement, and reopen when object sizes grow (D-MEDIA-01 removes the 16 MiB ceiling), where the absolute saving scales with object size while the liveness cost does not.

## Disjoint lookup paths raise success against a 20% adversary from 41% to 99%

Ingmar Baumgart and Sebastian Mies, "S/Kademlia: A Practicable Approach Towards Secure Key-Based Routing" (ICPADS 2007), full text read.

The paper gives the success formula directly. With m the fraction of adversarial nodes, d the number of disjoint paths, and h_i the path-length distribution, the probability that a lookup succeeds is:

P_K = Σ_i h_i · (1 − (1 − (1−m)^i)^d)

The mechanism: the initiator takes the k closest nodes from its own routing table and distributes them into d independent lookup buckets, then runs d parallel lookups in which each node is used only once across the entire lookup, which is what makes the paths disjoint. A lookup succeeds when at least one path contains no adversarial node.

Measured in the OverSim simulator at N=10,000 nodes with s=16 siblings: **with 20% adversarial nodes, 99% of lookups succeed when disjoint paths are used.** The paper's recommendation is d=4 to 8 with k=8 to 16, and it states that higher values of d and k are not worth the additional communication cost. Communication overhead increases linearly with d.

Two further findings from the same paper that the architecture should adopt:

**Adaptive bucket size is worse than fixed.** Setting k = 2d to save bandwidth produced a lower success fraction than fixed k=16, because the smaller routing table increases average path length, and the success formula is exponentially sensitive to path length. Keep k at 16 and vary only d.

**A sibling list replaces Kademlia's irregular bucket-splitting rule.** Kademlia's exception — splitting buckets that do not contain the node's own ID — exists to prevent an incomplete neighborhood in an unbalanced tree, and it makes the protocol substantially harder to implement. S/Kademlia replaces it with an explicit sorted sibling list of size η·s, where η ≥ 2c ≥ 5 suffices with high probability. At s=16 and c=2.0, the probability of an incomplete sibling list is 6.6 × 10⁻⁴; at s=32 and c=3.0 it is 1.0 × 10⁻¹³. This decouples the replication factor (s) from the routing redundancy factor (k), which Kademlia conflates.

**Two crypto puzzles gate node-ID generation.** A static puzzle requires finding a key pair whose double-hashed public key begins with c₁ zero bits, which prevents free choice of node ID (the Eclipse defense). A dynamic puzzle requires finding X such that H(nodeID ⊕ X) begins with c₂ zero bits, which makes generating many IDs expensive (the Sybil defense). c₁ is fixed at key-generation time; c₂ can be raised over time as hardware improves. Verification is O(1); creation is O(2^c₁ + 2^c₂).

## The 32-walker random walk reduces messages by two orders of magnitude, and square-root replication reduces them by another factor of four

Qin Lv, Pei Cao, Edith Cohen, Kai Li, Scott Shenker, "Search and Replication in Unstructured Peer-to-Peer Networks" (ICS 2002), full text read.

**Topologies tested:** Power-Law Random Graph (9,230 nodes, α=0.8, max degree 1,746, median degree 1), Normal Random Graph (9,836 nodes, max degree 13), the actual Gnutella topology captured October 2000 (4,736 nodes, max degree 136), and a 100×100 grid (10,000 nodes).

**The k-walker result.** A single random walker cuts messages by an order of magnitude versus expanding ring, at an order of magnitude increase in user-perceived delay. Sending k walkers cuts the delay by a factor of k, because k walkers after T steps reach approximately the same node count as 1 walker after kT steps. The paper states 16 to 64 walkers give good results. Against flooding, the 32-walker configuration reduces message overhead by roughly two orders of magnitude across all four topologies, with hops rising from 2–6 to 4–15.

**Termination must be adaptive, and TTL does not work.** The paper tested two termination methods and concluded that "checking" — each walker periodically contacts the originator to ask whether to continue — is correct, and TTL-based termination reproduces the TTL-selection problem of flooding. Checking every fourth step balances the checking overhead against its benefit. Message implosion at the originator is avoided because the walker count is fixed at 16–64.

**The three design principles the paper extracts**, which apply to any unstructured search design: termination must be adaptive; message duplication must be minimized (each query should visit a node once); and coverage granularity must be small, meaning each additional step should increase the node count by a constant rather than exponentially. Flooding fails all three.

**Square-root replication and what it actually requires.** With n sites, m objects, object i replicated at r_i sites and requested at rate q_i, the average search size is A = n·Σ(q_i/r_i). Uniform replication (r_i = R/m) and proportional replication (r_i = R·q_i) give **exactly the same** average search size, m/ρ, independent of the query distribution. Square-root replication (r_i ∝ √q_i) minimizes A, giving A_optimal = (1/ρ)(Σ√q_i)².

The distributed mechanism that achieves it: replicate each object at a number of sites proportional to the search size that found it. Under a k-walker search, the node count between requester and provider is 1/k of the total visited, so **path replication** (store at every node along the path from requester to provider) yields square-root distribution. Measured against owner replication (store only at the requester, which yields proportional distribution): path replication reduces average messages per node by a factor of 2.95, and random replication (store at p randomly chosen nodes among those the walkers visited, where p is the path length) by a factor of 3.91. Queries finishing within four hops: 71% for owner, 86% for path, 91% for random.

**The deletion-policy constraint that is easy to miss.** For either scheme to reach its fixed point, replica lifetimes must be independent of object identity and query rate. Fixed lifetimes, FIFO replacement, and random deletion satisfy this. **LRU and LFU do not**, because square-root replication gives different utilization rates to replicas of different objects, so under usage-based eviction the scheme converges to a different and non-optimal fixed point. A system that uses LRU eviction cannot reach square-root replication by path replication.

**Topology finding.** Uniform random graphs outperform power-law graphs for both search and replication. In power-law and Gnutella topologies, high-degree nodes carry disproportionate load and produce higher duplication because many nodes' neighbors overlap. The paper's recommendation is to use graph-building algorithms that reduce the likelihood of very-high-degree nodes.

## Two malicious nodes out of 800 destroyed 15% to 60% of stored content within about three hours

Nathan Evans, Chris GauthierDickey, Christian Grothoff, "Routing in the Dark: Pitch Black" (ACSAC 2007), full text read.

**The mechanism attacked.** Freenet 0.7 assigns each node a location in [0,1) and periodically swaps locations between node pairs to cluster nodes with similar locations, using a probabilistic acceptance rule based on the product of distances to each node's neighbors. The swap is accepted deterministically if it reduces the product, and probabilistically otherwise to escape local minima.

**The attack.** An attacker sets its location to a target value m and initiates swap requests claiming neighbor locations that favor the swap. In a friend-to-friend topology the victim cannot verify the claim, because it cannot communicate with the attacker's neighbors. After the swap, the attacker resets its own location to m and repeats with another neighbor. Victims then propagate the malicious location through ordinary swapping.

**Measured results.** Testbed of up to 800 nodes on up to 18 GNU/Linux machines running actual Freenet 0.7 code, connected in a Kleinberg 2d-torus small-world topology with O(log²n) connections per node. Content was seeded at one quarter of total network storage capacity. The attack began after 75 iterations of normal operation; each iteration is 90 seconds. **After 200 iterations, the network had lost on average between 15% and 60% of its content, depending on whether 2, 4, or 8 attack nodes were used, with 8 target locations.** Each attacker was granted exactly the same resources as an ordinary user.

**The finding that matters more than the attack.** Ordinary join-leave churn produces the same degeneration with no adversary present. When short-lived fringe peers join with random locations and swap with a stable, well-connected core, the swap rule systematically transfers core locations outward and concentrates the core around a few values. Simulation with 600 nodes (500 stable, 10 of the remaining 100 replaced per round) showed locations converging to a small set after 100 rounds and severely after 100,000. The paper notes this degeneration had been observed and reported by Freenet users but never explained.

**No effective countermeasure was found.** Increasing swap intervals or periodically resetting locations to random values limits the attack but also prevents the convergence that makes the routing efficient in the first place. Detecting an attacker by network-size estimation fails because size cannot be reliably estimated in an open friend-to-friend network. Proving a node's neighbor count by secure multiparty computation fails because in a restricted-route topology the computation can be faked with sufficient compute, and allowing direct communication with a node's neighbors to verify would reveal the topology the design exists to hide.

The conclusion the authors state: secure and efficient routing in restricted-route networks remains an open problem. **This rules out location-swapping small-world routing for any design that must tolerate either adversaries or ordinary churn**, which is every real deployment.

## Range-based set reconciliation completes in a number of rounds logarithmic in the smaller set

Aljoscha Meyer, "Range-Based Set Reconciliation" (IEEE SRDS 2023; arXiv 2212.13567), read.

**Mechanism.** Both peers hold a totally ordered set. One peer computes a fingerprint over all items in a range and sends it. The other computes the fingerprint over its own items in the same range. Equal fingerprints mean the range is already synchronized and needs no further work. Unequal fingerprints leave two options: split the range and recurse on each half concurrently, or, when the range holds few enough items, transmit the items directly.

**Round complexity.** With a threshold t (a node sends the item set for a range only when it holds at most t items in that range) and branching factor b, the round count is bounded by 2 + 2·⌈log_b(n_min)⌉ − ⌊log_b(t)⌋, where n_min is the smaller set size. Raising t reduces round trips and increases the chance of transmitting items the other peer already holds.

**What the 2023 paper contributes over prior presentations:** a generic description independent of any specific fingerprinting scheme, a characterization of the fingerprint design space that permits surveying cryptographically secure schemes, and a reduction of local computation time by a logarithmic factor.

**Follow-on work.** Meyer and Scherer (preprint, 2024) show range-based reconciliation can be realized with conventional cryptographic hashes over history-independent, clamping-invariant search trees, removing the homomorphic-hashing requirement. A 2026 paper (arXiv 2603.19820) formalizes the storage interface as a range-summarizable order-statistics store, separating protocol complexity (queried range count Q and explicit output size K) from storage complexity (B+-tree height h), with local time O(Qh). Willow protocol implements a 3-dimensional variant. Nostr NIP-77 (Negentropy) is the deployed implementation.

**The predecessor line** is Minsky and Trachtenberg's characteristic-polynomial set reconciliation (Allerton 2002; IEEE Trans. Information Theory 49(9), 2003), which achieves near-optimal communication complexity but requires knowing or bounding the difference size in advance.

## Bloom filters lose their false-positive guarantee against an adversary who inserts and observes

David Clayton, Christopher Patton, Thomas Shrimpton, "Probabilistic Data Structures in Adversarial Environments" (ACM CCS 2019).

The classical false-positive bound assumes the data and queries are chosen non-adaptively and independently of the randomness used to build the structure. The paper gives a provable-security treatment with a syntax covering a wide range of in-use structures, and shows that an adversary permitted to perform insertions and observe results drives the observed error rate above the nominal bound. **The proposed fix is a salt and keyed pseudo-random functions** rather than an unkeyed hash.

Related results in the same line: Naor and Yogev (CRYPTO 2015) proved that non-trivial adversarially-robust Bloom filters exist if and only if one-way functions exist. Naor and Oved (2022) generalized the robustness notions. Clayton, Patton, and Shrimpton's follow-up on compact frequency estimators (CCS 2023) showed that Count-Min sketch and heavy-keeper structures can be forced into significant estimation errors by concrete attacks exploiting adaptivity, and proposed Count-Keeper as a composition of the two.

**The architectural consequence:** any gossiped filter must be keyed per-recipient or per-epoch, and an unkeyed filter published to untrusted peers has no meaningful false-positive guarantee. This compounds with the privacy result below.

## The BIP37 deployment record shows what publishing a filter over held content costs

Arthur Gervais, Srdjan Capkun, Ghassan Karame, Damian Gruber, "On the Privacy Provisions of Bloom Filters in Lightweight Bitcoin Clients" (ACSAC 2014). Bitcoin Improvement Proposal 37 (2012) had light clients send a Bloom filter of their addresses to serving full nodes. The paper showed a serving node recovers much of a wallet's address set from one filter, and that intersecting two filters from the same client strips the false positives that were supposed to provide cover. Bitcoin Core 0.19 (2019) disabled `-peerbloomfilters` by default, and the ecosystem moved to server-side compact block filters (BIP157/158), where the server builds the filter and the client downloads it — inverting who learns what.

## Decentralized continuous group key agreement removes the total-order requirement from group messaging

Matthew Weidner, Martin Kleppmann, Daniel Hugenroth, Alastair Beresford, "Key Agreement for Decentralized Secure Group Messaging with Strong Security Guarantees" (ACM CCS 2021; ePrint 2020/1281).

**What it provides.** Existing secure group messaging targets a centralized model where all messages route through one server trusted to impose a consistent total order on group-state updates. DCGKA removes that requirement: it establishes shared symmetric keys in dynamic groups over a network with no central authority, where servers may be used but are trusted less. It provides forward secrecy and post-compromise security, handles members being added and removed at any time, and handles devices being offline.

**What it requires.** Causal order only, which a content-addressed DAG already supplies. This is the reason it composes with a DAG-based system where MLS does not: MLS (RFC 9420) and TreeKEM require a delivery service that imposes total order.

**Cost.** The paper's Table 1 compares against related work by the number of public-key cryptographic operations; total broadcast message size equals the sender column except for Sender Keys and Matrix, which have total broadcast network cost O(n²). The pairwise Signal fan-out that Sender Keys and Matrix use is the O(n²) case that DCGKA improves on.

**Note on the concurrency handling**, which is where the protocol earns its complexity: the `process-seed` function has a distinct code path for concurrent messages, and acknowledgments are recorded in history because the member-view function must determine group membership in the face of concurrent additions and removals — a problem the paper notes several prior protocols do not specify how to solve.

## The anonymity trilemma has been strengthened since the 2018 result

Debajyoti Das, Sebastian Meiser, Esfandiar Mohammadi, Aniket Kate, "Anonymity Trilemma: Strong Anonymity, Low Bandwidth Overhead, Low Latency — Choose Two" (IEEE S&P 2018; ePrint 2017/954). The result: an anonymous communication protocol achieves at most two of strong anonymity (anonymity up to a negligible chance), low bandwidth overhead, and low latency overhead, against a global passive network-level adversary. For a given number of passively compromised protocol nodes, the paper derives a necessary relationship between bandwidth and latency overhead whose violation makes strong anonymity impossible.

**The 2020 strengthening, which was not in the first pass.** Das, Meiser, Mohammadi, and Kate, "Comprehensive Anonymity Trilemma: User Coordination is not enough" (PoPETs 2020) observes that the 2018 analysis excludes protocols where users proactively coordinate to improve their anonymity, shows such protocols achieve better anonymity than the 2018 bound predicts, and then presents a stronger impossibility result covering every anonymous communication network the authors are aware of. The paper also derives qualitatively stricter requirements for the anytrust assumption. **A design cannot escape the bound by adding user coordination.**

A third paper in the line, "Anonymity Trilemma: Beyond Mix-Nets" (Das, Meiser, Mohammadi, Kate), identifies the class of protocols that exceed mix-net anonymity through out-of-band user coordination.

## Corrections to the first research pass

**The IPFS DHT publication latency figures stand**, but the S/Kademlia result changes the security analysis: the first pass stated that disjoint paths raise success against a 20% adversary from 41% to 79% at three paths. The primary source shows 79% is the three-path figure and **99% is achieved at d=8 with k=16**, which is the paper's own recommended configuration. Eight disjoint paths, not three, is the operating point the paper supports.

**Square-root replication requires a feedback signal that a privacy-preserving search channel destroys.** Cohen and Shenker's optimality result, as implemented by path replication in Lv et al., requires each node on a successful search path to observe *which* object succeeded so it can replicate that object. A search channel that returns only an opaque match/no-match result cannot support path replication, because a forwarding node never learns what to replicate. This is a genuine incompatibility between the strongest published replication result and query privacy, and no published work resolves it.

**LRU eviction is incompatible with square-root replication.** Stated in Lv et al. and easy to miss: usage-based eviction policies change the fixed point. Any system that both wants square-root replication and uses LRU cache eviction will not converge to the optimum.

## Correction to the MINERVA figures used in the SPIP document

The SPIP document states that MINERVA was "measured on 1.2 million .gov documents across 750 peers: contacting 45–130 peers reaches the recall levels the paper targets." The VLDB 2005 paper (Bender, Michel, Triantafillou, Weikum, Zimmer, "MINERVA: Collaborative P2P Search") states that the .GOV data was partitioned into **50 overlapping collections**, and its recall figure plots recall against **1 to 20 queried peers**. The paper's stated finding is that a small number of carefully selected peers already supply the large majority of relevant results, and that accounting for overlap between peer collections — rather than ranking peers by estimated quality alone, as CORI does — sharply reduces how many peers must be contacted to reach a given recall. The 750-peer and 1.2-million-document figures do not appear in this paper and may come from a different paper in the MINERVA series; they should not be cited against this one.

MINERVA's architecture is worth stating precisely because it is the closest published relative of the hybrid index selected earlier in this document: every peer keeps its own local index and answers every query locally first, incurring no network cost when the local answer satisfies the user; only when it does not does the peer retrieve per-term peer lists from a Chord-style distributed directory, select a small number of promising peers, and query those directly point to point.

# Third research pass: three papers read in full from uploaded copies

## BeeKEM achieves logarithmic group-key update cost without a central server, superseding DCGKA for this architecture

Derek Yen, Andrés Fábrega, Liangrun Da, Martin Kleppmann, John Mumm, Sunoo Park, Brooklyn Zelenka, "BeeKEM: Decentralized, Secure and Efficient Group Key Agreement," Cryptology ePrint Archive Paper 2026/1434. Read in full.

**This replaces the DCGKA selection made earlier in this document.** The earlier selection of Weidner et al.'s DCGKA (referred to in the BeeKEM paper as WKHB, after the authors' initials) was made because it was the only decentralized construction with security proofs. BeeKEM is now the first decentralized continuous group key agreement scheme with **O(log n) update cost in the common case** and security proofs, where WKHB's cost is O(n). Measured asymptotic costs from the paper's Table 1:

| Scheme | Sender primitives | Recipient primitives | Message size | Persistent storage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OpenMLS (centralized) | O(log n) | O(1) | O(log n) broadcast | O(n) |
| BeeKEM (decentralized) | O(log n) | O(1) existing recipient | O(log n) broadcast | O(n + h_B) |
| WKHB (decentralized) | O(n) | O(1) | O(n) | O(n² + h_D) |

where n is group size, h_B is BeeKEM's operation-history size, and h_D is WKHB's group-membership-history size.

**Mechanism.** BeeKEM belongs to the Asynchronous Ratcheting Trees family, as does TreeKEM inside MLS. It maintains a perfect binary tree in which each leaf holds a member's identifier and public key, each inner node holds a public key plus encryptions of the matching private key, and the root holds the group secret. The invariant is that access to any node's private key suffices to decrypt the private key of every node on the path from that node to the root, so any member recovers the group secret by decrypting upward from their own leaf with no interaction. Each inner node's secret is encrypted under a key derived by non-interactive key exchange between the node's two children, so either child recovers it. The implementation uses Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman for the key exchange and ChaCha20-Poly1305 for symmetric encryption.

**What makes it decentralized.** BeeKEM records the full operation history in a second structure, a hash directed acyclic graph called the operation graph, in which each operation contains the hashes of its predecessors. This supplies causal order without any party imposing a total order. When concurrent operations are detected, BeeKEM replays the entire history in a deterministic topological sort, so two members who have received the same set of operations compute an identical tree regardless of arrival order. The paper states directly that BeeKEM is a conflict-free replicated data type.

**The security property that did not exist before.** Cross-Fork Security: when the group forks into branches and an attacker compromises a member who performed an update on one branch, the attacker cannot read group secrets from updates on a branch that diverged before that update. Weidner et al. noted this attack informally and their scheme is not secure against it. BeeKEM is the first decentralized construction that defines the property formally and proves it.

**The tradeoff parameter that must be chosen before deployment.** BeeKEM takes a key-retention parameter κ: each member keeps their κ most recent personal secrets so they can read group secrets produced on other branches during a partition. Raising κ improves Correctness Under Concurrency (the ability to catch up on another branch's messages after a partition heals) and weakens both Forward Secrecy and Cross-Fork Security, giving κ-FSU and κ-CFS rather than the full properties. At κ=1 both reduce to the full properties and cross-branch recovery is lost entirely. The authors conjecture this tradeoff is inherent to the decentralized setting. **This is a deployment decision with no correct default: a system that expects long partitions needs high κ and accepts weaker forward secrecy.**

Two variants are sketched. BeeKEM^FS uses Updatable Public-Key Encryption so that a key pair encrypts only one message, and members delete old secrets immediately on processing an update, which gives full Forward Secrecy and Cross-Fork Security and removes cross-branch recovery. BeeKEM^PQ replaces the non-interactive key exchange with a key encapsulation mechanism, giving post-quantum security.

**Measured performance.** Simulated on one machine with a 16-core AMD processor and 128 GB of memory, group sizes 8 to 512, 5 runs per configuration with the median reported. BeeKEM had the lowest CPU time of the three systems for both senders and recipients. OpenMLS showed linear rather than logarithmic growth at large group sizes, which the authors traced to OpenMLS recomputing tree and parent hashes across the whole ratchet tree at O(n) cost, not to TreeKEM itself. BeeKEM is the most expensive of the three for a newly added member, who must replay the O(n) operation history; each prior Update adds 2.5 kB to the welcome message and 40 µs to the new member's processing time.

**Behavior under partition, measured.** A 64-member group was split into four equal subsets, updates were performed during the partition, and the partition was then healed. Post-partition recovery cost rises linearly with the fraction of members who updated during the partition and plateaus once every member has updated once; further updates add little. Worst-case conflict resolution is linear in group size.

**Why this matters beyond efficiency.** The paper's stated motivation is the same threat this architecture addresses. It cites 313 internet shutdowns across 52 countries in 2025, Iran's months-long national shutdown in early 2026 that permitted domestic services while restricting international access, Russia's intermittent restrictions pushing users toward a state-surveilled national messaging application, and the Hong Kong protests of 2014 and 2019 where mesh-networking applications were used and later found to have weak security. Under a centralized delivery service, a partition leaves the service on one side, and members on the other side cannot add members, remove members, update keys, or communicate.

**Revised selection: use BeeKEM, not DCGKA/WKHB.** It costs O(log n) rather than O(n), it requires only the causal order the content-addressed operation graph already supplies, it has a production-ready open-source implementation, and it is the only decentralized scheme with proven Cross-Fork Security. Set κ according to expected partition duration.

## 4P demonstrates 76% recall at 313 messages per query against 9,108 to 13,379 messages for flooding

Niels Zeilemaker, Johan Pouwelse, Henk Sips, "4P: Performant Private Peer-to-Peer File Sharing," 14th IEEE International Conference on Peer-to-Peer Computing, 2014. DOI 10.1109/P2P.2014.6934311. Read in full.

**The measurements are real and the experimental conditions are these.** The dataset came from a deployed semantic overlay (Tribler), from which the preferences of more than 75,000 peers were collected. 1,000 peers were selected at random, each required to have at least 10 preferences, with items preferred by only one peer removed. Each peer's preferences were split 80% training and 20% testing; the training set found similar peers and the testing set generated queries. The 10 most similar peers were computed for each peer. The emulation ran 1,000 peer processes across 10 supercomputer nodes, with 200 peers issuing queries.

| Strategy | Recall | Mean messages per query |
|---|---|---|
| Random overlay | 9% | 20 |
| Semantic overlay | 40% | 20 |
| 4P | 76% | 313 |
| RetroShare | 86% | 10,316 |
| Gnutella | 98% | 13,379 |
| OneSwarm | 99% | 9,108 |

The 313 figure includes replies from all peers; only 157 search messages are sent. The paper states that 4P's message count is set by the initial TTL, the initial extension probability, and the final extension probability, and does not grow with network size, whereas RetroShare's and OneSwarm's counts depend on how many friends each peer has.

**The three privacy mechanisms and their parameter values.** The initial extension probability (IEP) is the probability that a peer receiving a query at the maximum TTL declines to decrement it, which prevents the next peer from determining whether its predecessor originated the query. The final extension probability (FEP) is the probability that a peer forwards a query that arrived with TTL=1, which prevents an attacker from sending TTL=1 queries to enumerate one peer's holdings. The probability that at least one peer extends the initial path is PIE = 1 − (1 − IEP)^(initial TTL), and the paper reports empirically that flooding does not occur while PIE stays below 0.5. The experiments used initial TTL 4, IEP 0.15, FEP 0.5, giving PIE 0.45 and an expected initial extension of 1.5 hops.

**Correction to the SPIP document's stated objection.** The SPIP document refused 4P's homomorphic similarity on the grounds that "its private-set-intersection construction requires a global list of all possible items with fixed indices." The paper describes no such requirement. Alice builds a polynomial whose roots are the items in her own set, encrypts the coefficients under Paillier, and sends them; Bob evaluates the polynomial homomorphically at each item in his set, multiplies each result by a fresh random number, adds a session key he generated for Alice, permutes the list, and returns it; Alice decrypts and identifies the session key as the repeated 128-bit value, with the repetition count giving the similarity. No enumeration of a global vocabulary occurs. The paper does apply a hash that bounds the item space to 2^40, which is a bound on the space, not a list that must be transmitted or indexed. **The stated objection does not hold against the paper as written.** A different objection does hold and the paper states it directly: a single handshake costs approximately 79.5 kBytes on average, and Alice must attach a proof of work (the paper suggests HashCash requiring a hash with at least 20 leading zero bits) to each request, because otherwise Alice could repeatedly run the protocol with one preference at a time to enumerate Bob's set.

**Anonymity, as the paper's own table states it.** Against a local eavesdropper defined as an ISP observing all of one peer's traffic, both sender and receiver anonymity are "provably exposed", because the eavesdropper sees that no query arrived before one was sent, and sees response size grow. Against collaborating peers, both are "probably innocent." The paper notes that encrypting, padding, and combining messages would improve the local-eavesdropper case, which is what a padded-cell transport supplies.

**One design element worth adopting independently of the rest.** 4P separates the search path from the content path. A search returns metadata only; downloading then runs over a tunnel established by keeping open the connection along which the result was back-propagated. This means intermediate peers do not cache whole files, which is the cost Freenet pays. The download uses a modified Peer-to-Peer Streaming Peer Protocol that splits data into pieces, verifies them with a Merkle tree, and multiplexes parallel downloads from several sources over a single socket. Each peer in the search path appends its own results, sorts by the same relevance ranking, and forwards only the top K, which prevents peers from inferring path length by counting results.

## HSkip+ solves the heterogeneous-capacity problem that Kademlia leaves open, and self-stabilizes from any connected state

Matthias Feldotto, Christian Scheideler, Kalman Graffi, "HSkip+: A Self-Stabilizing Overlay Network for Nodes with Heterogeneous Bandwidths," 14th IEEE International Conference on Peer-to-Peer Computing, 2014. Read in full. This paper was not on the request list and addresses a problem this document had left unresolved.

**The problem it solves.** Every DHT selected earlier in this document distributes load uniformly, which assumes participants have comparable capacity. Real participants do not: the architecture must run on devices ranging from a phone with a metered connection to a rack with a gigabit link. HSkip+ orders participants by bandwidth rather than by identifier, and routes so that a low-capacity participant is never used as transit between two high-capacity participants.

**Topological self-stabilization, which no DHT in this document previously provided.** A self-stabilizing overlay recovers the correct topology from *any* starting state, provided the graph is initially weakly connected. This is stronger than churn tolerance: it covers arbitrary corruption of routing tables, not only participants joining and departing. The paper proves both required properties — convergence (from any weakly connected state the system reaches a legal state) and closure (once legal, it stays legal absent external change) — and proves them **under the asynchronous message-passing model**, where the prior Skip+ result held only for synchronous message passing.

**Measured and proven costs.**

| Property | HSkip+ | Skip+ (prior work) |
|---|---|---|
| Self-stabilization time | O(log n) rounds | O(log n) rounds |
| Self-stabilization messages | O(log²n) | O(log⁴n) |
| Work per join, leave, or bandwidth change | O(log²n) w.h.p. | O(log⁴n) w.h.p. |
| Time per join, leave, or bandwidth change | O(log n) w.h.p. | — |
| Routing dilation | O(log n) w.h.p. | — |
| Expected routing congestion | O(log n) | — |

Simulations ran 100 times per configuration at sizes up to 1,024 participants, with bandwidths drawn from measurements of German connections. Skip+ stabilizes in slightly fewer rounds; HSkip+ uses two orders of magnitude fewer messages at 1,024 nodes.

**The routing guarantee that matters for mixed-capacity networks.** The paper's Lemma 22 states that routing from participant u to participant w uses only participants v with v.bw ≥ min{u.bw, w.bw}. A phone is never conscripted to carry traffic between two servers. Congestion is normalized by each participant's own bandwidth, and the expected normalized congestion is O(log n).

**Churn resilience, measured.** Under a random crash in which a percentage of participants depart simultaneously and an equal number join, all participants remain correctly connected up to a **60% churn rate**. Under an adversarial pattern where departing participants are chosen from one neighborhood rather than at random, the threshold is about **35%**. Above these, the topology loses participants that become unreachable. Routing dilation in a stable 1,024-participant network is about 11 hops, rising only to 14 or 15 during a crash or attack.

**How this changes the architecture.** Kademlia remains the right choice for content lookup by identifier, because content lookup must be uniform across the identifier space and because Kademlia has the deployment measurement base. HSkip+ addresses a different function: organizing participants by capacity so that work can be assigned in proportion to what each participant can supply. The two coexist. Adopt HSkip+ as the capacity-ordered membership layer that decides which participants serve as relays, caches, and high-capacity indexes, and keep Kademlia for content location. The self-stabilization property also supplies something Kademlia lacks: a proven recovery path from arbitrary routing-table corruption, which is the state an adversary aims to produce.

**One requirement to check before adopting.** The construction assumes bandwidth values are unique across participants, which the paper notes is easy to arrange given unique identifiers, and assumes participants report bandwidth honestly. The paper does not address a participant that misreports its bandwidth to place itself advantageously in the ordering. That is an open problem for adoption in an adversarial setting, and it belongs in the open-problems table.
