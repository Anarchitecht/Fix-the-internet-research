## Moderation: Client-subscribed labeling services are selected for unwanted-but-lawful content, because every mechanism acting at server or list granularity removes content for every viewer of that server regardless of individual preference; illegal content instead routes through an identifier deny list honored at retrieval, and the one live measurement of that mechanism in this corpus shows voluntary, unevenly enforced adoption defeated by re-encoding the same bytes under an alternate identifier

A content identifier in this architecture is a fixed value derived from a piece of content's own bytes, so no protocol-level action can strike a record from existence: changing the bytes changes the identifier, and every node already holding a copy of the old identifier still answers requests for it unless that node itself chooses to refuse. Moderation is therefore never deletion at the identifier layer; it is a decision, made somewhere, to withhold a response or a display for a request that would otherwise succeed. Two distinct decisions get called moderation, and the evidence below shows they need different mechanisms because they answer different questions. Unwanted content is content a specific viewer, or a specific server operator, does not want shown, for a reason particular to that viewer or operator — nudity, harassment, a topic, a language — and the correct action is filtering: a decision that legitimately differs from one viewer to the next. Illegal content is content whose possession or distribution a jurisdiction criminalizes outright, the clearest case in the measured evidence being child sexual abuse material; the correct action is refusal to serve it to anyone, not a per-viewer preference.

### Selection

For unwanted-but-lawful content, independently operated labeling services, each client subscribed individually, are selected over instance-level blocking and shared policy lists. For illegal content, an identifier deny list honored at retrieval is selected as the only mechanism this corpus evidences in live deployment — selected not because it works, but because it is the sole candidate measured, and its own measurement shows it failing along two specific, stated dimensions rather than succeeding.

Against instance-level blocking: the labeler mechanism's filtering unit is one object and one viewer's subscription list; a labeler's decision reaches only the viewers who chose to subscribe to that labeler, and 3.2% cross-labeler overlap shows labelers operate largely independently of one another rather than converging on one shared judgment [`BALDUF-IMC-24`]. Instance-level blocking's filtering unit is one remote domain and every locally hosted user simultaneously: a block removes a domain's content for every viewer on the blocking instance whether or not that particular viewer objects to that content [`BONO-WEBSCI-24`], and the measured 82.3-day average response delay [`ANAOBI-WWW-23`] means the decision, once made, is both coarse and slow. The interview evidence adds a cost the labeler mechanism does not carry in the same form: instance-level blocking is performed by a human administrator who reports the work as tedious, stressful, and in one documented case exposed them directly to child-abuse material while triaging what to block [`ZHANG-PACMHCI-24`] — the labeler mechanism does not eliminate human judgment, since a labeler is still typically an operated service, but it does not concentrate that judgment and its exposure onto one person per server who must decide for every user of that server at once.

Against shared policy lists: this candidate inherits instance-level blocking's whole-domain granularity and adds a further, less transparent trust hop — none of the five measured lists discloses its per-domain evidence, and one incorporates further undisclosed lists on top of its own [`ZHANG-ARXIV-25`]. The labeler mechanism already supplies the function a shared list provides — delegating a filtering judgment to a party a viewer trusts — through per-viewer subscription, without imposing that delegated judgment on every other user of the same server. The collaborative-classifier variant of this candidate is the more capable of the two: FedMod raises a small instance's macro-F1 by 12.69% over training alone [`ZIA-ARXIV-25`], and the conversation-model variant raises small-instance macro-F1 from 0.60 to 0.8826 [`AGARWAL-ICWSM-24`]. Both remain, however, an instance-wide classification applied uniformly to every user of the receiving instance, not a per-viewer choice, and both degrade as participating instances' own policies diverge — FedMod's benefit disappears entirely once half of peers hold an inverted label definition [`ZIA-ARXIV-25`]. Evidence that individual viewers' own "unwanted" judgment is itself heterogeneous, not one shared signal a classifier or a list could converge on, comes from a fully user-controlled setting: on memo.cash, the strongest predictor of which user gets personally muted is that user's own platform-activity count, not any hatefulness or toxicity score, which ranks 26th among 406 candidate features [`ZUO-ICWSM-24`]. A mechanism whose filtering unit is one viewer's own subscription list accommodates that heterogeneity directly; a mechanism whose filtering unit is one shared list or one shared classifier, applied identically to every subscribing instance's users, does not.

Against reserving the labeler mechanism for illegal content too, instead of a separate deny list: none of the three per-viewer or per-server mechanisms above stops a person who never subscribed to any relevant labeler, and is not a user of any blocking instance, from retrieving illegal content directly — filtering is opt-in by construction, and illegal content specifically requires refusal that does not depend on any viewer's or administrator's opt-in choice. The identifier deny list is the only candidate in this corpus built to act at the point every retrieval passes through, independent of which labelers or blocks any particular requester has chosen. Its own measured failure — enforcement voluntary and uneven (≈100% at the maintaining organization's own gateways versus ≈18% at CDN-operated ones), and near-total evasion by re-encoding the same bytes under an alternate valid identifier (97.4% of usually-blocked identifiers) [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`] — is a documented limitation of the only measured candidate, not evidence that a labeler or blocking mechanism would do better at this specific job; those mechanisms were never measured operating at this job's required granularity (every request, independent of viewer choice) at all.

### What this selection requires from the rest of the system

- The naming or identity component supplies one stable, network-wide identifier per moderable object (post, account, media item) that a labeler can attach a label to and that a deny-list check can be run against; the labeler mechanism's own measured deployment attaches labels to identifiers drawn from exactly this kind of per-object identity scheme [`BALDUF-IMC-24`].
- Either a content-location or indexing component supplies a service equivalent to the measured deployment's centralized AppView — subscribing to every known labeler, storing every label, and answering a client's "does object X carry a label from any labeler I subscribe to" query — or every client bears the ≈30 GB/day full-stream bandwidth cost of consuming labelers' streams directly [`BALDUF-IMC-24`]; no entry in this corpus measures a bandwidth or storage cost for the aggregation-service alternative, so which of these two paths is viable is unsettled (see below).
- The retrieval-serving component — whatever node or gateway answers a request for a given content identifier — performs the deny-list check at the moment of serving, before returning bytes, since the underlying content-addressed store supplies no deletion primitive at the identifier layer [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`].
- The storage-encoding or naming component supplies a canonicalization step mapping every valid identifier encoding of one underlying byte sequence to one comparable check target before the deny-list check runs; absent this, a re-encoding under any of the other supported identifier schemes evades the check, as measured [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`].
- Some network-observation capability — sustained passive monitoring of retrieval and lookup traffic over a long window and large request volume — is needed to translate a hash-only deny-list entry back into an actual, checkable content identifier, mirroring the passive Bitswap and DHT observation the source measurement itself relied on [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`].
- Sybil resistance or an equivalent registration control is explicitly not assumed for labeler registration: any account can register as a labeler in the one measured deployment [`BALDUF-IMC-24`], so the choice of which labelers are trustworthy is left entirely to each client, and no mechanism in this corpus is evidenced to help a client make that choice at the labeler-discovery problem's scale (see below).
- The illegal-content deny-list check and the unwanted-content labeling mechanism are independent and both mandatory where applicable: a client's labeler subscriptions must not be treated as satisfying, replacing, or gating the deny-list check, since the deny-list check's entire value is that it does not depend on any viewer's subscription choice.

### What it costs and where it fails

The labeler mechanism's clearest measured cost is the bandwidth of the only alternative to a centralizing aggregator: ≈30 GB/day per client for full network visibility, the condition under which the one live deployment's own authors state it "remains to be seen" whether an independently operated (non-platform-operated) equivalent of that aggregator is economically viable [`BALDUF-IMC-24`]. It fails to supply coverage when no relevant labeler yet exists for a given kind of content or community: for eleven months after this deployment's launch, only one labeler (the platform operator's own) existed at all, and the 88.7% share of monthly label volume now issued by independently operated labelers accumulated only after public labeler registration opened [`BALDUF-IMC-24`] — a client relying on this mechanism from a network's earliest days has thin coverage until an independently operated population of labelers has time to form.

The identifier deny list's measured cost is the ≈24-hour reporting-to-listing latency once a takedown request is received, against content persisting "for years" before that report happens at all [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`]. It fails in two independently measured, structurally different ways. First, adoption is voluntary and uneven: enforcement ranges from ≈100% (the maintaining organization's own gateways) to ≈18% (CDN-operated gateways), so the mechanism's actual network-wide coverage is set by the least-compliant class of gateway a requester can reach, not by the existence of the list itself [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`]. Second, even a fully compliant gateway is evaded by an adversary who re-encodes the same bytes under a different valid identifier: 97.4% of usually-blocked identifiers became retrievable this way [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`]. The content actually removed by the one measured deployment of this mechanism skews overwhelmingly toward copyright and phishing content (≈87.97% and ≈5.81% respectively) rather than the CSAM or terrorist-content categories (<0.01% and ≈0.06%) the mechanism is most often justified by [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`] — a fact about what this specific deployment actually does, not a claim about what the mechanism could do differently configured.

### What the corpus does not settle

1. No entry isolates the bandwidth or storage cost of running a label-aggregation service alone (the measured 30 GB/day figure is for the full content-plus-identity-plus-label stream, not labels alone) [`BALDUF-IMC-24`]. A builder choosing between a centralizing aggregator and full-stream client-side consumption has no measured cost for the aggregator side of that choice.
2. No entry measures which labelers users actually subscribe to, or how many labelers a typical user subscribes to; the source paper's own authors state they deliberately did not attempt to infer this, for ethical reasons [`BALDUF-IMC-24`]. Whether the labeler mechanism achieves adequate coverage for an ordinary user in practice, rather than only in principle, is unmeasured.
3. No entry proposes or measures a labeler-discovery mechanism — how a new client, with no prior relationships, learns which of many independently operated labelers exist and are worth subscribing to. This is a cold-start problem structurally similar to the naming component's own first-contact bootstrap problem, and no paper in this corpus addresses it for labelers specifically.
4. No entry measures a canonicalization step or a content-based (non-hash) detection layer actually deployed and resisting the alternate-identifier evasion the deny-list measurement documents; the source paper's own proposed improvement (tighter anti-phishing-service integration, measured as a 227% increase in detected phishing content and a 43% reduction in reporting latency) was evaluated only by the authors' own methodology and explicitly "not deployed on the live network," and it addresses reporting speed for one content category, not the hash-substitution evasion itself [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`]. The identifier-canonicalization problem this selection depends on is an open problem in this corpus, not a solved one — stated here as a guess-labeled gap, because no entry in the corpus measures a working solution to it.
5. No entry measures whether making deny-list enforcement structurally binding — rather than each gateway operator's voluntary choice — is achievable at all in a design where retrieval nodes are independently operated; the only measured deployment is voluntary, and its own authors describe uneven enforcement as the direct cause of the mechanism "facilitat[ing] [the] spread" of the content it targets [`SOKOTO-USENIXSEC-24`]. What a structurally binding version would require is not stated or tested by any paper in this corpus.
6. No entry measures legal-liability allocation for a retrieval node that does, or does not, enforce a deny list under a specific jurisdiction's law; interview evidence records that administrators themselves report exactly this uncertainty about what they are legally permitted to do about illegal content, with no institutional or protocol-level support described for resolving it [`ZHANG-PACMHCI-24`], but no paper in this corpus measures or resolves the underlying legal question.
