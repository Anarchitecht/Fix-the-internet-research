## Naming: Private, per-zone delegation over self-certifying public keys is selected over synchronized naming ledgers and Domain Name System links, both of which measured deployments show collapsing into single-party control or into a name-scarcity market

Naming resolves a stable identifier to the specific key-holding party or content that identifier currently refers to, so a person or a piece of code can address a target across the target's own changes of location, hosting provider, or key rotation, using a string shorter and more durable than a raw network address. Every candidate below produces or resolves such an identifier by one of two structurally different means: binding the identifier directly to a cryptographic key, so identity and address are one fact verified without a third party, or binding a separately chosen readable string to that key through a third mechanism — a ledger, a per-user delegation graph, or the existing Domain Name System (DNS) — which then becomes the point at which the binding can be contested, captured, or fail.

### The three-property bound

Three separate papers in this corpus each independently state the same impossibility result in their own words, without citing a shared source paper the corpus holds as full text (Wilcox-O'Hearn's original statement of it exists only as an unretrieved archived page; see "What the corpus does not settle" below). The GNU Name System's (GNS) own design paper states it as the premise its design works around: "no name system can provide globally unique and memorable names and be secure" [`WACHS-CANS-14`]. The Ethereum Name Service (ENS) measurement paper states the same conclusion, applied to blockchain naming specifically, as its own finding: "DNS and the aforementioned solutions still cannot achieve human-readability, security and decentralization simultaneously" [`XIA-IMC-22`]. The W3C's Decentralized Identifiers (DID) specification states that mapping a human-friendly identifier to a DID "is outside the scope" of DID resolution and that "this trade-off follows Zooko's Triangle" [`W3C-DIDCORE-22`]. The Namecoin measurement paper states the claim a naming ledger makes against this bound: a blockchain-secured directory can supply "decentralization, security, and human-readable names at once (the three properties jointly conjectured impossible before Bitcoin, called Zooko's triangle)" [`KALODNER-WEIS-15`] — the one claim of the four that asserts an escape, and the one checked against its own paper's measured facts below.

### Selection

Every human-readable label is bound to a self-certifying public key through private, per-zone delegation — GNS's mechanism [`WACHS-CANS-14`] — rather than through a synchronized naming ledger or a Domain Name System link. The raw self-certifying key remains the identifier that always resolves on its own; a label is an additional, non-authoritative convenience a zone owner layers on top through delegation, never a replacement for the key.

Against Namecoin: Namecoin's own founding claim, as this corpus's paper on it frames it, is an escape from the three-property bound through blockchain consensus [`KALODNER-WEIS-15`]. Checked against that same paper's own measured data, the claim survives only by redefining "secure" from an unconditional guarantee into one conditioned on holding a majority of a specific external resource — computational power, roughly one-third of Bitcoin's contemporaneous hash rate through merged mining [`KALODNER-WEIS-15`] — a condition absent from the original three-property statement. Even granting that redefinition, the same paper's data shows "human-readable" degenerating under the resulting competition for names: registration availability falls from 100% at one and two characters to 0.02% at five characters [`KALODNER-WEIS-15`], and at least 76% of registered names are held by squatters, leaving 28 domains, of roughly 120,000 registered, with real, distinct content [`KALODNER-WEIS-15`]. WACHS's own characterization of the same consensus mechanism, stated as fact rather than as a value judgment: an adversary "with more computational power than all other participants combined can construct an alternative valid timeline" [`WACHS-CANS-14`]. GNS's per-link security instead reduces to a single, fixed cryptographic hardness assumption (ECDSA over Curve25519) with no resource-majority condition attached [`WACHS-CANS-14`].

Against Handshake: Handshake secures only the root-zone (TLD) level and explicitly delegates everything below a TLD to conventional DNS operated by that TLD's owner [`HANDSHAKE-WP-18`], so even granting its auction-and-destroy scarcity mechanism, it hands the large majority of readable names — everything below a TLD — back to ordinary domain registration, the same single-registrar-per-name structure the selected mechanism avoids. Its performance figures are project-stated, not independently measured [`HANDSHAKE-WP-18`], so no independently verified cost comparison exists against GNS's stated design bounds. Its own text supplies no quantified bound on the proof-of-work hardware-monopoly risk it acknowledges [`HANDSHAKE-WP-18`], where the selected mechanism's per-link guarantee carries no comparable unquantified risk.

Against ENS: ENS's own measured deployment is the strongest evidence in this corpus that a naming ledger's readable-name property degrades in practice: 7.2% of registered names are squatting by the paper's own two-heuristic count (33,662 of 465,827), expanding to hundreds of thousands more under a guilt-by-association pass [`XIA-IMC-22`], and a name's records are not cleared on expiry, so a resolved binding can point at content its nominal owner no longer controls with no signal to the resolving client [`XIA-IMC-22`]. GNS's per-zone labels are not competed for in an open market and carry no possibility of expiry-linked record persistence, since each zone's owner alone controls that zone's records for as long as that owner's key is uncompromised [`WACHS-CANS-14`].

Against Domain Name System links: the one deployed measurement of this mechanism in the corpus shows it reproducing exactly the single-party control this architecture exists to avoid — 98.9% of measured handles sit under the platform operator's own custodial domain, and even among the 1.1% of users who manage their own domain, half register through four registrars [`BALDUF-IMC-24`]. GNS's zone-delegation mechanism has no registrar or DNS-provider role at all: every zone owner alone signs their own records [`WACHS-CANS-14`], so it cannot reproduce this specific concentration mechanism, though (see below) whether it avoids concentration by a different mechanism — a small number of widely-delegated-into zones becoming de facto naming authorities as the delegation graph grows — is a question this corpus has no deployment data to answer.

Against bare self-certifying identifiers with no naming layer at all (SFS-style: the name is the raw hash, and any human-readable label stays purely local and unpublished): this achieves the same per-link cryptographic guarantee GNS achieves [`MAZIERES-SIGOPSEW-98`] but supplies no network-visible resolution of a human-chosen label — a readable identifier that is readable only to the one person who created the local symbolic link, and resolvable by no other party without a separate, manual, out-of-band exchange for every pairwise relationship [`MAZIERES-SIGOPSEW-98`]. This fails the design requirement that a client reproduce the functional patterns of the most-visited websites, where one user's chosen handle is visible to and resolvable by every other user without a private manual exchange per relationship. GNS is preferred specifically because it publishes labels onto a network-reachable DHT, encrypted per label so a party who does not already hold both the label and the zone's public key cannot enumerate it [`WACHS-CANS-14`], rather than leaving publication entirely outside the protocol as SFS does [`MAZIERES-SIGOPSEW-98`].

### What this selection requires from the rest of the system

- The identity or key-issuance component supplies one ECDSA-over-Curve25519 (or equivalent) key pair per zone a user or service operates; a zone's authority to publish or delegate records is entirely the possession of that zone's private key [`WACHS-CANS-14`].
- The content-location component's DHT accepts publication of encrypted, per-label record blocks and serves them on query, and every other component treats it as authoritative for availability only, never for integrity — each record self-verifies through its own signature and expiration field [`WACHS-CANS-14`].
- The transport or mesh layer supports flooding a small (few-byte) revocation notice to every neighbor, gated by a proof-of-work check the revoker performs once, not one every resolver repeats, to bound flooding abuse [`WACHS-CANS-14`].
- The synchronization and replicated-state component supplies a set-reconciliation mechanism bounding a rejoining or newly partitioned peer's bandwidth to the size of its revocation-set difference from its peers, not to the full revocation set; WACHS cites Eppstein's method for this, and this corpus separately evidences that mechanism family achieving bounded-round reconciliation [`EPPSTEIN-SIGCOMM-11`, `MEYER-TR-24`].
- Whatever component handles first contact between two identities that have never interacted — onboarding, search, or an explicit out-of-band introduction flow — supplies the first delegation for any zone a party has not yet been introduced to; the selected mechanism provides no in-band bootstrap of its own for an unfamiliar zone [`WACHS-CANS-14`], so without this supplied elsewhere, a name into that zone does not resolve at all.
- Any content-location or client-facing resolver consuming this mechanism tolerates indefinite latency, not a definite negative response, for a record that does not exist, because the query-privacy construction makes "not published yet" and "will never exist" indistinguishable to the querying client [`WACHS-CANS-14`]; a client-facing timeout policy must be supplied elsewhere, since the naming mechanism states none of its own.
- Any application layer resolving multi-label names budgets up to roughly 125 sequential zone lookups for the worst-case delegation chain the label-length bound permits, and up to 63 kB per reply payload [`WACHS-CANS-14`], though ordinary chains are shorter.

### What it costs and where it fails

No entry in this corpus measures latency, resolution success rate, or delegation-graph resilience for GNS, or for any private-per-user-scope naming mechanism, in actual deployment [`WACHS-CANS-14`] — a limitation the mechanism's own authors state directly, not one inferred here. The only quantified costs available are protocol-stated design bounds, not figures measured under load: DHT reply payload up to 63 kB, trusted computing base under about 125 entities per individual name (derived from DNS label-length limits carried into GNS names), and revocation notices a few bytes in size [`WACHS-CANS-14`].

It fails at three points its own authors state explicitly. First, first-contact resolution of a zone with no existing delegation is impossible in-band; a party must obtain an initial delegation through an out-of-band channel before any name into that zone resolves [`WACHS-CANS-14`]. Second, an absent record produces indefinite lookup latency rather than a definite negative answer, because the same encryption construction hiding which record is queried from an outside observer also prevents the client from distinguishing "not published yet" from "never will be" [`WACHS-CANS-14`]; the paper's own BOX record reduces, without eliminating, this cost for one specific case (grouping dependent records under a single lookup), not the general case [`WACHS-CANS-14`]. Third, a confirmation attack always succeeds: a party who already holds both a zone's public key and one candidate label can perform the identical query as any other resolver and confirm that record's existence [`WACHS-CANS-14`] — the mechanism defends against blind enumeration of a zone's labels, not against confirming an already-guessed pair.

Overall correctness of a multi-hop delegation chain depends on every zone along that chain having delegated correctly and honestly; compromise or social engineering of any single zone in a chain compromises resolution of every label reachable through it, and the paper's own authors state they cannot give a numeric estimate of this risk because "the system is not deployed yet," with no measurement of a real GNS delegation graph existing anywhere [`WACHS-CANS-14`].

### What the corpus does not settle

1. No entry measures GNS, or any private-per-user-scope naming mechanism, in actual deployment: latency, resolution success rate, and delegation-graph structure under real social-engineering pressure are all unmeasured, deferred by the paper's own authors to future work [`WACHS-CANS-14`]. The selection above rejects the ledger and DNS-link candidates on measured grounds, but does not itself rest on a positive deployment measurement of the selected mechanism; no entry elsewhere in this corpus fills that gap.
2. No entry measures how a new participant with zero existing delegations obtains a first one at social-network scale. The same cold-start problem appears identically for bare self-certifying naming [`MAZIERES-SIGOPSEW-98`] and for GNS [`WACHS-CANS-14`], and no paper in this corpus proposes or measures a solution.
3. Whether combining a naming ledger, used narrowly as an anti-squatting reservation mechanism for a small number of widely recognized root labels, with private per-user scopes for everything reachable through delegation from those roots, would recover a ledger's own measured squatting failure without inheriting its continuous-synchronization cost, is not evaluated by any paper in this corpus. This composition is suggested by reading the individual mechanism descriptions together; it is stated here as an open question, not a finding.
4. No entry evaluates GNS's DHT-key derivation (a scalar multiple of the zone's private key by a representation of the label, hashed to form the DHT lookup key) against an adaptive, query-choosing adversary. Whether an adversary who observes many queries into one zone gains any advantage over blind guessing at further labels in that zone is not measured or proved in the retrieved text.
5. No entry measures whether a delegation-graph structure resembling GNS's, layered under a client reproducing the functional patterns of the most-visited websites — where most users expect a discoverable, memorable handle from first use, not a private key they must distribute out of band — produces adoption or usability outcomes comparable to the default-custodial-domain pattern measured for the AT Protocol [`BALDUF-IMC-24`]. This corpus contains deployment data for the DNS-link and ledger families and none for the selected family, so a like-for-like usability comparison cannot be made from the entries retrieved here.
6. Zooko Wilcox-O'Hearn's original statement of the three-property bound is cited by three papers in this corpus [`WACHS-CANS-14`, `KALODNER-WEIS-15`] but was never itself retrieved as full text: the only archived copy is served from an address this retrieval environment could not reach [retrieval-log.md, "Zooko's original page"]. Every formulation of the bound cited above is therefore a restatement by a citing paper's own authors, checked against that paper's own measured or stated facts, not a quotation of the original proof or its exact scope conditions.
