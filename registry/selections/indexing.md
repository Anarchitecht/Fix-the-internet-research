## Indexing and search: a client-local index answers first, and a probably-approximately-correct random-peer-sample query is the network fallback — the only fallback candidate with an analytically demonstrated path under the standing per-query bound, at web scale, and only under three preconditions this component does not itself supply

A search mechanism lets a person locate content by the words it contains, not by an identifier they must already hold. The standing per-query cost bound for this problem is Li, Loo, Hellerstein, Kaashoek, Karger, and Morris's 1-megabyte budget: 100-gigabit 1999 U.S. Internet backbone bisection bandwidth, 10% of it allocated to search, 1,000 queries per second (`LI-IPTPS-03`). Against that budget, `LI-IPTPS-03` measures naive document-partitioned query broadcast at 6 megabytes per query (6x over) and naive term-partitioned distributed-hash-table (DHT) posting-list intersection at 530 megabytes per query (530x over), both at a 3-billion-document target. No entry in this corpus reports a lower cost under matching conditions — a full-text, ranked, multi-term query, keyword-partitioned, at a multi-billion-document collection. Every lower figure in the corpus changes the document count, the network architecture, or the query semantics, usually more than one at once; this is stated directly, entry by entry, in `registry/conflicts/search.md` section 1 and `registry/open-problems/web-scale-search.md`, and nothing found while building this selection overturns it. The nearest paper is Asthana, Fu, and Cox (`ASTHANA-ICTIR-11`), which reports 1.0-3.6 megabytes per query at a larger, 10-billion-document collection — below Li's own bound — by replacing exact posting-list intersection with probabilistic random-peer sampling, an entirely different mechanism addressed as its own candidate below.

### Selection

A client-local index of content the node has itself fetched, authored, or been shown answers a query first, at no network cost. A query the local index cannot answer is broadcast under the PAC (probably-approximately-correct) protocol to z peers drawn from a random-peer-sampling service: each queried peer searches its own locally held, uniformly random document subset (`COX-ICTIR-09`, `ASTHANA-ICTIR-11`) and returns its top-ranked local matches, each response carrying the local statistics `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14`'s global-statistics-estimation-plus-skewness-filter technique needs to defend the combined ranking against a minority of adversarial responders. No entry in this corpus establishes that this pipeline runs at web scale against an adversarial peer population; `ASTHANA-ICTIR-11`'s 1.0-3.6-megabyte figure is a closed-form analytical result over stated parameters, not a measurement from a running system, and no experiment in this corpus — analytical, simulated, or deployed — combines web-scale document count, web-scale network size, ranked retrieval, and an adversarial peer population in one result (`registry/open-problems/web-scale-search.md`). This selection is the best-supported partial answer in the corpus, not a demonstrated one; the two sections after this one state exactly what remains unproven and what would have to be true elsewhere in the architecture for the analytical figure to hold in practice.

Against document-partitioned query broadcast (`LI-IPTPS-03`): rejected. Broadcast's 6-megabyte-per-query figure is fixed and directly measured, with no compression sequence available to reduce it — the paper's own optimization chapter never touches this baseline (`registry/conflicts/search.md` section 1). PAC's figure is unproven, but it is the only candidate in this table with a stated, if unconfirmed, path under the 1-megabyte budget at a larger document count than broadcast's own 3-billion-document target. Choosing PAC over broadcast trades a directly measured, fixed, over-budget cost for an analytically projected, under-budget cost carrying three named, currently unsupplied dependencies, stated below rather than hidden.

Against term-partitioned DHT posting-list intersection (`LI-IPTPS-03`): rejected. Even the paper's own full, unimplemented-at-scale optimization stack leaves the average query about 7x over budget, and the paper states the remaining gap closes only by dropping term-proximity ranking or by an unmeasured 10x per-Internet-service-provider replication of the whole index. PAC's figure, unproven as it is, sits inside the budget without either compromise, at a larger document count.

Against semantic overlay networks (`CRESPO-AP2PC-04`, `TANG-CCR-03`, `NEAGUE-ARXIV-25`): rejected on scale evidence, not mechanism quality. Every measured result sits 3 to 7 orders of magnitude below the web-scale target that `ASTHANA-ICTIR-11` and `COX-ICTIR-09` at least analytically project to; no semantic-overlay paper in this corpus offers a comparable web-scale projection. Semantic overlays also demand a stronger cross-network precondition than PAC — universal agreement on one classification hierarchy or embedding model, versus PAC's single shared random-sampling service — and `NEAGUE-ARXIV-25`'s own 12.75% two-hop retrieval rate, after 10 gossip rounds, shows the mechanism unresolved even at its own tested scale.

Against the high-capacity volunteer index with client verification (`WEI-NSDI-24`): rejected as the primary keyword-search mechanism, though it remains the correct choice for exact-identifier lookup, a different sub-problem. `WEI-NSDI-24`'s own security argument for a compromised Indexer or Hydra Booster rests specifically on the content identifier being self-validating, a property a cryptographic hash has and a relevance rank does not. `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14` demonstrates concretely what a malicious high-capacity indexer can do that hash verification never catches: move a target's rank from 5 to 582 at 10% malicious control, with no defense active. A volunteer-index architecture for ranked search inherits this exposure at whatever scale it runs, with no client-side check available for it in this corpus. PAC's own skewness defense (`RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14`) targets this exact failure mode directly, even though its bound (40%) currently exceeds what its own sampling foundation (Brahms, 20%) can deliver.

Against trusted-cluster learned vector retrieval (`ADAMS-ARXIV-25`, `MALKOV-TPAMI-20`): rejected outright for this architecture, not on cost grounds. `ADAMS-ARXIV-25` states its own precondition directly — its measured 90.8%/71.9% recall requires operator-placed scoring code on every storage host, a precondition a network of mutually distrusting, individually operated participants cannot supply. `MALKOV-TPAMI-20` independently states its fixed top-layer entry point does not scale in aggregate throughput once distributed across machines outside one trust domain. Both papers describe a single-operator managed cluster, not a decentralized network; a dedicated search across the corpus and beyond it found no published construction closing this gap as of 2026 (`registry/open-problems/adversarial-ann.md`).

Against single-untrusted-server private vector retrieval (`HENZINGER-SOSP-23`, `ZHOU-EPRINT-24`): not rejected as a mechanism, reserved instead for the tier of this component a person reaches by selecting query privacy over lower latency. Both systems assume one server holding a static snapshot of the whole corpus, not a network of independently publishing content contributors, and `ZHOU-EPRINT-24` states directly that database updates after preprocessing are unsupported — an assumption the default-tier mechanism, where new content is continuously published, cannot make. This selection therefore places two mechanisms across two tiers of one component: local-index-first with PAC network fallback for the tier a person has not opted into strong privacy, and Tiptoe- or Pacmann-style single-server private search for the tier they have, each requiring the composition check elsewhere to state separately what corpus-snapshotting or per-tier server deployment it needs.

Against a purely local index with no network fallback: rejected as incomplete, not wrong. A local-only index answers zero queries for content the node has never seen, and no figure in this corpus states what fraction of a person's real queries a local index alone would answer; `TRAUTWEIN-SIGCOMM-22`'s local-then-DHT pattern for hash-addressed retrieval is the nearest measured analog, and it adds a fixed 1-second timeout on every local miss before the network path even starts — a cost this design inherits and must budget for, not one that disappears by adding a local tier.

### What this selection requires from the rest of the system

- A secure, Byzantine-resilient random-peer-sampling service reachable by every node, whose actual tolerated-malicious-peer fraction must be measured for this deployment and published, because `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14`'s own skewness defense is bounded above by whatever this service tolerates — the cited figures (Brahms at 20%, the skewness defense at 40%) mean the lower of the two binds, and no component selected elsewhere in this corpus's selection set is confirmed to supply a uniform, unbiased random sample as opposed to a trust-weighted or proximity-weighted one; a peer-discovery layer that biases connectivity toward already-trusted or already-nearby peers would violate the uniformity `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14`'s coverage argument depends on, and this is a candidate composition conflict for whichever agent runs the cross-component check.
- A Sybil-resistance or admission mechanism capping how many rho-sized virtual nodes one physical high-capacity participant can register, because `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14`'s accuracy analysis assumes uniform per-node document share and states virtual-node splitting "must be defended against as a Sybil attack using a mechanism outside the scope of this paper."
- A content-signing or provenance check from the identity or naming component, since `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14` and the PAC framework generally assume malicious peers cannot forge document content that scores well under an honest local ranker; this indexing mechanism supplies no such check itself.
- A per-node local storage commitment, fixed and roughly uniform across nodes, on the order of `COX-ICTIR-09`'s and `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14`'s rho parameter, that the storage-encoding component must guarantee is actually held rather than merely claimed, since accuracy figures assume this uniformity.
- A client-local record, from the application-data-model component, of every piece of content the node has fetched, authored, or displayed, checked before any query leaves the device.
- For the strong-privacy tier: a per-corpus embedding function computed once and periodically republished to every client (`HENZINGER-SOSP-23`), or a full encoded-graph download repeated on every corpus change (`ZHOU-EPRINT-24`, 59.6 gigabytes at 100 million vectors) — a cost the storage or distribution component must carry, and `ZHOU-EPRINT-24`'s own stated limitation (dynamic updates unsupported) means this tier cannot serve continuously published content without a periodic full rebuild whose interval is not measured anywhere in this corpus.
- Per-query observability: the querying node itself must observe, for each of the z sampled peers, whether it responded and what local statistics it returned, and compute the skewness statistic `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14` defines over those responses before trusting the combined ranking — this is a client-side computation the network layer does not perform on the querying node's behalf.

### What it costs and where it fails

Local-index answers cost nothing beyond local storage lookup, but no figure in the corpus states their hit rate, so the fraction of queries this tier actually resolves without a network round trip is unknown. On a local miss, the network fallback costs 1.0-3.6 megabytes per query at 90% top-1 accuracy under `ASTHANA-ICTIR-11`'s own stated conditions: 1,000,000 nodes, a 10-billion-document collection, 10 million documents held per node. That per-node storage figure is large — `COX-ICTIR-09`'s own worked example at 200 times less storage per node (50,000 documents, the commodity 1-gigabyte case) needs 340,000 of 1,000,000 nodes queried for only 63% accuracy at a comparable 17-billion-document collection. The actual bandwidth this architecture pays therefore depends directly on how much local storage participating nodes are asked to commit, and this document does not derive that figure; it is NOT DERIVED, because it depends on a target document count and a target accuracy this component's own definition does not fix. The mechanism fails outright once the malicious-peer fraction reaching the sampling layer exceeds the lower of the two bounds the corpus reports — 20%, Brahms's own published sampling-tolerance limit — even though the ranking-side skewness defense would otherwise hold to 40%, because sampling bias, not ranking manipulation, becomes the binding failure once the true adversarial fraction crosses 20% (`RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14`). It fails silently under content forgery, since content-signing is assumed by this mechanism, not supplied by it.

### What the corpus does not settle

- No paper combines web-scale document count (billions), web-scale network size (millions of nodes), ranked keyword retrieval, and an adversarial peer population in one measurement, stated directly in `registry/open-problems/web-scale-search.md` after a dedicated search of the corpus and beyond it. This is the single largest gap under this selection.
- No paper measures what fraction of a real person's search queries a client-local index of previously seen content would answer without any network query — the local-index-first half of this selection has no cost or hit-rate figure anywhere in the corpus; `TRAUTWEIN-SIGCOMM-22`'s 1-second Bitswap timeout is the nearest measured analog, and it is for hash-addressed retrieval, not keyword search.
- No paper measures how a PAC-style local ranker (BM25 or a language-model score, computed locally per queried peer) responds to the same crafted-passage attacks that reach 98-99% success against learned dense retrievers (`LI-SIGIR-25`, `ZHONG-EMNLP-23`). The attack is demonstrated against a different ranking mechanism than the one this selection uses; whether it transfers is unmeasured.
- The actual adversarial-peer fraction any specific deployment of this architecture will face is not knowable from the corpus. `RICHARDSON-SIGIR-14` and `ASTHANA-ICTIR-11` both treat it as a swept experimental parameter, not a deployment-specific measurement; the failure threshold stated above (20%, Brahms-bound) can only be checked against a real number once whatever Sybil-resistance mechanism this architecture selects elsewhere states what fraction it actually holds an adversary to.
- No confirmed published follow-up to `LI-IPTPS-03`'s exact bandwidth bound was found; `registry/conflicts/search.md` and `registry/open-problems/web-scale-search.md` both state this directly after a dedicated search. A synthesis that treats `ASTHANA-ICTIR-11` as that follow-up is doing so by topical proximity, not by confirmed authorship or citation continuity.
- The local-storage figure a real deployment should ask each node to commit (`COX-ICTIR-09`'s rho) is a direct, three-orders-of-magnitude-wide trade against `ASTHANA-ICTIR-11`'s low-node-count figure, and the corpus does not resolve it; deriving it needs a target document count and a target accuracy this component's task definition does not fix, so it is left NOT DERIVED here rather than filled with a plausible-sounding number.
- No paper in the corpus measures the interaction between this selection's two tiers — whether a person who switches from the default tier to the strong-privacy tier mid-session, or whose local index holds content indexed under one tier but not the other, experiences any correctness or leakage effect from the switch itself.
