## Ranking: Independently operated feed generators a client subscribes to compute every ranking, but the only deployed measurement of default-switching shows fewer than 3 in 100 users ever pick an alternative — so whatever mechanism computes the default, not the marketplace around it, is what nearly everyone experiences, and no candidate in this corpus has been deployed as that default while also being measured for content-ranking quality

Ranking orders a set of candidate items — posts, videos, search results — for one viewer, without any party holding a global index of the whole collection. The question this component answers is not only which computation produces the order, but who is allowed to choose that computation. Four candidates answer that second question differently: a marketplace of independently operated ranking services a client opts into (`KLEPPMANN-CONEXT-24`, `BALDUF-IMC-24`, `QUELLE-PLOSONE-25`), a value each viewer's own device computes from its private position in a linkage graph with no company or committee involved (`NASRULIN-BRAINS-22`, `ZIEGLER-ISF-05`), a single designated aggregation point that produces one ranking every viewer downstream of it sees identically (`KAMVAR-WWW-03`, `GYONGYI-VLDB-04`, `POLINSKI-CCR-24`), and a ranking that emerges from raw behavioral signals peers exchange directly with each other by gossip, with no marketplace, no committee, and no company (`GOLD-ARXIV-23`, `HEGEDUS-ECMLPKDD-19`, `GREGORIADIS-ARXIV-25`). Only the first candidate has been deployed to real users at a scale anyone has measured, and that measurement (`QUELLE-PLOSONE-25`: 139,033 of 5,000,000 Bluesky users, 2.8%, ever liked even one alternative feed) is the only entry in this corpus that speaks to the decisive question stated in this component's own task definition — whether a person changes a default. For every other candidate, whether a person would change away from a default computed that way is not measured anywhere in this corpus; stating so is a guess, not a citation-backed claim, and is labeled as one below.

### Selection

Client-selected feed generators are selected as the governing pattern deciding who may compute a ranking, combined with gossip-based collaborative filtering specifically selected — not merely retained — as the mechanism computing whichever feed a client is shown by default, because the only measurement in this corpus that bears on the decisive question named in this component's task (whether a person changes a default) shows that almost nobody does: 2.8% of 5,000,000 measured users ever liked an alternative feed at all (`QUELLE-PLOSONE-25`), and among a smaller lab sample of people actively given a feed-composition tool, 9 of 10 still weighted the unranked default source highest (`LIU-PACMHCI-25`). Because the marketplace's value is realized by a small, engaged minority while the default determines what nearly everyone else experiences, whatever computes the default carries the architecture's stated goal — that ranking cannot be captured by any company — directly, and no company-operated or single-authority mechanism can be selected to fill that role without contradicting the goal for the majority of users this measurement describes. This pairing itself — a gossip-computed default inside a feed-generator marketplace — is not measured anywhere in this corpus; no entry deploys gossip-based collaborative filtering as a majority-facing default and measures the resulting retention behavior. That composition is a guess, stated as one, not a selection with a citable basis of its own; the basis cited above supports each half of the pairing separately, not the pairing itself.

Against the client-side source-merge pattern with no separate generator network (`LIU-PACMHCI-25`): not rejected as a mechanism, reserved instead as the simplest instance of the selected governing pattern — a client merging existing, unranked server feeds is a valid, minimal feed generator, and its own measured preference data (8 of 11 favoring the unranked chronological order) is the strongest reason in this corpus to make the default itself low-opacity rather than an elaborate learned ranking. It is not selected as the sole answer because its own sample (n=10-11, one platform) is too small to state what a production-scale default-switching rate would be, unlike `QUELLE-PLOSONE-25`'s 5-million-user figure for the marketplace pattern generally.

Against per-viewer graph metrics, both the propagation family (`NASRULIN-BRAINS-22`) and spreading activation (`ZIEGLER-ISF-05`): rejected as the mechanism for the default, though retained as a compatible option any individual generator inside the marketplace could choose to implement. Both are measured only for reputation or trust assessment, never for ranking content by relevance, and neither paper's own text or numbers speak to whether a person would keep or leave a ranking computed this way — selecting either as the default would be exactly the unlabeled guess this synthesis's own rule against unsupported selections prohibits. `NASRULIN-BRAINS-22`'s own largest tested graph (2,057 nodes) is three to four orders of magnitude below `QUELLE-PLOSONE-25`'s 5-million-user deployed scale, so even setting the content-ranking gap aside, no measurement in this corpus establishes the mechanism functions at the scale the marketplace pattern is already measured to reach.

Against consensus-favouring aggregation over groups, in each of its three measured instances: rejected, on the same structural ground reputation.md's own selection already rejects the network-wide-eigenvector instance of this family for the reputation component — `registry/selections/reputation.md` reaches the identical conclusion independently, and the reasoning transfers directly because the mechanism, not the task, produces the exposure. EigenTrust requires a pre-trusted peer set "designated" out-of-band with "no verification procedure" (`KAMVAR-WWW-03`); TrustRank requires a human oracle to hand-label a seed set, sourced in the paper's own deployment from one evaluator manually checking 1,250 candidates (`GYONGYI-VLDB-04`); the PeerTube pattern requires every instance to defer to one origin instance's authoritative count for any item (`POLINSKI-CCR-24`). Every one of these three measured instances places one designated party — a committee, a human labeler, or a single server — in a position to determine or veto the ranking every downstream viewer sees identically, which is the exact capture point the architecture's stated goal rules out. This is not a cost trade-off weighed against the family's benefits; it is a disqualifying structural property present in all three measured instances of the family in this corpus.

Against gossip-based collaborative filtering as the sole governing pattern, replacing the marketplace entirely: rejected, because no entry in this corpus deploys it to real, identifiable end users and measures whether they would keep using it, or would leave it for something else — the same decisive-question gap that applies to per-viewer graph metrics and, unlike the client-selected-generator marketplace, this family has never been measured outside a simulation (`GOLD-ARXIV-23`: 100 simulated peers; `HEGEDUS-ECMLPKDD-19`: PeerSim simulation; `GREGORIADIS-ARXIV-25`: 1,000 simulated users). It is selected for the narrower role of computing the default specifically because, among the three candidates with no deployed-adoption data, it is the only one with any measured content-ranking-quality figure at all (`GOLD-ARXIV-23`'s rank-distance-to-optimal convergence; `GREGORIADIS-ARXIV-25`'s MRR 0.38 to 0.67), and the only one with measured behavior under an adversarial population reaching the item-ranking task directly rather than a reputation or trust-scoring proxy task.

### What this selection requires from the rest of the system

- A Relay- or Firehose-equivalent component delivering every signed content update to any party wishing to operate a feed generator, at a bandwidth this corpus measures at approximately 30 gigabytes/day per full-network-observing subscriber (`BALDUF-IMC-24`); a generator that cannot sustain this either serves a partial view or depends on someone else's full-network component.
- Stable, network-wide content and account identifiers a generator can reference across the whole population, from the naming/identity component (`KLEPPMANN-CONEXT-24`).
- A hosting-diversity mechanism this component's own selection does not supply: measured hosting concentration (85.86% of generators through one platform, `BALDUF-IMC-24`) shows the marketplace pattern alone does not prevent re-concentration at the infrastructure layer beneath it — this is a composition gap for whichever component governs service hosting or admission, not resolved here.
- For the gossip-computed default: a peer-sampling or membership service supplying live peer addresses, stated by two separate entries as assumed rather than supplied by the ranking mechanism itself (`HEGEDUS-ECMLPKDD-19`, `GOLD-ARXIV-23`'s bootstrap-peer dependency).
- A Sybil-resistance or admission mechanism bounding how many adversarial identities can enter one honest peer's gossip neighborhood; `GOLD-ARXIV-23`'s own measured degradation scales directly with adversarial peer count (10 of 100 versus 75 of 100), with no defense in the source preventing eventual convergence to a shared degraded ranking once a supermajority is reached.
- A per-node local click-log or interaction-history retention commitment, measured at approximately 600 bytes per row and gossip messages rarely exceeding 600 kilobytes (`GOLD-ARXIV-23`) — a cost the local-storage component must carry for every participating node, not only generator operators.
- Content-signing or provenance from the identity/naming component, since `GOLD-ARXIV-23`'s own defense against fabricated evidence — preferring metadata matches over click-history matches — is stated by its authors only to blunt, not stop, an attack manufacturing spurious click-log entries.
- For the Shapley-value poisoning defense specifically: every participating node must already hold a minimum local click history (3 clicklogs in the tested configuration) before it can value incoming peer data at all (`GREGORIADIS-ARXIV-25`); a newly joined node has no such history and is exposed to unfiltered poisoning until it accumulates one, a cold-start gap this candidate does not itself close.

### What it costs and where it fails

The marketplace layer costs approximately 30 gigabytes/day of bandwidth per full-network-observing generator operator (`BALDUF-IMC-24`) and produces measured exposure inequality independent of the specific generator: in 33% of naturally occurring byte-identical duplicate-content groups, one copy is returned while its pair is not; a first-time author on a given feed carries a robust -0.061 reciprocal-rank penalty across all 250 individually tested feeds, independent of follower count (`WANG-ARXIV-26`). Despite architectural pluralism, generator hosting itself re-concentrates: the top 3 hosting platforms serve 95.8% of all generators, one platform alone serving 85.86% (`BALDUF-IMC-24`). The default's own mechanism, once selected as gossip-based collaborative filtering, fails specifically once an adversarial supermajority is reached: at 75 of 100 simulated peers adversarial, the source states the ranking "converges towards a single set of rankings that it appears unable to escape from" (`GOLD-ARXIV-23`) — the sharpest documented risk for this exact selection, because the decisive-question evidence above shows a default, once poisoned this way, would not be escaped by switching away either. The Data-Shapley variant of this same family holds Mean Reciprocal Rank flat against up to 50% poisoned peer data and only falls below its own local-only floor (0.38) at 90% poisoned share (`GREGORIADIS-ARXIV-25`), a materially higher measured tolerance than G-Rank's own tested conditions, though the two papers evaluate different tasks (unsupervised click ranking versus a learned ranking model with an explicit data-valuation filter) and neither is confirmed in this corpus to reach the other's tolerance under the other's attack model.

### What the corpus does not settle

- Whether a gossip-computed default would retain users at anything near the 97.2% no-alternative-adoption rate `QUELLE-PLOSONE-25` measures for Bluesky's actual, company-operated default is not measured anywhere in this corpus. Stating that it would is a guess; no entry evidences it, and this is the single largest gap under this selection.
- No entry measures gossip-based collaborative-filtering ranking at production content scale. The largest tested populations in this family are 1,000 simulated users (`GREGORIADIS-ARXIV-25`), 69,878 simulated users (`HEGEDUS-ECMLPKDD-19`), and 100 simulated peers over a 256-item catalog (`GOLD-ARXIV-23`) — three to five orders of magnitude below `QUELLE-PLOSONE-25`'s 5-million-user deployed measurement of the marketplace pattern itself.
- `QUELLE-PLOSONE-25`'s 2.8% figure measures adoption (a like on an alternative feed), not a stated cause; it cannot distinguish satisfaction with the default, unawareness that alternatives exist, or a switching cost, from an actual preference for the default's own ranking. `LIU-PACMHCI-25`'s interview data is the only causal or attitudinal figure in this corpus bearing on the decisive question, and it is an n=11 sample on one platform, not a production behavioral measurement.
- No entry attributes `WANG-ARXIV-26`'s measured new-author exposure penalty to any specific ranking mechanism; the study observes the outcome across black-box, operator-chosen algorithms without access to their internal computation, so whether the penalty is a general property of using per-author history as a ranking input, specific to certain mechanisms among the four candidates above, or specific to none of them, is unmeasured.
- `GOLD-ARXIV-23` tests only two fixed adversarial populations (10 of 100, 75 of 100 peers), not a swept range with a measured collapse threshold comparable to the 20%/40% figures the indexing component's own selection cites for a different random-sampling mechanism (`registry/selections/indexing.md`); the adversarial fraction at which this candidate's ranking quality actually collapses, as opposed to merely degrading, is not derived anywhere in this corpus.
- No entry measures what happens when a person who has adopted an alternative feed generator, under the selected marketplace pattern, subsequently experiences a change to that generator's underlying computation — for instance, an operator switching from a locally computed ranking to a gossip-degraded one following the failure condition described above. Whether such a change would be visible to that person, or would itself change their measured retention behavior, is unaddressed by any source in this corpus.
