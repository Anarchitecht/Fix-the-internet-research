## Identity: A Decentralized Identifier holds two purpose-separated keys — a threshold Schnorr key spread across a participant's own devices for everyday signing, never reconstructed, and a secret-shared, hash-based post-quantum key exercised only to rotate that device set

A participant's cryptographic identity is the set of keys that authorize action under one persistent identifier, and the mechanism that lets a verifier keep resolving that same identifier correctly after the participant loses a device, adds a device, or must replace a compromised key. Every candidate below answers a different part of that problem: an identifier syntax and document format for stating which keys currently apply (`W3C-DIDCORE-22`), a way for an external party to attest additional claims about the identifier's holder (`W3C-VCDM-25`, `BORMANN-SSR-25`), or a way to hold or use a signing key across more than one device without any single device holding the whole key (`SHAMIR-CACM-79`, `PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91`, `KOMLO-SAC-20`, `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`). A long-lived identifier also has to choose a signature algorithm that stays unforgeable if a large-scale quantum computer becomes available during the identifier's own lifetime (`BERNSTEIN-EUROCRYPT-15`, `HULSING-CCS-19`, `DUCAS-TCHES-18`).

### Selection

The identifier layer is the W3C Decentralized Identifier (DID) document (`W3C-DIDCORE-22`), which registers two purpose-separated keys rather than one. An `authentication` key is a t-of-n threshold Schnorr key produced and used with FROST (`KOMLO-SAC-20`), spread across the participant's own devices, never reconstructed at signing time. A `capabilityInvocation` key — the key that authorizes replacing which devices hold shares of the authentication key — is a single SPHINCS+ keypair (`HULSING-CCS-19`) whose seed is itself split under Pedersen verifiable secret sharing (`PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91`) across a custodian set distinct from the everyday device set, reconstructed only at the moment of a deliberate rotation and immediately re-split afterward.

Against the bare single-unshared-key baseline: `W3C-DIDCORE-22` states plainly that no common recovery mechanism exists across DID methods, and the one production deployment measured in this corpus confirms the practical consequence — `did:plc`, at over 10 million registered users, ended up with signing keys "held custodially by the PDS operator for ordinary users" rather than solving device-loss recovery in the protocol (`KLEPPMANN-CONEXT-24`). Splitting the key across the participant's own devices removes the single point of failure the baseline has no answer for, without reintroducing the custodian the deployed system was pushed into.

Against the issuer-attested verifiable credential as the identity's own root: rejected as a base representation, not as a mechanism. A credential's `credentialSubject` binds claims to a DID that must already resolve to keys behind it — the credential itself carries no answer to device loss for the holder's own proof-of-possession key, and establishing that a DID corresponds to a real-world entity is explicitly outside what the credential mechanism supplies (`AMBATI-SACMAT-26`: "the protocol itself supplies no mechanism to establish that correspondence"). A credential is a claims layer that presupposes a working identity root, not a substitute for one.

Against BBS-based anonymous credentials as the identity's own root: rejected for the same layering reason, and concretely because none of the surveyed constructions is quantum-safe (`BORMANN-SSR-25`) and native device binding "does not work for immediate deployment" given current Secure Element hardware (`BORMANN-SSR-25`). Confining BBS to an optional presentation layer, chosen per interaction rather than fixed at the identity's root, keeps that cost — and that quantum-safety gap — out of the mechanism every other action depends on.

Against reconstruct-to-sign secret sharing (plain Shamir, or Pedersen's verifiable variant) as the day-to-day signing mechanism: rejected, because reconstruction assembles the complete secret at one point in order to sign, which is exactly the exposure a threshold signature scheme is built to avoid — FROST's own combiner "does not perform a DKG during signing operations" and no participant's share is ever assembled into the whole key (`KOMLO-SAC-20`). Pedersen's mechanism is selected instead for the one role that matches its own description: backing up a key deliberately reconstructed once, rarely, immediately followed by generating a fresh key and re-splitting — the periodic re-share technique `SHAMIR-CACM-79` itself describes — which is exactly the root-rotation role assigned to it above, not the everyday signing role.

Against robust BLS threshold signing (TGS) as the day-to-day mechanism: `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`'s robustness — continuing past up to t misbehaving parties without aborting — is precisely the property FROST gives up (`KOMLO-SAC-20`: "a single misbehaving or unresponsive participant causes the entire signing operation to abort"). FROST is selected anyway for this role on the one dimension both papers report, round count: one round after one-time preprocessing, against TGS's requirement of both a broadcast channel and secure point-to-point channels among all n parties for its distributed key generation (`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`), plus a combiner that must keep every party's public commitment persistently reachable. TGS's robustness matters most when signers are a set of mutually distrusting servers that cannot be relied on to respond — the deployment model `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03` states explicitly — whereas a personal device set under the identity holder's own control can supply FROST's required authenticated exclusion-and-rerun path directly. Choosing FROST also avoids importing a second, pairing-based hardness assumption alongside the hash-based assumption the selected post-quantum root key already carries. This specific trade-off is not settled by a head-to-head measurement in either paper; it is stated here as a reasoned choice, not a citation-backed comparison, and is flagged again below.

Against a raw, unshared post-quantum key (SPHINCS+ or Dilithium) as the sole root, with no secret-sharing layer: rejected because neither has a published threshold variant in this corpus, so a single unshared copy reproduces the exact single point of failure `W3C-DIDCORE-22` already states is unsolved. Layering Pedersen verifiable secret sharing underneath removes that single point for the rare rotation role without requiring a threshold signature scheme to exist for either post-quantum algorithm, because the split key is reconstructed, used once, and immediately re-split rather than held open across live, repeated signing operations.

Between SPHINCS+ and Dilithium specifically, for the root key's algorithm: SPHINCS+ is selected. Dilithium's own paper states its classical forgery-security reduction "does not transfer over to the quantum setting," and states its substitute quantum-era argument (SelfTargetMSIS) is itself non-tight, defended only empirically, not by proof (`DUCAS-TCHES-18`). SPHINCS+'s security instead rests only on stated hash-function properties with no comparable stated proof gap (`BERNSTEIN-EUROCRYPT-15`, `HULSING-CCS-19`). SPHINCS+'s roughly two-orders-of-magnitude larger signature (30,696 bytes against Dilithium's 2,701 bytes, `HULSING-CCS-19`, `DUCAS-TCHES-18`) is exactly why this selection confines it to the rare rotation role rather than the frequent authentication role — where the size difference would recur on every signature — and not a reason to prefer Dilithium at the root, since the root key's own proof gap, not its size, is what this selection weighs against it.

### What this selection requires from the rest of the system

- A DID method plus a verifiable data registry component (the naming and content-location components) available whenever any party resolves this identity's current DID document `W3C-DIDCORE-22`.
- A key transparency component (a separate synthesis component) observing every DID document update and producing non-equivocation evidence, since DID Core supplies no non-repudiation of updates on its own `W3C-DIDCORE-22`, and the one production deployment measured in this corpus left exactly this gap open, stating its own planned transparency log "does not exist yet" `KLEPPMANN-CONEXT-24`.
- An available, correctness-checked location for FROST's preprocessing-stage nonce commitments, reachable by the identity holder's own devices; this location need not be trusted for confidentiality, only to serve correct, unused values `KOMLO-SAC-20`.
- An authenticated channel among the holder's own device set so an unresponsive or misbehaving device can be attributed and excluded, since FROST is non-robust `KOMLO-SAC-20`, and reliable message delivery among that device set during a live signing operation, which FROST's authors state as an assumption the protocol does not itself tolerate violations of `KOMLO-SAC-20`. This is a property of the identity holder's own small device set, not of arbitrary network peers; the transport/reachability component supplies it only at that scope.
- Retry-and-exclusion logic external to FROST after an abort, since "FROST itself only detects and halts" `KOMLO-SAC-20`.
- A group setup (primes p, q and generators g, h with unknown discrete logarithm log_g(h)) agreed once, at enrollment, for the Pedersen split of the SPHINCS+ root seed, established by a trusted authority or a coin-flip protocol among the custodian set, plus a confidential per-custodian channel separate from the public commitment broadcast `PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91`.
- A live coordination event of at least k custodians, simultaneously reachable, to execute any root rotation; fewer than k custodians together learn nothing about the seed `PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91`, `SHAMIR-CACM-79`.
- Every verifier resolving this identity supports both signature algorithms (threshold Schnorr and SPHINCS+) and reads the DID document's `verificationMethod` type field to know which key authorizes which action `W3C-DIDCORE-22`.

### What it costs and where it fails

FROST signing is measured in this corpus only by round count, not wall-clock time: one round after a one-time, non-interactive preprocessing stage `KOMLO-SAC-20`. It fails — aborts, requiring a rerun with the offending device excluded — the instant any one of the t selected devices is unresponsive or returns an invalid share `KOMLO-SAC-20`.

SPHINCS+ root-key operations cost 29,015,002 cycles signing and 2,739,770 cycles verification on a 3.5 GHz Haswell core (≈8.3 ms and ≈0.78 ms respectively) at the Haraka-robust, n=192 parameter set, with a 30,696-byte signature `HULSING-CCS-19` — a cost this selection accepts because the key signs only rare rotation events, not because the size is small in absolute terms.

Pedersen verifiable secret sharing costs fewer than 2|q|k modular multiplications to distribute and (2k+1)|q|+(k-1) multiplications per custodian to verify `PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91`, negligible against either signature scheme's own cost. It exposes the raw SPHINCS+ seed at the single moment of reconstruction; a set of k colluding or compromised custodians present at exactly that moment defeats it `PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91`.

The compound failure condition for this selection is: the device set falling below FROST's threshold t at the same time the custodian set falls below Pedersen's threshold k. That double-threshold event is unrecoverable, the same "no common recovery mechanisms" residual `W3C-DIDCORE-22` states for DIDs generally, now bounded to a joint condition rather than a single lost key. No entry in this corpus measures the frequency of device replacement or custodian availability under a composition like this one; the one production system measured avoided the underlying problem by concentrating key custody at the server operator rather than solving it `KLEPPMANN-CONEXT-24`.

### What the corpus does not settle

1. No entry measures a published post-quantum (t, n)-threshold signature scheme with per-share confidentiality preserved through signing. FROST and Boldyreva's TGS both depend on discrete-log hardness, which Shor's algorithm breaks on a sufficiently large quantum computer; SPHINCS+ and Dilithium have no published threshold variant anywhere in this corpus. Combining device-loss survival and quantum-safety in a single, frequently used signing key is an open problem this selection routes around — by confining the quantum-safe key to the rare rotation role — rather than one it resolves. A solution would need a threshold signature construction built on hash-function or lattice hardness in which no subset of t or fewer participants, at any point including during signing, holds enough information to forge a signature or reconstruct the key.
2. No entry measures FROST's behavior when the device set's membership itself changes. A fresh distributed key generation round for the new device set is the implied mechanism, but no measurement of its cost, round count, or failure rate under real device churn exists in this corpus `KOMLO-SAC-20`.
3. No entry measures FROST signing wall-clock time on real hardware; `KOMLO-SAC-20` reports round counts and asymptotic security bounds only, stating explicitly that "the paper reports no runtime benchmark."
4. No entry evaluates the two-key decomposition proposed above (a frequent threshold key plus a rare, Pedersen-backed post-quantum root) as a composed system. Each measured figure cited above comes from a paper evaluating that one piece in isolation; no paper in this corpus evaluates the composition as a whole.
5. `AMBATI-SACMAT-26` states key recovery is "arguably the most critical unsolved problem" across the 47 papers it surveyed, and found zero of 12 real deployments achieving full self-sovereignty on any of five dimensions, including key control. This selection is a composition of individually measured pieces, not a deployment measurement in its own right, and no entry in this corpus measures a deployed multi-device-threshold DID at any population scale.
6. No entry recommends or measures values for (k, n) or (t, n). `SHAMIR-CACM-79` and `PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91` both leave the threshold and total-share count as designer-chosen with no recommended values; `KOMLO-SAC-20` leaves FROST's (t, n) similarly unassigned. Choosing them for a real population requires a measurement this corpus does not supply: how often a real participant loses or replaces a device, and how many custodians a real participant can keep simultaneously reachable.
7. No entry describes or measures a proactive-refresh (periodic re-randomization) technique compatible with FROST specifically. Boldyreva's TGS states compatibility with the general Herzberg-Jarecki-Krawczyk-Yung proactive-secret-sharing technique `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`; this compatibility claim is not established for FROST's Schnorr construction anywhere in this corpus, so whether the selected everyday key can be refreshed against a slowly rotating set of compromised devices, without a full re-share, is unmeasured.
8. No entry measures whether device pairing under this threshold construction is less error-prone in practice than the device pairing key-transparency systems report generally as "cumbersome and error-prone for users in practice" `MELARA-USENIXSEC-15`. The structural argument for this selection — no single device ever holds the complete private key `KOMLO-SAC-20` — is a property derivable from FROST's own design, not a usability measurement.
