## Key recovery: A standing (t,n) threshold signing key held by user-chosen guardians, invoked only to co-sign a fresh identity key and never to reconstruct the lost one, is selected over raw secret reconstruction, unstructured guardian authorization, memorized-secret derivation, and hardware-held keys — none of which the corpus shows solving loss unassisted

Key recovery restores a person's ability to act under their existing network identity after the private key that identity depends on becomes unusable — lost, on a destroyed device, or forgotten — without any party capable of resetting it unilaterally, since no single party is allowed to control identity in this architecture. Two structurally different targets answer that need. A mechanism can regenerate the literal private key value that was lost, so the recovering device ends up holding the same bytes it held before — this is what Shamir reconstruction and password-derived keys both do. Or a mechanism can authorize a replacement key under the same identity without ever bringing the original value back into existence — this is what a threshold signature over a "rotate to this new key" statement does, and what an ordinary guardian vote does. The two targets carry different exposure properties, developed below; a person recovering access needs only the second, since what matters to every other party in the network is that the identity's current key changed with proper authorization, not that any particular bytes returned.

### Selection

A standing (t,n) threshold BLS signing key (`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`'s TGS construction) is distributed once, at identity-creation time, among guardians the user chooses from their own existing contacts in the same network — not a dedicated service. Recovery consists of collecting t guardians' signature shares over one statement, "the current key for this identity is now PK′," and combining them into a single signature the rest of the network verifies against the one standing guardian-threshold public key. The original private key is never regenerated at any point in this flow.

Against unstructured guardian authorization, where each guardian instead signs the rotation statement with its own independent key: no evidence entry in this corpus supplies any formal security bound for that construction, since it is not a distinct cryptographic scheme but a policy layered over ordinary signature verification — whereas the threshold-signing selection carries a proven, cited bound (t < n/2, stated to be optimal for this class of scheme) [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`]. The unstructured form also requires every verifier in the network to track a guardian list that can itself change, and changing it is an authorization event the mechanism does not itself define who may perform; the threshold-signing selection instead presents one fixed public key to every verifier regardless of guardian-set changes behind it, since the underlying shares can be refreshed through the same proactive re-sharing technique both `SHAMIR-CACM-79` and `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03` state their constructions are compatible with. This preference for one stable externally-visible key is a structural consequence drawn from reading the two mechanisms' descriptions together, not a claim either paper makes about identity systems.

Against raw Shamir or Pedersen reconstruction of the original private key: `SHAMIR-CACM-79`'s own text states that whoever combines t shares has the secret "easily computable" at that moment — a single point where the full key exists again in the clear, safe only if the party performing combination is the legitimately recovering user's own device and nobody else. Threshold BLS signing avoids this exposure entirely: each guardian applies its share directly inside signature computation, and the combiner assembles only signature shares, values that reveal no more about the underlying key than any ordinary signature does [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`]. Pedersen VSS adds the ability for guardians to check their shares are consistent without contacting each other [`PEDERSEN-CRYPTO-91`], which the selection also needs during its own key-generation step, but Pedersen's reconstruction path still recombines to the raw secret through ordinary interpolation, inheriting the same combination-time exposure Shamir has.

Between the two signing-without-reconstruction instantiations, robust threshold BLS (`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`) is preferred over FROST (`KOMLO-SAC-20`) specifically for recovery, though FROST remains preferable for day-to-day signing where its single-round efficiency dominates and guardians are reliably online. Recovery is triggered by exactly the condition robustness defends against: some guardians unreachable or misbehaving. FROST's own paper states it "trades off robustness for more efficient signing operations" [`KOMLO-SAC-20`] and that a single bad or absent response aborts the entire operation, requiring an out-of-band retry that excludes that guardian — a cost better absorbed during routine signing, where a retry is cheap, than during a rare, high-stakes recovery event, where every guardian re-contacted is another chance for the recovery window itself to be observed or delayed. Threshold BLS instead detects and excludes a bad share within the same attempt, via the public per-share commitment check, and still produces a valid signature from the remaining t honest guardians [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`].

Against deterministic derivation from a memorized secret, in every hardness tier measured here: the unhardened form is defeated outright by realistic password entropy — an 8-character password costs under 1 USD in estimated attack hardware under a plain hash, at parameters PERCIVAL uses specifically to illustrate why stretching is needed [`PERCIVAL-BSDCAN-09`]. The memory-hard-hardened form raises that cost by orders of magnitude but remains an unlimited offline target with no rate limit, and the specific parameters a deployer would naturally choose following the Argon2 designers' own selection procedure sit inside the range an independent attack later showed halves the effective cost [`ALWEN-EUROSP-17`], disputing the designers' own prior claim that this attack class posed no practical threat [`BIRYUKOV-EUROSP-16`]. The network-hardened forms (Pythia, PPSS) close the rate-limiting gap with measured or proven bounds this corpus's guardian-signing papers do not supply — Pythia's 15 ms measured LAN latency [`EVERSPAUGH-USENIXSEC-15`] and PPSS's proven q/\|D\| guessing bound [`JARECKI-ASIACRYPT-14`] are stronger, better-quantified guarantees than anything cited for threshold BLS above — but both require t+1 (or one) always-online, independently operated servers reachable at the moment of recovery, structurally the same guardian-availability dependency the selection carries, with named service operators substituted for a person's own chosen contacts. Choosing guardians the user already has a relationship with over dedicated server operators is a value judgment made specifically to satisfy this architecture's stated requirement that no company be able to capture the network — a deployment without that requirement, or one that already depends on independently operated server infrastructure elsewhere, should weigh `JARECKI-ASIACRYPT-14` and `EVERSPAUGH-USENIXSEC-15`'s better-quantified bounds against this tradeoff instead. Finally, deterministic derivation in every variant shares one failure mode threshold sharing does not: if the one memorized secret is forgotten outright, no partial recovery exists, whereas losing contact with fewer than n − t guardians still permits recovery.

Against hardware-held keys: `AMBATI-SACMAT-26` states directly that hardware-backed storage "does not solve backup if the device is lost or destroyed," which is exactly the loss this component exists to survive — hardware-held keys are not a competing recovery mechanism at all, but a complementary protection for whichever key an operating device holds day to day. The selection's guardian shares protect the capacity to authorize a rotation, not the device's own operating key, so a Secure Element can and should still hold that operating key between recovery events [`BORMANN-SSR-25`].

### What this selection requires from the rest of the system

- The identity component supplies an onboarding flow, run while the user still controls their key, in which n guardians are chosen and a dealer-free Gennaro-Jarecki-Krawczyk-Rabin key generation runs among them over a channel providing both broadcast and secure point-to-point delivery [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`].
- The storage or content-location component hosts every guardian's per-share public commitment (B_i = g^xi) persistently and discoverably by identity, surviving the loss event itself, since these values cannot live only on the device that is later lost [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`].
- The key-transparency component accepts a valid t-of-n guardian-threshold signature over a "rotate to PK′" statement as authoritative proof of continuity for the identity, publishes the rotation, and treats it as equivalent to a signature from the identity's own prior key — a verification rule this component states the requirement for but does not itself implement or log.
- Guardian availability: at least t of the n guardians (or their refreshed shares, if proactive re-sharing per `SHAMIR-CACM-79` and `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`'s cited compatibility has run) reachable and willing to compute a signature share when recovery is needed — an unquantified dependency; see below.
- n and t are chosen jointly at setup with n > 2t, the bound `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03` states is optimal for a robust, non-interactive threshold scheme of this kind, trading the size of a corrupting-or-blocking minority against the size of a cooperating majority.
- A discrete-log or Gap-Diffie-Hellman hardness assumption in the chosen pairing group, and a random-oracle-modeled hash function, since `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`'s proof is stated to hold only under that model.
- No defense once t guardians actively cooperate against the user — an assumption inherent to every (t,n) threshold construction cited here, not specific to this instantiation.
- If proactive re-sharing is used to bound exposure from a guardian compromised long before recovery is ever needed, periodic synchronized share-refresh rounds among all n guardians must be scheduled and enforced by a component this construction describes but does not itself supply [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`, citing Herzberg-Jarecki-Krawczyk-Yung].

### What it costs and where it fails

No entry in this corpus measures wall-clock latency, bandwidth, or throughput for threshold BLS signing or key generation; both `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03` and `KOMLO-SAC-20` state they report proofs and round counts, not benchmarks. What is stated: signing itself needs no round beyond each guardian broadcasting one share, matching FROST's single-round efficiency while additionally detecting and excluding a bad share through the public commitment check [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`]. Robustness holds only while corrupted or unavailable guardians number fewer than n/2; at or beyond that fraction, correctness and secrecy fail together, a bound the paper states is the optimal achievable one for this class of scheme, so no construction cited anywhere in this corpus does better on this specific axis [`BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`]. The security proof holds only in the random-oracle model and rests on a computational hardness assumption, not an information-theoretic one — a strictly weaker guarantee than raw Shamir sharing's unconditional secrecy against fewer than t shares [`SHAMIR-CACM-79`], the price paid here for avoiding Shamir's combination-time exposure.

### What the corpus does not settle

1. No entry anywhere in this corpus measures real guardian availability or response time: how many of a person's chosen contacts remain reachable and willing to act within a bounded window (hours, days, weeks) after that person loses a device. `AMBATI-SACMAT-26` states this is the field's largest open problem in these words but supplies no figure. A builder needs an empirical study of this before n and t can be chosen with any evidence behind the choice, rather than as an unlabeled guess.
2. No wall-clock latency, bandwidth, or throughput benchmark exists for `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`'s TGS or `KOMLO-SAC-20`'s FROST specifically. A builder needs an actual implementation measurement, in the shape `EVERSPAUGH-USENIXSEC-15` supplies for Pythia, to size real recovery latency against a target user-tolerance window.
3. The Gennaro-Jarecki-Krawczyk-Rabin distributed key generation `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03`'s key generation reuses is cited, not independently retrieved and measured in this evidence file; its own round count and communication cost, needed to size the guardian-onboarding step, is not available from any entry here.
4. No entry evaluates how a relying party first learns and comes to trust the identity of a user's chosen guardians before any loss occurs — `AMBATI-SACMAT-26`'s identity-binding challenge covers the general problem but not this specific composition of it.
5. Neither `SHAMIR-CACM-79` nor `BOLDYREVA-PKC-03` states how long a proactive re-sharing period can run before the security margin it is meant to preserve is itself at risk; both state the technique exists without measuring or proving a safe interval.
6. No entry in this corpus directly compares guardian-threshold recovery against password-protected secret sharing on any shared metric — cost, success rate, or measured attacker advantage. The comparison drawn in this document's Selection section is reasoned from each mechanism's separately stated properties, not a head-to-head experimental result; a builder choosing between them on cost alone, rather than on the centralization tradeoff argued here, has no corpus entry to settle that choice.
