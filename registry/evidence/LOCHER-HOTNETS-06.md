## [LOCHER-HOTNETS-06] Free Riding in BitTorrent is Cheap
**Citation:** Thomas Locher, Patrick Moor, Stefan Schmid, Roger Wattenhofer. "Free Riding in BitTorrent is Cheap." Proc. 5th ACM Workshop on Hot Topics in Networks (HotNets-V), 2006. Pages 85-90.
**Retrieved:** full text via http://conferences.sigcomm.org/hotnets/2006/locher06free.pdf
**Source URL:** http://conferences.sigcomm.org/hotnets/2006/locher06free.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
BitThief is a BitTorrent client that completes downloads of complete files while uploading no data at all, defeating BitTorrent's tit-for-tat admission mechanism through the single non-reciprocal admission path that mechanism leaves open: optimistic unchoke. BitTorrent divides participants into seeders, who hold the complete file and upload to all requesters in round robin, and leechers, who upload only to peers that reciprocate, except that every leecher periodically unchokes one neighbor for free (optimistic unchoke) so that newly joined peers with nothing to share can start downloading. BitThief exploits this path three ways. First, it opens far more simultaneous connections than the reference client (up to 500, against a reference-client default of 80), which raises the number of seeder round robin slots and leecher optimistic-unchoke slots it can occupy at once; download rate rises linearly with the number of open connections because remote peers act independently of how many connections the requester holds, except that a second connection from the same IP address is refused by the reference client and by Azureus. Second, it re-announces to the tracker far more often than the reference client's roughly 1800-second interval, starting at a configurable short interval and backing off exponentially to once every 30 minutes, to acquire more peer addresses faster. Third, in place of the reference client's rarest-first piece selection, BitThief requests a random missing piece from whichever peer unchokes it, so no unchoke period goes unused. BitThief never announces any pieces to remote peers and never chokes or unchokes any remote peer, so every peer it contacts treats it as a peer that has just started downloading.

### Measured results

|---|---|
| 7 torrents (170 MB-798 MB; A-G), unrestricted BitThief (permitted to download from seeders and leechers), reference client v4.20.2 permitted up to 500 connections for fair comparison, 3 runs per client per torrent, over the public Internet | BitThief completed every torrent. On torrents A, B, F, BitThief was on average slightly faster than the reference client (reference client uploaded 232 MB on torrent A, 129 MB on torrent B in one run). On torrents E and G (few seeders, many leechers), BitThief took roughly 4x longer than the reference client, which itself uploaded over 3.5 GB. |
| Same 7 torrents, BitThief restricted to download only from leechers (seeders excluded), 3 runs, same conditions | All downloads finished. Average download time increased relative to the seeders-permitted case. On torrent C, where only 15% of peers were leechers (so BitThief could use less than one sixth of available peers), BitThief took roughly 5x longer than the reference client, which was permitted to use both seeders and leechers. |
| Controlled private-network testbed: one private tracker, a configurable number of reference clients as seeders/leechers, one BitThief instance, scenario with a single slow seeder | BitThief started 9 minutes after three reference clients but caught up, and all four clients finished at roughly the same time. |
| Same torrent (359 MB on TorrentLeech, a sharing community requiring a minimum upload ratio; 350 MB on Mininova, an open public tracker), 3 BitThief runs per site | Mininova runs (531 seeders, 303 leechers reported): 32, 32, 37 minutes. TorrentLeech runs (24 seeders, 1 leecher reported): 7:25, 7:08, 7:08 minutes -- more than 4x faster despite roughly 33x fewer reported peers, because community peers upload more broadly to protect their upload ratio. |
| Block-contribution distribution across peers for the same torrent, BitThief connected to 309 peers on Mininova and 349 on TorrentLeech | On TorrentLeech (community), contribution across peers was more balanced. On Mininova (open), only a small number of peers supplied a large fraction of all blocks. |
| Sharing-ratio manipulation on TorrentLeech, by sending fabricated upload totals in tracker announcements (the tracker does not verify announced upload/download totals) | BitThief attained a reported sharing ratio of 1.4 on TorrentLeech without uploading any data. |
| Garbage-piece exploit from a prior paper (Liogkas et al., IPTPS 2006), reimplemented and tested against the reference client and Azureus | Answering all subpiece requests for a piece with random data caused both clients to ban the sending IP address once hash verification failed. Withholding exactly one subpiece per piece (so blame could not be pinned on the sender alone) still fooled the reference client, but Azureus tracked which peer supplied the most subpieces of a failing piece, reserved the remaining requests for that peer, and banned the IP once requests went unanswered. The authors conclude uploading garbage in any tested form does not improve download performance. |

### Parameters
- Tracker-reported peer list size: default 50 addresses per announcement; protocol maximum requestable is 200, but most trackers observed trimmed the response to 50 regardless of the requested count.
- BitThief simultaneous connection limit used in experiments: up to 500 (reference client's is 80 by default; raised to 500 for the fair-comparison experiments via --min_peers 500 --max_initiate 500 --max_allow_in 500).
- Tracker re-announce interval: reference client follows the tracker-supplied interval (observed near 1800 s); BitThief starts at a configurable shorter interval and backs off exponentially to a ceiling of 1800 s (once every 30 minutes).
- Piece-availability announcement: BitThief announces an empty piece list at connection setup and never updates it; announcing any percentage below 100% did not change measured performance, but announcing 100% caused remote peers to treat BitThief as a seeder and refuse to send it any pieces.

### Stated limitations
The paper's controlled-testbed experiment illustrating recovery from a slow seeder is described by the authors as an idealized case because no additional leechers joined the swarm during the run. BitThief as built does not implement collusion among multiple free-riding instances, does not use the distributed (DHT-based) tracker protocol, and does not perform a Sybil attack of presenting multiple fake identities to the same remote peer, though the authors state this would be straightforward to add given multiple IP addresses and would plausibly double the achievable download rate. The authors state that faking whole pieces by finding SHA-1 hash collisions to defeat per-piece integrity checking is computationally infeasible with then-current techniques and storage. The authors state that a mechanism combining tit-for-tat with an efficient bootstrap allowance for newly joined peers (who have nothing to share yet) remains an open problem, which they name as future work rather than solved in this paper.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
For an incentive mechanism to withstand this attack, it cannot rely solely on a reciprocity check triggered only by past upload behavior, because BitThief supplies zero upload and still receives service through the optimistic-unchoke and seeder round robin paths, which are unconditional by design. Any defense that closes this path must either meter or price the optimistic-unchoke slot itself, or supply new peers with reciprocity credit from some other source, since the bootstrap problem (new peers have nothing to reciprocate with) is the reason the unconditional path exists in the first place. A tracker-side or community-side ratio-enforcement layer requires the tracker to verify announced upload/download totals against independent evidence (for example, corroboration from receiving peers), because self-reported totals in the announce message are accepted without verification in the deployed protocol and can be fabricated by a client that never transfers the claimed bytes. A per-peer integrity/ban mechanism such as Azureus's blame-single-supplier logic requires the receiving client to track, per remote peer, which subpieces of a not-yet-hash-verified piece it supplied, so that a failed hash check can be attributed to a specific peer rather than diffused across a piece's several suppliers.

### Contradicts
The paper's own framing distinguishes its finding from Jun and Ahamad [9], who define free riding as contributing considerably less than other peers and propose a tit-for-tat-oriented fix; this paper's authors state they instead demonstrate downloads with strictly zero upload, a stronger claim than reduced contribution. No other paper in the current evidence file's W3-5 batch reports free-riding numbers under the same connection-count and torrent-size conditions to compare directly; SIRIVIANOS-IPTPS-07 in this batch reports an independent free-riding construction and its completion-rate figures should be compared against BitThief's at the level of "peer-set size used" rather than at the level of a shared torrent, since the two papers use different torrents and swarm compositions.

### References worth retrieving
- Adar, Huberman, "Free Riding on Gnutella," First Monday 5(10), 2000 -- foundational (earliest measurement of free riding in a P2P file-sharing network).
- Douceur, "The Sybil Attack," IPTPS 2002 -- foundational (defines the multi-identity attack the paper explicitly notes BitThief could add).
- Liogkas, Nelson, Kohler, Zhang, "Exploiting BitTorrent For Fun (But Not Profit)," IPTPS 2006 -- competing/foundational (the closest prior work; implements three individual selfish exploits including the garbage-piece exploit this paper reimplements and extends by combining attacks).
- Jun, Ahamad, "Incentives in BitTorrent Induce Free Riding," P2PECON 2005 -- competing (argues BitTorrent's tit-for-tat is insufficient using a weaker definition of free riding; proposes an iterated-prisoner's-dilemma-based fix).
- Andrade, Mowbray, Lima, Wagner, Ripeanu, "Influences on Cooperation in BitTorrent Communities," P2PECON 2005 -- foundational (measures sharing-ratio enforcement effects in communities; this paper's community exploit builds directly on that measurement).
- Grolimund, Meisser, Schmid, Wattenhofer, "Havelaar: A Robust and Efficient Reputation System for Active Peer-to-Peer Systems," NetEcon 2006 -- competing (proposed reputation-based incentive mechanism for P2P systems).
- Vishnumurthy, Chandrakumar, Sirer, "KARMA: A Secure Economic Framework for P2P Resource Sharing," P2PECON 2003 -- competing (currency-based incentive mechanism for P2P resource sharing).
- Tamilmani, Pai, Mohr, "SWIFT: A System with Incentives for Trading," P2PECON 2004 -- competing (alternative incentive mechanism for content trading).
