## [PIATEK-NSDI-07] Do Incentives Build Robustness in BitTorrent?
**Citation:** Michael Piatek, Tomas Isdal, Thomas E. Anderson, Arvind Krishnamurthy, Arun Venkataramani. "Do Incentives Build Robustness in BitTorrent?" USENIX NSDI, 2007.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/nsdi07/tech/piatek/piatek.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/nsdi07/tech/piatek/piatek.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
The paper measures how much of BitTorrent's observed cooperation comes from its tit-for-tat (TFT) reciprocity rule versus from unrewarded contribution by high-capacity peers, then builds BitTyrant, a client that raises download performance per unit of upload contribution without changing the wire protocol. BitTorrent divides a peer's upload capacity equally among the peers in its active set (the set of peers it is currently unchoking, sized by the reference client as floor(sqrt(0.6 * capacity)) minus the number of optimistic-unchoke slots); the paper terms this per-connection share the equal split rate. Standard TFT unchokes peers that reciprocated fastest in the recent past, plus a small number of randomly chosen optimistic unchokes that bootstrap new peers into the reciprocation game. The paper's model shows that a peer's chance of reciprocation from a candidate partner depends on that partner's active-set size, and that high-capacity peers therefore need hundreds of connections to fully reciprocate; because typical local-neighborhood sizes (50-100 peers in existing clients) are far smaller, most peers under-fill their upload capacity relative to what the model computes as their maximizing allocation, and the unused capacity is contributed without earning proportional download benefit — the paper's operational definition of altruism. BitTyrant exploits this gap with an alternative unchoke algorithm: for each candidate peer p, it tracks u_p (the upload rate needed to induce reciprocation from p, initialized from the measured equal-split-capacity distribution) and d_p (the observed or estimated download rate received from p), ranks connections by the ratio d_p/u_p, and greedily unchokes in ranked order until its own upload capacity is exhausted, so it maximizes total download rate per unit of upload spent rather than splitting capacity equally. After each round, u_p is decreased by a factor gamma if p reciprocated for r consecutive rounds (signaling u_p is set higher than necessary), and increased by a factor delta if p failed to reciprocate after being unchoked (signaling u_p is too low); for peers that have not yet sent data, BitTyrant estimates d_p from the rate at which the peer announces new blocks, then divides by the reference client's recommended active-set size for that implied capacity to approximate the peer's equal split rate. BitTyrant enlarges its own local neighborhood beyond the typical 50-100 peers by requesting more peers from both the centralized tracker and the DHT-based distributed tracker at the maximum allowed frequency, because the modeling shows high-capacity peers need a much larger candidate pool to find reciprocating partners. To avoid TCP throughput loss from splitting capacity across too many low-capacity connections, BitTyrant peers recognize one another via a Peer-ID hash advertised at connection time and switch between themselves to a block-based TFT variant that ramps send rates up to full capacity with fewer connections.

### Parameters
- Optimistic-unchoke slots omega: 2 simultaneous optimistic unchokes per peer (reference-client default, used in the model).
- Local neighborhood size lambda: 80 directly connected peers (reference-client default in the model); BitTyrant increases this beyond the reference client's default via more frequent tracker and DHT queries (no fixed target size stated as a single number).
- Active-set sizing function: active(r) = floor(sqrt(0.6r)) - omega, for upload-capacity rate r (reference BitTorrent client default formula).
- BitTyrant reciprocation-estimate update factors: gamma = 10% decrease in u_p if a peer reciprocates for r = 3 consecutive rounds; delta = 20% increase in u_p if a peer fails to reciprocate after being unchoked in the previous round.
- Optimistic-unchoke period T: 30 seconds (used in the convergence-time formula).
- Upload-capacity cap in the real-swarm evaluation: 128 KB/s for both BitTyrant and the comparison Azureus client.
- PlanetLab scaling factor: 1/10 applied to upload-capacity draws, file size, initial unchoke bandwidth, and block size, to fit available PlanetLab bandwidth while preserving the shape of the measured distribution.
- Selfish-cap experiment: high-capacity-peer upload capped at 100 KB/s, chosen as the empirically observed diminishing-returns point.
- File size in PlanetLab experiments: 5 MB, with 3 seeds totaling 128 KB/s combined capacity.

### Stated limitations
The authors state their analytic altruism model is not intended to be predictive: it rests on simplifying assumptions (a representative capacity distribution held constant across a swarm's lifetime, and uniform active-set sizing by all other peers) that the authors say are not realistic for every swarm, and its purpose is only to suggest where altruism originates and to guide BitTyrant's design. The paper states BitTyrant is not strategyproof, even when extended with the block-based TFT variant used between mutually recognizing BitTyrant peers, and explicitly leaves open for future work whether further strategic countermeasures against BitTyrant itself would be effective. Three additional cheating strategies (exploiting optimistic-unchoke weighting via identity churn, downloading-only-from-seeds exploits, and falsifying block availability) are described but explicitly not implemented in BitTyrant because the authors judge them fixable by simple client-side countermeasures; they are documented for completeness, not evaluated experimentally. The paper reports that when high-capacity peers behave strategically and selfishly (withholding capacity beyond the diminishing-returns point), aggregate swarm performance decreases and low-capacity peers are harmed the most, stated as a direct measured consequence of universal strategic adoption, not a hypothetical. The paper explicitly frames its own conclusion as an open question rather than a settled result: whether real-world adoption of BitTyrant-style selfishness will in practice degrade swarm performance, prompt a shift to centrally enforced incentives, or induce users to buy more bandwidth, is stated as unresolved and left for future observation following the client's public release.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
BitTyrant's unchoke-ranking mechanism requires an active set larger than the reference client's typical 50-100 peers to find enough high-value reciprocating partners; it achieves this by requesting more peers from the tracker and the DHT rather than needing any protocol change, so a system reusing this mechanism needs an underlying peer-discovery channel (centralized tracker, DHT, or both) capable of returning a larger and more frequently refreshed peer set on request. The reciprocation-rate estimator needs an initial estimate of a candidate peer's equal-split capacity before any direct data exchange with it, and the paper's method for that estimate (dividing an observed block-announcement rate by the reference client's recommended active-set size for the implied capacity) assumes the reference client's sqrt(0.6r) active-set-sizing formula remains the network's dominant convention — if the observed swarm's clients use a different active-set policy, this bootstrap estimate is stated by the authors to potentially misestimate capacity. The mechanism assumes the ability to observe, per connection, whether the remote peer reciprocated in each round (dp) and whether it reciprocated for the past r rounds, so it needs the underlying protocol to expose or allow inference of a peer-level reciprocation signal over time; a protocol that only reports a swarm-level or opaque aggregate result would not supply the per-connection signal this ranking algorithm consumes. The block-based TFT fallback between mutually recognizing BitTyrant clients requires each peer to reveal a stable, protocol-visible identifier (the Peer ID hash) at connection time, so any anonymity layer suppressing stable per-connection identifiers would remove the signal BitTyrant uses to switch into that faster-converging mode with other BitTyrant peers.

### Contradicts
The paper explicitly contradicts the "consensus" (its own characterization) that TFT alone makes BitTorrent robust to strategic behavior, attributed to Cohen's original 2003 IPTPS paper and repeated in subsequent work; it also contradicts the conclusion of Qiu and Srikant (SIGCOMM 2004) that BitTorrent's rate-based TFT converges to a Nash equilibrium in which all peers upload at full capacity — the paper states that result does not hold for typical file sizes under realistic bandwidth distributions and churn, which is precisely the gap BitTyrant exploits. No contradiction with another paper in this corpus was found.

- **Competing**: D. Qiu, R. Srikant. "Modeling and performance analysis of BitTorrent-like peer-to-peer networks." SIGCOMM, 2004. (Analytic Nash-equilibrium result for rate-based TFT that this paper's measured results contradict for realistic parameters.)
