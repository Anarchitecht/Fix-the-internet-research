## [COHEN-IPTPS-03] Incentives Build Robustness in BitTorrent
**Citation:** Bram Cohen. "Incentives Build Robustness in BitTorrent." Workshop on Economics of Peer-to-Peer Systems, presented at IPTPS 2003. May 22, 2003.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://bittorrent.org/bittorrentecon.pdf
**Source URL:** https://bittorrent.org/bittorrentecon.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
BitTorrent redistributes the upload cost of serving a file from a single host to the set of concurrent downloaders, by having every downloader upload pieces of the file it already holds to its peers while it continues downloading pieces it lacks. This removes the requirement that hosting a file with an unbounded number of downloaders needs unbounded upload capacity at one machine.

A file is published by placing a small static .torrent file (containing the file's length, name, per-piece SHA1 hashes, and the address of a tracker) on an ordinary web server, and starting at least one seed, a peer that already holds the complete file. The tracker is a lightweight coordination point reached over HTTP: a downloader reports what file it wants and what port it listens on, and the tracker replies with a list of other peers downloading the same file, drawn at random rather than through any peer-selection heuristic that would bias the resulting connection graph toward a structured or power-law shape. Peers connect directly to each other over these tracker-supplied addresses; the tracker never routes file data itself.

Each file is divided into fixed-size pieces (a quarter megabyte in the deployments described), and each piece is further divided into 16-kilobyte sub-pieces for pipelined transfer over TCP, with a fixed number of sub-piece requests (five) kept outstanding at any time to keep the TCP connection saturated. A peer reports to every connected peer which pieces it currently holds, and only reports a piece as held once its SHA1 hash has been checked. Piece download order follows four combined rules. Once a sub-piece of a given piece has been requested, all remaining sub-pieces of that same piece are requested next, ahead of sub-pieces from any other piece, so that complete pieces finish quickly. Subject to that priority, a peer downloads whichever piece is held by the fewest of its own peers ("rarest first"), which keeps rare pieces from disappearing when the peers holding them leave, and keeps every peer's held-piece set useful to upload to others. At the very start of a download, before a peer has anything to upload, pieces are instead selected at random rather than by rarity, because a rare piece is more likely to be held by only one peer and so download more slowly than a piece obtainable in parallel sub-piece chunks from many peers. Near the end of a download, once every remaining needed sub-piece is already requested from some peer, a peer additionally requests all remaining sub-pieces from every peer that has them ("endgame mode"), and cancels the redundant in-flight requests as each sub-piece arrives, to avoid a single slow peer stalling completion.

Upload allocation among peers is governed by a choking algorithm run independently by each peer. Choking is a temporary refusal to upload to a given connected peer; it does not close the connection, so the choked peer can resume receiving without renegotiation. A peer always unchokes a fixed number of other peers (four, by default), chosen by highest current download rate received from them, recomputed once every ten seconds, a period the author states is long enough for TCP to ramp a new transfer up to its available capacity. Current download rate is calculated as a rolling 20-second average, rather than a long-term cumulative total, because bandwidth availability is stated to shift quickly as other transfers start and stop. Independent of that four-way rule, one additional connection is always unchoked regardless of its current download rate, called the optimistic unchoke, and which connection holds that role rotates every third recompute period (30 seconds); this lets a peer discover whether an otherwise-unused connection would in fact yield a better download rate than the currently favored ones, in the same role that always cooperating on the first move plays in a repeated prisoner's-dilemma strategy. If a peer receives no data for over a minute from a peer it was formerly downloading from, it marks that peer as having "snubbed" it and stops uploading to it except via the optimistic-unchoke slot; because this can produce more than one concurrent optimistic unchoke, it speeds recovery when a peer's download rate has collapsed. Once a peer has finished downloading and has no download rate left to measure, it instead prioritizes uploading to peers to which it has the best upload rate, to keep utilizing upload capacity productively.

None of these figures come with node counts, topology, dataset composition, or run counts sufficient to reproduce them; the paper is a design and mechanism description supported by informal field observation of real deployments, not a controlled experiment.

### Stated limitations
The paper states that erasure codes had been suggested by other work as a technique for the piece-verification and distribution problem, but that BitTorrent's much simpler fixed-piece SHA1-hash approach "has proven to be workable" instead, without a quantitative comparison of the two approaches. It states that for deployments with a single seed whose upload capacity is well below that of the aggregate downloaders, and for deployments where the original seed is eventually taken offline, a piece can become completely unavailable if rarest-first replication does not spread it in time; the paper presents rarest-first as reducing but does not claim eliminating this risk. It does not report a formal robustness or fairness analysis of the choking algorithm; the claim that seeking pareto efficiency through tit-for-tat "tend[s] to have" properties of full resource utilization, consistent download rates, and resistance to non-contributing peers is presented as a consequence of a cited general economic argument, not as a property proven or measured for BitTorrent specifically. The paper does not model or measure adversarial peer behavior (for example peers that upload junk data, or coordinate to defeat the choking algorithm); it addresses only unmodified good-faith peer behavior under high churn.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
BitTorrent's choking mechanism requires a bidirectional TCP connection between any two peers exchanging pieces, since choking blocks only the upload direction while leaving the same connection able to resume downloading without renegotiation. It requires the tracker to supply peer lists chosen at random rather than through a structured peer-selection rule, since the paper attributes BitTorrent's churn robustness to the resulting random-graph topology and explicitly attributes the fragility of power-law peer graphs (produced by many other peer-selection algorithms) to their vulnerability to segmentation after modest churn. It requires a piece-integrity check (the per-piece SHA1 hash from the .torrent file) to exist before a peer is willing to advertise or serve that piece to others, since the reported piece set doubles as the input that both piece selection (rarest-first) and choking-partner selection operate on. It requires a mechanism outside the wire protocol (the choking algorithm is stated to not be part of the BitTorrent wire protocol itself) to select which unchoked peers to reciprocate with, meaning any peer-selection or reputation input other than recent observed download rate would have to be added at that separate layer, not the protocol itself. The rarest-first piece-selection policy requires each peer to know, from its own connected peers' advertised piece sets, which pieces are locally rare; this signal comes from the same piece-announcement messages the choking algorithm's traffic accounting does not use, so a peer that withheld its held-piece set from others to preserve privacy would prevent both the choking algorithm's income signal to it, since others would not know to reciprocate, and rarest-first's ability to protect that peer's pieces from disappearing.
