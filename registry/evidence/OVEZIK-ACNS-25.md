## [OVEZIK-ACNS-25] SoK: Measuring Blockchain Decentralization
**Citation:** Christina Ovezik, Dimitris Karakostas, Mary Milad, Aggelos Kiayias, Daniel W. Woods. "SoK: Measuring Blockchain Decentralization." Applied Cryptography and Network Security (ACNS), 2025. DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-95761-1_7.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18279
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18279
**Domain:** J

### What it does
The paper gives a decision procedure for measuring blockchain decentralization that separates the measurement into three sequential steps, so that anyone reproducing a decentralization claim can identify at which step a disagreement with another study arose. Step one selects a resource on a layer of the system (for example, blocks produced on the consensus layer, or tokens held on the tokenomics layer). Step two extracts, from raw ledger data, the distribution of that resource across real-world parties, which itself requires five sub-choices: whether and how to cluster addresses into real-world entities, what time window to aggregate the resource over before computing a distribution snapshot, what time window to use to decide which parties count as "active" for population purposes, how frequently to take new snapshots, and whether to discard low-holding parties below some inclusion threshold before computing a metric. Step three applies a decentralization metric to the resulting distribution.

Five decentralization metrics are defined formally over a distribution of shares s_1...s_n across n entities. Shannon entropy, H(X) = -sum p(x) log p(x), treats block-producer identity as a random variable and reports how unpredictable the identity of the next block's producer is; entropy of zero means one entity always produces the block. The Gini coefficient, G = (A-B)/A with A = n * sum(x_i)/2 and B = sum(i * x_i) over entities sorted ascending by share, is the normalized area between the cumulative-share (Lorenz) curve and the line of perfect equality; 0 is perfect equality, 1 is total concentration in one entity, and the paper notes the value depends on whether zero-holding parties are included in the population (worked example: Gini of shares {3,2,1} is 0.22, but of {3,2,1,0} is 0.42). The Nakamoto coefficient (NC) is the minimum number of entities whose combined share exceeds 50% of the total; the paper notes some prior work uses a >=51% threshold instead of >50%, which it attributes to the informal "51% attack" label rather than to the actual majority threshold, which is any share strictly greater than half. The tau-decentralization index generalizes NC to an arbitrary threshold tau instead of fixing it at 50%, useful for systems whose liveness requires two-thirds honest participation rather than a simple majority (Algorand, and pre- and post-merge Ethereum consensus at the beacon-chain layer are cited as needing tau=0.33 rather than 0.5). Concentration ratio CR_n is the summed share of the n largest entities. The Herfindahl-Hirschman index (HHI) is the sum of squared entity shares, ranging 0 to 10,000, for which the paper reports US antitrust agencies' own classification bands (below 1,500 unconcentrated, 1,500-2,500 moderately concentrated, above 2,500 highly concentrated) as a possible reference scale for blockchain use, without itself proposing blockchain-specific bands. A sixth, cruder approach simply counts the number of active parties holding any nonzero amount of the resource; the paper states this ignores how unevenly that resource is held among the counted parties.

To determine whether these six metrics are measuring the same underlying property or different ones, the paper performs exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the metrics computed from real historical data (see Measured results). EFA infers a small number of latent factors that best explain the observed correlations between the input variables (here, the metrics); if all metrics reduce to one common latent factor for a given blockchain, that is read as evidence they measure the same underlying construct, and if two or more factors emerge, the metrics loading on different factors are read as capturing distinct signals.

### Parameters

|---|---|---|
| Resource-estimation window (consensus layer) | 7 days | Prior work (ref. | Measurement/sampling frequency | Weekly for consensus-layer data, monthly for tokenomics-layer data, chosen to produce non-overlapping windows | Not varied as an independent experiment beyond this choice; justified by the statistical-inference use case requiring non-overlapping observations |
| Inclusion threshold | None (full distribution), for consensus; "greater than transaction fees" or none for tokenomics | Range tested: top-100, top-500, top-1000, top-10,000 wealthiest entities, entities holding enough to pay a transaction fee, top 50% by wealth, and no threshold; the top-X family (100-10,000) was found to disagree with the other choices |

### Stated limitations
The paper explicitly excludes the governance layer from its own empirical analysis, stating this is because of a lack of sufficient measurement studies on layer-1 blockchain governance to analyze. It states its systematization framework applies only to fungible resources distributed across participating entities, and explicitly states it is "not suitable for blockchain layers involving non-fungible resources, such as the network layer, where the graph structure must be incorporated into any analysis of decentralization." It states that token clustering (grouping addresses into real-world entities) is harder for the tokenomics layer than for the consensus layer, because token holders, unlike mining pools, do not voluntarily disclose which addresses they control, and it states this as unresolved future work rather than solved by any technique the paper applies. It states that determining whether an inactive address represents a permanently abandoned account or a long-term holder is an open, unaddressed problem for population estimation on the tokenomics layer. It states explicitly that improving decentralization measurement for layers other than consensus and tokenomics (network, governance) requires two unsolved prerequisites: handling inconsistent and missing off-chain data sources, and identifying a fungible resource, or a principled way to weight non-fungible resources, for those layers — and recommends researchers do primary research on those layers before attempting further systematization. It states a thorough evaluation of the optimal population-estimation window is left to future work, having tested only the "entire history" choice in its own analysis.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Applying any of the paper's decentralization metrics to a resource requires the resource first be represented as a distribution of shares summing to a fixed total across a finite, enumerable set of parties (s_1...s_n); the framework explicitly cannot be applied as-is to a resource like network topology position that is not fungible in this sense. Computing a metric that is sensitive to zero-holding entities (the paper demonstrates this specifically for Gini) requires the population-extraction step to include currently-inactive-but-formerly-active entities, since the paper shows the Gini value of {3,2,1} and {3,2,1,0} differ by roughly a factor of two; a downstream synthesis step citing a Gini figure must therefore also know, and state, which population definition produced it, since two Gini values computed on the same underlying blockchain under different population windows are not directly comparable (this paper's own Figure 4 result). Computing HHI or Gini meaningfully at all requires an entity-clustering step (grouping addresses controlled by one real-world party) to precede it, since the paper's own Bitcoin HHI result shows tagged and untagged clustering diverge starting in 2016; a synthesis step citing tokenomics-layer concentration figures from any paper must check whether that paper applied clustering, since this paper found no-clustering is the choice found in 14 of 20 surveyed prior studies (the paper's own discouraged choice). The recommended >=7-day resource-estimation window for the Nakamoto coefficient and related consensus metrics requires whatever downstream mechanism consumes those metrics (for example, a Sybil-resistance or reputation scheme keyed to a measured concentration level) to tolerate a measurement lag of at least a week, since a shorter window is shown by the cited prior work to fail its own statistical confidence test in a majority of cases for a low-throughput ledger like Bitcoin.

### Contradicts
None found within this corpus for any specific numeric figure. The paper's finding that "higher levels of participation are not always associated with greater decentralization" (the number-of-parties metric loading on a separate factor from the other six metrics, for Bitcoin, Ethereum PoW, and Litecoin) is presented by the authors as counter to "popular belief," which the paper frames as an unattributed general assumption rather than a specific citable prior claim; no entry in this batch makes the contrary claim explicitly, so this is not recorded as a contradiction with another paper in this corpus.
