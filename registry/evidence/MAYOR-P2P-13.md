## [MAYOR-P2P-13] On Unstructured Distributed Search over BitTorrent
**Citation:** William Mayor, Ingemar Cox. "On Unstructured Distributed Search over BitTorrent." IEEE P2P, 2013. DOI 10.1109/P2P.2013.6688715.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1420897/1/Mayor_Billy.pdf
**Source URL:** https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1420897/1/Mayor_Billy.pdf
**Domain:** B

### What it does
The mechanism lets a BitTorrent node discover a torrent's participants through unstructured probabilistically-approximately-correct (PAC) search over tracking data, instead of through a centralized tracker or a structured distributed hash table (DHT), so torrent discovery survives the failure or attack of both of those. PAC search finds a target document (here, a torrent's tracking data, meaning its infohash together with the list of nodes believed to hold it) by having the querying node contact z nodes sampled uniformly at random from the network and asking each whether it locally indexes the document; the probability of success is P(d_i) = 1 - (1 - r_i/n)^z, where n is the network size and r_i is the number of nodes that index the document. Because most BitTorrent torrents are held by too few nodes for this formula to give a workable success probability directly on torrent participation, the paper adds a protocol modification: every node, on receiving any query for a torrent, records the querying node in a local index entry for that torrent's infohash, regardless of whether the query succeeds. A search is now successful if it finds any node indexing the torrent's tracking data, not only a node directly participating in the torrent, so every query issued for a torrent increases that torrent's tracking-data replication r_i by up to z nodes, even if the query itself failed to find a participant. A torrent's original author can bootstrap this replication by issuing r(0) dummy queries at publication time, before any real querying node has searched for it.

### Measured results
Live-network distribution measurement: 13 public BitTorrent trackers periodically scraped from 1 May to 3 July 2012 (64 days), observing 1.6 million distinct torrents (m) across 5.4 million distinct nodes (n), nodes identified by IP address. The observed node-count-per-torrent distribution follows a power law (fitted line 0.6x^-0.6 on log-log axes, Figure 1); 25% of all torrents were found on only 1 node, 76% on 10 or fewer, only 2% on more than 100 nodes; torrent replication ranged from 0 to 21,445 nodes, mean 27, median 3. Under this measured distribution, achieving P(d_i)=0.8 with z=100 queried nodes requires 86,214 nodes of replication (1.6% of the 5.4M-node network); with z=500, the required replication is 17,354 nodes; only 9 of the 1.6 million observed torrents met or exceeded that 17,354-node level. Overhead analysis (analytical, not measured on a live deployment): under stated packet-size assumptions (TCP/IP overhead 64 B/packet, BitTorrent handshake 68 B, 4 B length prefix, 20 B infohash, 6 B per returned node), for z=100, u=100 queries/hour, n=5,000,000, churn rate c=0.06/hour, the upper-bound download cost of a query is 6.8 KB when 1 result is returned and 8.8 KB when 100 results are returned. A node's response-side bandwidth is estimated at 39 bits/sec download and between 22 and 198 bits/sec upload, based on an observed average query rate of q=1.33 searches/node/hour from a cited prior measurement study. A node's local index reaches 1 GB after 289,184 hours (about 33 years) of continuous operation at an average received-request rate of 133 requests/hour. Simulation study: three separate simulation sets, each on a modeled network of 5,000,000 nodes with one observed torrent, run under a fluid churn model (seeding time exponentially distributed with rate gamma; downloading time bounded by an exponentially distributed patience with rate theta, subject to a 10%-of-file-per-hour download cap and 1%-of-file-per-hour upload cap per node), z=100 fixed, each simulation run to a 5% confidence-interval stopping criterion or a minimum trial count, whichever is greater. (1) Constant participation (a departing participant immediately replaced): at 10 constant participants, average query-success probability 14.26% (average 8 repeated queries to reach success); at 1000 constant participants, average success probability 87% on the first query, over 450 simulated hours. (2) Fixed participation with no replacement and no new queries: success probability declined by between 11.55 and 28.51 percentage points over 450 hours for participation levels of 1000, 100, and 10 nodes; stabilizing this decline would require an estimated 181, 46, and 15 dummy requests per hour respectively at those three participation levels. (3) Fluid query-rate model (peaking at 10,000, 1,000, or 100 simultaneous participants): average success probability 99.06% for the 10,000-peak torrents, 78.61% for the 1,000-peak torrents, 25.68% for the 100-peak torrents; even the worst-performing curve (100-peak) required an average of only 6 repeated queries before success.

### Parameters
- z (nodes queried per search attempt): discussion and simulations use z=100 as the working value; the paper's Figure 2 relates required query rate u to z at fixed target success probabilities P(d)=0.5, 0.7, 0.9, showing success probability is more sensitive to z than to u.
- n (network size): 5,000,000, taken from the 64-day measurement study.
- c (network churn rate): 0.06 per hour (6% of nodes leave and are replaced hourly), derived from a cited prior study's estimate of 8.06 hours average download time and 8.42 hours average seed time (16.48 hours total residence, so 1/16.48 ≈ 6%/hour); the paper states this may be an overestimate of churn because it does not account for nodes downloading multiple torrents.
- u (query rate for a torrent, queries/hour): example values of 50 and 100 used in the analytical discussion; at u=100, z=100, P(d_i)=0.96; at u=50, P(d_i)=0.81; at z=50 (u held at 100), P(d_i)=0.57.
- r(0) (author bootstrap replication): example target minimum success probabilities of P(d)=0.1 requires r(0)=5,265; P(d)=0.5 requires r(0)=34,538 (costing the authoring node about 4.5 MB of upload at the paper's per-query cost figure).
- gamma (fluid-model seeding-time rate) = 0.01(6 repeating) per hour in the simulation; theta (download-patience rate) = 0.025 per hour; lambda (node arrival-time rate in the peak-participation simulation) = 0.0(3 repeating) per hour.

### Stated limitations
The authors state that node identification by IP address means the study cannot distinguish nodes behind network address translation (NAT) from each other, and cannot distinguish nodes that share a reallocated IP address over time, both of which the authors state cause the reported node counts to be an undercount; they state they assume this impacts the reported figures only slightly, without a derivation. The overhead and replication analysis in Sections 4-5 assumes network churn removes a constant proportion of nodes every hour; the paper states this is a simplification later relaxed only in the Section 6 simulations, which use a fluid churn model instead. The paper explicitly leaves two mechanisms unbuilt and files them as future work: adaptively selecting the query size z based on a torrent's popularity, and adaptively triggering dummy queries so a torrent's tracking-data replication is kept high enough for a target success probability, rather than only at publication time. The index-eviction problem (removing tracking-data entries that have become stale or wrong because the recorded node no longer holds the torrent) is analyzed only qualitatively; the paper states three candidate strategies (least-recently-used eviction, periodic-ping health checks, and fixed-age eviction) and states that evaluating them is left as future work. The paper positions PAC search as a complement to existing torrent-discovery mechanisms, not a replacement.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
The mechanism requires every node to be reachable for direct point-to-point query and response (a node sending its torrent infohash to z randomly sampled peers and receiving either an empty response or a list of participant/indexer nodes), so it requires a random-peer-sampling capability at the network layer that can select z peers close to uniformly at random from the full node population; the paper assumes this sampling primitive exists (citing Kademlia and Brahms in its bibliography as prior mechanisms for it) without evaluating one itself. The bootstrap mechanism requires the torrent's authoring node to be willing to issue r(0) dummy queries at publication time and to bear that node's own upload bandwidth for doing so; the analysis assumes this authoring node is honest and cooperative, since a torrent whose author does not bootstrap replication and that has too few natural participants (the paper's own measurement shows 76% of torrents have 10 or fewer participants) is not shown to become discoverable without it. The replication model (Equation 3) treats query rate u as an external, uncontrolled input to the system; achieving a target discovery probability for an unpopular torrent requires either an out-of-band mechanism to raise u (participants issuing dummy queries) or accepting the lower probability, and the paper does not specify what triggers a participating node to start or stop issuing dummy queries beyond noting it as a future-work direction.

### Contradicts
None found against other entries in this evidence corpus. Within the paper, the authors address an apparent tension between their own 64-day measurement (most torrents held by very few nodes) and a cited prior study (Gummadi et al., SOSP 2003) reporting that most downloads are of highly replicated items, resolving it as the two studies measuring different things: torrent-count distribution here versus download-request distribution there.

- Cohen, Shenker, "Replication Strategies in Unstructured Peer-to-Peer Networks," ACM SIGCOMM CCR 2002 — foundational; the square-root-replication result this brief already treats as verified, cited here for its PAC-search framework.

- Terpstra, Kangasharju, "BubbleStorm: Resilient, Probabilistic, and Exhaustive Peer-to-Peer Search," ACM SIGCOMM CCR 37(4), 2007 — competing exhaustive probabilistic search mechanism.
- Bortnikov, Gurevich, Keidar, Kliot, Shraer, "Brahms: Byzantine resilient random membership sampling," Computer Networks 53(13), 2009 — foundational; a Byzantine-resilient implementation of the random-peer-sampling primitive this paper's z-node query step assumes.
- Timpanaro, Cholez, Chrisment, Festor, "BitTorrent's Mainline DHT Security Assessment," NTMS 2011 — attack; cited as showing the widely deployed BitTorrent DHT allows passive monitoring and node removal by malicious nodes, motivating this paper's unstructured alternative.
- Sit, Morris, "Security Considerations for Peer-to-Peer Distributed Hash Tables," IPTPS 2002 — attack; foundational DHT-security critique cited alongside the Mainline DHT assessment.
- Qiu, Srikant, "Modeling and Performance Analysis of BitTorrent-Like Peer-to-Peer Networks," ACM SIGCOMM CCR 34(4), 2004 — foundational; source of the fluid churn model used in this paper's Section 6 simulations.
- Guo, Chen, Xiao, Tan, Ding, Zhang, "A Performance Study of BitTorrent-Like Peer-to-Peer Systems," IEEE JSAC 25(1), 2007 — foundational; source of the average query rate (q=1.33 searches/node/hour) and download/seed duration figures this paper's bandwidth estimate is built on.
