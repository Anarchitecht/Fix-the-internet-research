## [SHENG-NDSS-24] Proof of Backhaul: Trustfree Measurement of Broadband Bandwidth
**Citation:** Peiyao Sheng, Nikita Yadav, Vishal Sevani, Arun Babu, S. V. R. Anand, Himanshu Tyagi, Pramod Viswanath. "Proof of Backhaul: Trustfree Measurement of Broadband Bandwidth." NDSS, 2024.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2024-764-paper.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2024-764-paper.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
Proof of Backhaul (PoB) lets a verifier that does not trust any single participant measure the backhaul bandwidth of an access point (the "prover") using a group of ordinary-bandwidth helper nodes ("challengers") instead of one dedicated high-bandwidth measurement server. A challenge coordinator selects n challengers, of which up to f = βn may be corrupted. Each selected challenger sends the prover a burst of UDP packets, each packet payload built only from Ed25519 digital signatures (so a corrupted party cannot omit plaintext content and still pass verification). The challenge duration is set long enough that the combined traffic from all challengers queues on the prover's single backhaul link, so the aggregate arrival rate at the prover reflects that link's capacity rather than any one challenger's own bandwidth. The prover hashes the packets it received from each challenger and builds a Merkle tree over those per-challenger hashes, then returns each challenger its own hash and the Merkle root; each challenger checks its Merkle proof and reports its measured round-trip time and packet count to the verifier. The verifier's bandwidth estimate uses the median of the reported round-trip times across challengers, which lowers the effect of individual challengers that report false round-trip times. Two participant attacks are named: a withholding attack, where a corrupted challenger sends fewer probe packets than instructed, and a rushing attack, where a corrupted challenger colludes with a corrupted prover to relay packets or acknowledgments outside the measured link to lower the apparent round-trip time and inflate the measured bandwidth. The protocol applies a correction factor α = (n − 2f)/(n − f) to the raw measured bandwidth to produce a "guaranteed bandwidth" that an honest prover is assured of, curtailing the inflation a rushing attack could otherwise produce. A deployed variant records challenge requests, participant selection, and results on the Ethereum blockchain, with a payer escrowing tokens that are distributed to the challengers used for that specific challenge.

### Parameters
- Adversarial threshold: protocol proven for β < 1/3 corrupted challengers with no verifier timer; the authors state β up to 1/2 is achievable if the verifier has access to a timer.
- Guaranteed-bandwidth correction factor: α = (n − 2f)/(n − f), applied to the raw measured bandwidth (tested at n=10, f=2, i.e. β=0.2).
- Design-scope variants (Table II in the paper, not independently re-tabulated here): TCP-based variant with a fairness assumption reaches accuracy (1 − β); the no-signature variant drops accuracy to (1 − 3β)/(1 − β) because it loses non-deniability of packet delivery.
- Challenge packet size: 1514-byte UDP packets (1472-byte payload, 42-byte header), each carrying 23 distinct 64-byte Ed25519 signatures, chosen because bare 64-byte packets defeat WiFi's MAC-layer packet aggregation and lower measured throughput.
- Ed25519 signature generation: 50–60 microseconds per signature on a 1 GB RAM, 1-CPU-core Linux host; challengers precompute all needed signatures before the challenge starts because generation time (about 1.4 ms for 23 signatures) can exceed packet transmission time at low challenger bandwidths.
- Challenge duration tested: 25, 50, 100, 200 ms, with 10 repeated experiments per duration.
- Number of challengers tested: 4, 6, 8, 10 (testbed), 10 (security evaluation), up to 500–1000 challenger groups (PoB-Shuffle scaling experiment).
- Prover backhaul rate-limited with the Linux `tc` utility; per-challenger bandwidth set to θ_P / n.
- Packet drop compensation: each challenger sends 10% more data than the nominal requirement to compensate for an assumed maximum 10% packet drop rate.

### Stated limitations
The authors state that curtailing bandwidth inflation from a rushing attack necessarily lowers the guaranteed bandwidth even when all challengers report honestly (the 28% reduction at β=0.2 shown above). They state that a corrupted challenger with access to an external high-bandwidth "backdoor" link to the prover can still mount a rushing attack, and that eliminating this requires either limiting corrupted challengers' access to such links or adding a shuffle phase (PoB-Shuffle) that pairs challengers to jointly sign packets, at the cost of added communication overhead for verification; the authors state that "implementing an efficient system for such a shuffle protocol in practice is an active area of research." They state that designing a cross-traffic-aware available-bandwidth variant that is both data-efficient and holds a fixed accuracy guarantee "is an open problem." They state the protocol assumes the amount of cross traffic does not vary during a measurement's steps. The liveness of the system (whether a challenge terminates and produces a result at all) depends on a challenge coordinator behaving correctly; the authors state a poorly performing coordinator can compromise liveness, though not accuracy or soundness, and rely on economic incentives (commission fees) and a pool of redundant coordinators. The technique requires latency measurements accurate enough to overcome internet jitter, and the authors' own pathchar re-measurement (see the Measured results table above) documents 50–100 ms of jitter in minimum-RTT estimation on the same link across repeated experiments, which is the reason the authors give for why pathchar-family techniques cannot measure 100+ Mbps links but PoB's traffic-aggregation approach can.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
The mechanism assumes digital signatures are available to every challenger and verifiable by the prover and verifier, and it names Ed25519 specifically for its low computational overhead relative to signature size. It requires the verifier or a challenge coordinator to be able to select a fresh, randomly drawn subset of challengers per measurement from a larger active pool, so that a corrupted prover cannot predict which parties will probe it. It requires all selected challengers to have a synchronized-enough view of time to compute round-trip times; the authors name NTP synchronization error (tens of milliseconds over the internet) as a source of measurement error the protocol must tolerate via the median-RTT step rather than eliminate. It requires the prover's backhaul to be modeled as a single bottleneck link, with the rest of the network core assumed effectively infinite-bandwidth (the paper states this as a modeling assumption, "we model the network core as a single point"). The on-chain deployment variant requires an underlying blockchain (Ethereum in the implementation) to record challenge requests and results and to hold escrowed payer tokens, plus a challenge coordinator role that is not itself Byzantine-fault-tolerant for liveness (only for correctness). The PoB-Shuffle extension requires pairwise coordination among challengers within a group before packet transmission, which raises per-measurement latency in proportion to group size t.
