## [WALLEZ-SP-25] TreeKEM: A Modular Machine-Checked Symbolic Security Analysis of Group Key Agreement in Messaging Layer Security
**Citation:** Théophile Wallez, Jonathan Protzenko, Karthikeyan Bhargavan. "TreeKEM: A Modular Machine-Checked Symbolic Security Analysis of Group Key Agreement in Messaging Layer Security." IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (S&P), 2025. DOI 10.1109/SP61157.2025.00228.
**Retrieved:** full text
**Source URL:** https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/410.pdf
**Domain:** H

### What it does
This paper proves the security of TreeKEM, the group-key-agreement core of the Messaging Layer Security (MLS) standard (RFC 9420), against a bit-level precise, executable specification of the protocol rather than against a simplified abstract model, so the proof covers the exact serialization formats, tree operations, and cryptographic encodings an interoperable implementation must produce. TreeKEM organizes a group's participants as leaves of a binary tree and derives, at each protocol epoch, a fresh epoch secret known only to the current membership. A participant updates the group by generating a new secret at its leaf and re-deriving every node on the path from that leaf to the root — the path update — encrypting each new node secret so that only the participants below it can decrypt; the topmost derived value, the commit secret, feeds a key schedule (§2.3 of the paper) that combines it with the group's prior init secret to produce the new epoch secret, giving forward secrecy (an epoch's secret stays hidden even after a later compromise) through key erasure and post-compromise security (a corrupted participant's secrets recover once they perform an update) through the tree structure. The protocol distinguishes proposals (individually constructed add/remove/update operations), commits (a bundle of proposals applied together, possibly with a path update), and epochs (the group state after a commit is processed); messages are relayed through an untrusted Delivery Service that receives commits from participants and rebroadcasts them, with the ordering of concurrent competing commits handled by that external service, not by TreeKEM itself. The proof is written using the DY* framework (a Dolev-Yao style symbolic model implemented as an F* verification methodology, where Dolev-Yao treats cryptographic operations as unbreakable black boxes that can only be defeated by an attacker who already holds the relevant key) applied directly to an executable F* specification that doubles as a reference implementation testable against interoperability test vectors. The paper's confidentiality theorem for TreeKEM is stated to compose with the prior authentication theorem for TreeSync (the MLS sub-protocol governing group-membership authentication, already in this corpus as WALLEZ-USENIXSEC-23), together giving a modular guarantee for the combination of the two components of the published standard.

The paper reports no runtime, memory, or proof-checking-time performance figures — the "1.5k" and "4k" line counts are the only quantitative results in the paper, and they characterize specification and proof size, not runtime performance.

### Parameters
Not applicable in the sense of tunable numeric parameters — this is a formal-methods proof over a fixed specification of the published MLS standard, not a system with configurable operating points. The one structural distinction the proof exploits, relative to a predecessor computational proof (Alwen, Coretti, Dodis, Tselekounis, cited as [6]) of MLS draft 12, is that the published MLS standard separates the initialization key from the leaf node key, whereas draft 12 did not; the paper states this separation "yields a stronger theorem in our case" for forward secrecy because new participants no longer need to retain the medium-term initialization key after joining.

### Stated limitations
The paper's threat model does not include bad randomness as a possible attacker capability, unlike the prior computational proof [6] it is compared against, which does model bad randomness. TreeKEM as analyzed relies on an untrusted Delivery Service that receives and rebroadcasts messages; the paper explicitly states that how competing concurrent commits from two different participants are resolved "falls outside the scope of the MLS protocol" and is not covered in this paper — that resolution is a property the delivery service, not TreeKEM, must provide. The proof does not cover TreeDEM, the third MLS sub-protocol responsible for group message encryption once an epoch secret is established; the authors state completing a machine-checked proof of TreeDEM, to finish verification of the full MLS standard, is future work. Post-quantum security of TreeKEM is stated as a second direction of future work, not addressed by this proof. The proof is symbolic (Dolev-Yao model, treating public-key encryption as an unbreakable black box short of key compromise) rather than computational; the paper states symbolic and computational models "have their strengths and weaknesses" — symbolic models yield stronger proof tooling and can handle more low-level protocol detail, but computational cryptographic assumptions are more precise about what an attacker with bounded computing power can and cannot do, a precision this proof does not provide. The Welcome-procedure epoch-authentication weakness found in Appendix A is stated by the authors not to affect TreeKEM's proven confidentiality guarantees, so it is a documented gap in a property (epoch binding at join time) the paper does not claim to close, not a break of the paper's central theorem.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Requires an untrusted Delivery Service external to TreeKEM that receives commit messages from every participant and rebroadcasts them to the rest of the group; MLS as analyzed here is explicitly not peer-to-peer — messages are not sent directly between participants. Requires that service, or some other external mechanism, to resolve the ordering of concurrent competing commits submitted by different participants, since TreeKEM's own specification and this proof do not define or verify that resolution. Requires each participant to process the sequence of commits it receives from the delivery service in the order delivered — the paper states MLS "assumes that each participant receives a sequence of commits from the delivery service and attempts to process them in order." Requires participants to perform key erasure as specified — the paper notes that in its formalization the epoch secret "is never actually stored" by an honest participant precisely because retaining it would break TreeDEM's forward secrecy, meaning any real implementation must erase this and related secrets promptly to obtain the guarantees this proof establishes. Requires the TreeSync component's authentication guarantee to hold for TreeKEM's confidentiality theorem to compose into a full guarantee for the published standard — this proof's result is stated as valid in combination with TreeSync's prior authentication theorem specifically, not as a standalone guarantee for TreeKEM in isolation. The Welcome-procedure weakness means a caller relying on strict epoch-binding for newly joined participants (a guarantee that a participant knows precisely which epoch they were invited into) needs an additional mechanism beyond what TreeKEM as specified provides, since the paper documents this gap as unresolved by its own theorems.

### Contradicts
None found. This paper's finding that the published MLS standard's initialization/leaf-node key separation yields a strictly stronger forward-secrecy theorem than the computational proof over MLS draft 12 (reference [6]) does not contradict [6] — it identifies a specification difference between the drafts, not a disagreement over a measurement of the same object.

- Competing/predecessor computational proof: analysis of MLS draft 12 against malicious group members focusing on TreeSync integrity mechanisms (reference [6]) — the specific pen-and-paper proof this paper directly compares its compromise granularity and forward-secrecy theorem strength against; retrieve for its own coarse-grained-compromise theorem statement to check against this paper's finer-grained one.
- Foundational: Cohn-Gordon, Cremers, Garratt, Millican, Milner, "On Ends-to-Ends Encryption: Asynchronous Group Messaging with Strong Security Guarantees," ACM CCS 2018 (reference [16]) — introduces the Asynchronous Ratcheting Tree (ART) design TreeKEM was proposed as a more efficient KEM-based alternative to.
- Attack/critique: Cremers, Hale, Kohbrok, "The Complexities of Healing in Secure Group Messaging: Why Cross-Group Effects Matter," USENIX Security 2021 (reference [17]) — studies post-compromise security for group messaging protocols including MLS; a critique-adjacent source worth checking for measured cross-group healing failures.
- Foundational: Brzuska, Cornelissen, Kohbrok, "Security Analysis of the MLS Key Derivation," IEEE S&P 2022 (reference [15]) — analyzes the MLS key schedule this paper's key-schedule description (§2.3) builds on.
- Foundational (semi-automated tool precedent): reference [13], symbolic F* analysis of the original TreeKEM design comparing its security to alternate designs, and reference [18], Tamarin-prover analysis of a simplified TreeKEM for forward secrecy only (explicitly not post-compromise or post-remove security) — both cited as partial precedents this paper's full machine-checked proof supersedes in scope.
