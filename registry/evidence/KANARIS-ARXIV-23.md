## [KANARIS-ARXIV-23] Mass Adoption of NATs: Survey and experiments on carrier-grade NATs
**Citation:** Orestis Kanaris, Johan Pouwelse. "Mass Adoption of NATs: Survey and experiments on carrier-grade NATs." arXiv:2311.04658, 2023. DOI 10.48550/ARXIV.2311.04658.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.04658
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04658
**Domain:** L

### What it does
This paper surveys techniques for establishing a direct peer-to-peer connection through a Network Address Translator (NAT), then tests one of them (the IPv8 network overlay's built-in NAT-penetration routines) between real Dutch mobile carriers on live 5G connections. A Carrier-Grade NAT (CGNAT) is a NAT operated by an Internet Service Provider (ISP) itself, translating many customers' local addresses to a shared pool of public IPv4 addresses, distinct from a home NAT that translates one household's devices; a CGNAT means the last NAT hop between a mobile device and the public Internet is outside the user's control and cannot be reconfigured through port-mapping protocols the way a home router can. The paper classifies NATs by two axes carried over from STUN (Session Traversal Utilities for NAT) and RFC 4787: mapping behavior — Endpoint-Independent Mapping (EIM, one external IP:port per internal IP:port regardless of destination) versus Endpoint-Dependent Mapping (EDM, a new external mapping per destination) — and, within STUN's four legacy categories (full-cone, restricted-cone, port-restricted-cone, symmetric), maps full-cone and both restricted-cone types to EIM and symmetric NAT to EDM. For two peers each behind an EIM NAT, the paper describes address discovery through a synchronizer/rendezvous server (the STUN role) followed by a coordinated simultaneous outbound packet exchange (puncturing) opening each side's NAT. For a peer behind an EDM NAT whose external port cannot be predicted, the paper describes brute-forcing the 65,535 possible ports, and computes that at the Netherlands' stated median download/upload speeds (~128 Mbps down, ~40 Mbps up) and a minimum encoded packet size (8-byte UDP payload plus 84 bytes of Ethernet-level overhead), a sender can transmit approximately 57,000 packets per second, so scanning all 65,535 ports against one fixed destination port takes a little over 1 second. When both peers are behind EDM NATs, neither's external port is known in advance, so the paper models the two-sided brute force as choosing a source-port/destination-port pair from 65,535² ≈ 4.29 billion combinations, and instead of exhaustive search proposes a birthday-paradox-style probabilistic attack: each side fires packets across many ports simultaneously, relying on the same collision-probability argument behind the birthday paradox (any pair among many packets can collide, not one packet against one fixed target) to find one matching pair far faster than exhaustive brute force. The paper also covers hairpinning (both peers behind the same CGNAT contact each other by routing through the CGNAT's own translation table via a discovered external address) as a fallback when direct puncturing fails, and pwnat, a technique using spoofed Internet Control Message Protocol (ICMP) messages to attempt to elicit an initial contact from a NATed peer without a third-party rendezvous server, which the paper's own analysis argues cannot work when a NATed client must respond to a spoofed ICMP Echo Request whose original query identifier it does not know.

### Parameters
- Live experiment: 4 Dutch mobile carrier SIM cards (Lyca Mobile, Lebara, T-Mobile, Vodafone), each device on 5G, running Kotlin-ipv8 on Android and the JVM build of IPv8 on one computer, exchanging preset UDP messages and holding the punctured mapping open for the whole experiment duration (duration not stated as a number).
- Assumed connection speed for the brute-force and birthday-paradox timing estimates: ~128 Mbps download, ~40 Mbps upload, described as the Netherlands' median Internet speed at the time of writing, cited to an external source.
- Assumed packet size for the throughput derivation: minimum 8-byte UDP payload, 84 bytes total Ethernet-level packet overhead, giving ~57,000 packets per second at the assumed connection speed.
- Port space per NAT side: 65,535 possible ports (2^16 − 1), giving 65,535² ≈ 4,294,836,225 combinations for the two-sided brute-force case.
- Router connection-table ceiling cited as a practical bound on the birthday-paradox approach: some routers (the paper names the Juniper SRX 300 as an example) cap active connections at 64,000, which the paper notes is close to the ~54,000-probe count needed for 50% collision probability.

### Stated limitations
The paper states pwnat "can mainly work in cases where only one of the parties is behind a NAT, not both," and gives a mechanical reason: the NATed client does not know the external query identifier a spoofed ICMP reply would need to carry, so the client's response (an ICMP Time Exceeded packet) reaches the far NAT with no matching mapping entry and is discarded by most NAT implementations. The paper states hairpinning — both peers behind the same CGNAT contacting each other via their externally discovered addresses — is "not always" supported by CGNAT implementations, leaving relaying as the only remaining option when both puncturing and hairpinning fail. The paper states full IPv6 rollout, the solution it calls ideal for eliminating CGNAT, "won't be realized soon," citing Google's 41.93% IPv6-adoption figure as evidence of a long remaining transition. The 21-hour and sub-4-second timing figures for brute-force and birthday-paradox puncturing are derived arithmetic under one assumed national-average connection speed, not measurements against real routers under load; the paper itself flags that "some routers max out at 64,000 active connections," meaning the derived timing estimates may not hold once a real router's connection-table ceiling is reached during a scan. The live 4-carrier experiment tested reachability and a fixed message exchange only; the paper reports no measurement of latency, throughput, or repeated trials, and reports only a single pass/fail outcome per carrier pair rather than a success rate over multiple attempts.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Two-sided EIM-to-EIM puncturing, as described here, requires a synchronizer or rendezvous server both peers can reach, to exchange externally observed addresses and coordinate a simultaneous outbound packet from each side; the paper states both peers must initiate communication at the same time, so the mechanism further needs a shared clock or synchronization signal between the two sides. Brute-force and birthday-paradox puncturing against an Endpoint-Dependent-Mapping (symmetric) NAT require sending large volumes of probe packets in a short window, so a system that adopts this technique needs to tolerate, or explicitly rate-limit against, the router-side connection-table exhaustion the paper itself identifies as a practical ceiling (as low as 64,000 entries on some tested-elsewhere hardware) before the probability estimates in this paper hold. Any system that wants a peer's address discovery to work through a CGNAT depends on either CGNAT hairpinning being enabled by the operator (which the paper states is inconsistent across CGNAT deployments) or a fallback relay being available for the case where hairpinning fails. The comparison of NAT categories in this paper depends on the classification scheme in RFC 4787 and the STUN specification, so a system implementing this paper's decision logic needs the same NAT-type detection this paper assumes as an input, not something it supplies itself.
