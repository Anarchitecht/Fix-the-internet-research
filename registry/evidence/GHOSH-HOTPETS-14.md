## [GHOSH-HOTPETS-14] A TorPath to TorCoin: Proof-of-Bandwidth Altcoins for Compensating Relays
**Citation:** Mainak Ghosh, Miles Richardson, Bryan Ford, Rob Jansen. "A TorPath to TorCoin: Proof-of-Bandwidth Altcoins for Compensating Relays." Workshop on Hot Topics in Privacy Enhancing Technologies (HotPETs), 2014.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://dedis.cs.yale.edu/dissent/papers/hotpets14-torpath.pdf
**Source URL:** https://dedis.cs.yale.edu/dissent/papers/hotpets14-torpath.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
TorPath lets the participants of one Tor (The Onion Router, an anonymity network that routes traffic through a multi-relay circuit so no single relay learns both a client's identity and its destination) circuit jointly generate cryptographic proof of how much end-to-end goodput (successfully delivered payload throughput) that specific circuit carried, without any centralized bandwidth authority, so that proof can back the minting of a companion currency (TorCoin) proportional to measured transfer rather than to a self-report. The construction has two layers. TorPath first replaces Tor's directory servers with a decentralized group of assignment servers that assemble a circuit (one client plus an entry, middle, and exit relay) for each client through a three-stage protocol: in Group Initialization, clients and relays each submit a temporary public key onion-encrypted (successively encrypted in layers, one per assignment server, so no single server can read the innermost content alone) under all assignment servers in the forming consensus group; in Verifiable Shuffle, the consensus group jointly and verifiably permutes each column of the resulting key matrix using the Neff shuffle (a shuffle whose correctness -- that the output is a permutation of the input without revealing the permutation -- any observer can verify), so each output row becomes one anonymous circuit's four temporary keys with no single participant learning which real identity holds which key; in Path Lookup, each participant learns only the IP address of its immediate circuit neighbor by a second onion-encrypted, shuffled exchange, so a client learns only its entry relay and each relay learns only its adjacent circuit member. Each assigned circuit receives a Circuit Identifier formed from the hash of the shuffle matrix and the circuit's row index. TorCoin then runs a proof-of-bandwidth protocol on top of an assigned circuit: every m Tor packets, each of the four circuit participants commits to a fresh temporary hash value, the values accumulate into a shared blob as they pass from client to exit relay and back, and once the blob is fully formed and signed by all four participants using their circuit-specific temporary keys, the client checks whether a hash of the assembled blob has enough low-order zero bits to count as a mined coin (a Bitcoin-style proof-of-work check, applied to a value that could only have been assembled by all four participants having actually forwarded traffic to each other, rather than to arbitrary computation); because each of the four contributions is revealed only after all parties have committed to their own hash first, no strict subset of the four participants can reconstruct the blob on their own to mint coins without the others' cooperation. A valid coin's sequence number must be exactly one more than the last coin recorded from that circuit's identifier on the blockchain (Bitcoin's distributed, append-only transaction ledger, reused here to prevent a coin from that circuit being claimed twice), and is capped by a fixed, publicly known per-circuit coin limit. The mined coin is split with one third of its value paid to each of the three relays via the standard Bitcoin transfer protocol.

### Parameters
- m (the number of Tor packets between successive rounds of TorCoin proof-of-bandwidth generation): the paper states m greater than or equal to 10 yields roughly 5% overhead, and states m can be tuned in further experimentation to trade transmission efficiency against incentive granularity for relay operators; no other specific value is tested or recommended.
- Consensus-group quorum size: given only as an illustrative example ("if there are 10 assignment servers in the network, we might require at least 6 of them to participate"), not a value the paper adopts as a recommendation.
- Consensus-group size n (number of clients gathered before a shuffle proceeds): stated as configurable, trading a larger anonymity set against longer circuit-assignment wait time; no numeric value given or tested.
- Per-circuit TorCoin mint limit (the cap on coins any one assigned circuit may mine): stated to exist and to be recorded in the blockchain for verification, with no numeric value given.
- Assumed key/hash/signature length in the overhead measurement: 32 bytes.
- Standard Tor cell size used in the overhead derivation: 514 bytes.

### Stated limitations
The paper states the Neff shuffle used for circuit assignment is complex and requires several rounds of communication among assignment servers, and states this cost is tolerated only under the unverified expectation that assignment servers will number fewer than 10 and be well-provisioned; the paper offers a workaround (using conventional Tor circuits immediately, then transitioning to TorCoin circuits once assigned) for latency-sensitive users rather than a measured latency figure. TorPath's circuit-assignment mechanism is stated to be incompatible with the deployed Tor network because it requires route assignment and access control that ordinary Tor relays and directory servers do not implement, though the authors state a single physical machine could run both a standard Tor relay and a TorPath/TorCoin service simultaneously. The Neff shuffle applied per-position can assign the same physical relay to more than one position (for example, both entry and middle) on the same circuit; the authors state this should be rare with a reasonable number of participating relays and consider it no worse than Tor's existing risk of one operator controlling multiple relays on a circuit by chance, and explicitly defer building any detection mechanism for it to future work. The paper explicitly states that Bitcoin's transaction layer offers only limited payment anonymity and that this limitation is inherited unmodified by TorCoin, presenting Zerocoin, Mixcoin, and CoinShuffle only as candidate, unimplemented fixes to that separate problem. The design as specified always assigns three fresh relays per circuit rather than persisting a stable entry relay across circuits (Tor's actual practice, adopted for a different reason -- limiting a client's cumulative exposure to malicious entry relays over time); the authors sketch a persistent-entry-relay variant of the assignment matrix but state it comes at the cost of a higher chance of forming an all-colluding circuit, without giving a numeric bound on that increase.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Verifying that a claimed TorCoin corresponds to real bandwidth transfer requires a reader to hold the full public consensus log of shuffle matrices produced by assignment servers, since a Circuit Identifier is checked against that log and the four temporary public keys it names; a system without persistent access to that log cannot independently audit a coin. Assignment-server honesty is assumed only in majority: the adversary model states the design tolerates a minority of assignment servers being controlled by an adversary, with the rest merely honest-but-curious (they follow the protocol but may try to infer private information from what they see), and the anonymity and coin-integrity guarantees are stated to hold conditional on that majority assumption. The mechanism assumes that at most half of the temporary keys across the network belong to colluding participants, under which condition the paper computes that only 1/16 (1/2^4, since a circuit has four positions) of assigned circuits are fully colluding and able to mint coins for zero genuine bandwidth transfer; a higher colluding fraction was not analyzed by the paper and would raise this fraction. The mining check itself requires that the underlying transport reliably deliver each of the four onion-forwarded commit/reveal messages per round in order, since a message lost or reordered on the forward or return path prevents the blob from being validly formed and signed by all four participants. Anything reading TorCoin balances for a reward or reputation purpose downstream must treat coin possession as proof of goodput on one specific, now-identified circuit at mint time, not as a general, ongoing measure of a relay's capacity or honesty, since the mechanism produces no per-relay aggregate score, only per-circuit, per-round proofs.

### Contradicts
None found within this paper's own claims; the authors describe their own overhead figures explicitly as preliminary and simulated, not as a production measurement, so no later paper's field measurement of TorCoin overhead should be read as confirming or refuting a claim this paper does not make. No other entry in this batch reports a comparable bandwidth-backed incentive currency to compare numerically.
