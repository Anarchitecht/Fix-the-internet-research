## [RICHTER-IMC-16] A Multi-perspective Analysis of Carrier-Grade NAT Deployment

**Citation:** Philipp Richter, Florian Wohlfart, Narseo Vallina-Rodriguez, Mark Allman, Randy Bush, Anja Feldmann, Christian Kreibich, Nicholas Weaver, Vern Paxson. "A Multi-perspective Analysis of Carrier-Grade NAT Deployment." ACM Internet Measurement Conference (IMC), 2016. DOI 10.1145/2987443.2987474.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.prichter.com/imc176-richterA.pdf
**Source URL:** https://doi.org/10.1145/2987443.2987474
**Domain:** L

### What it does
The paper measures how many Internet access networks place a Carrier-Grade NAT (CGN), an address translator an Internet service provider (ISP) operates inside its own network rather than at the customer premises, between a residential or mobile subscriber and the public Internet, and characterizes how those CGNs behave. A CGN operates transparently to the endpoints it serves, which the paper states is what makes detecting and measuring one from outside difficult: no packet a CGN forwards carries evidence that translation occurred, and a sender cannot tell how many times, if any, its own source address will be rewritten on the path to a destination.

Two independent detection methods are used, combined rather than relied on singly. The first crawls the BitTorrent Distributed Hash Table (DHT), a peer discovery protocol, and exploits the fact that some peers behind a CGN mistake their internally assigned, non-routable address (drawn from address ranges reserved for private use, such as 10.0.0.0/8 or the CGN-specific 100.64.0.0/10 block) for a publicly reachable one and announce it into the DHT; other DHT peers that separately query that same host and observe its real, routable source address then "leak" both addresses together into peer lists the crawler collects, letting the paper associate one internal address with one external, routable address, and hence with an Autonomous System (AS, a network operated under one administrative routing policy). The second method extends Netalyzr, the authors' pre-existing network-troubleshooting tool users invoke voluntarily, adding: a device-reported local address compared against the address the Netalyzr server observes as the packet's source, revealing any translation; a User Plug and Play (UPnP) query to the customer's own router for its externally learned address, letting the paper distinguish translation performed by the customer's own equipment from translation performed further upstream; a TTL (Time To Live)-limited keepalive probing technique that forces the mapping state of one hop at a time to expire while others are kept alive, precisely locating which hop on the path is a stateful NAT and how long its mapping persists before timing out; and an implementation of the STUN (Session Traversal Utilities for NAT) protocol that determines the most restrictive NAT mapping type present on the path, because STUN's probe-and-reply exchange reports the behavior of whichever on-path NAT is least permissive.

### Parameters
- BitTorrent DHT crawl duration: one week, starting March 3, 2016.
- Netalyzr TTL-enumeration test idle-time ceiling: 200 seconds (a session-length practical limit, not a protocol limit).
- Port-preservation classification threshold: at least 20% of ports preserved across a session's 10 sequential TCP flows.
- Sequential-allocation classification threshold: consecutive port differences under 50.
- Chunk-based allocation classification: at least 20 sessions showing random port translation, all falling within a port range under 16,384 (16K).
- Eyeball-AS definition 1 (Spamhaus Policy Block List): AS holding at least 2,048 addresses in end-user address blocks.
- Eyeball-AS definition 2 (APNIC Labs AS Population list): AS with at least 1,000 recorded samples.
- Minimum session count per AS for TTL-enumeration and STUN filtering: at least 3 sessions from the same AS/CGN-classification combination.

### Stated limitations
The paper states its methods together give only a partial, not exhaustive, view of CGN deployment, because each depends on voluntary user-run software: the BitTorrent-DHT method does not illuminate mobile-carrier CGN use, since mobile devices rarely run BitTorrent clients; the Netalyzr method depends on users choosing to run the tool, which the paper states makes its measured population smaller than the DHT crawl's but under the authors' direct control (letting them add explicit tests such as UPnP probing that the DHT method cannot supply). The TTL-driven NAT enumeration test cannot detect a NAT whose idle-state mapping timeout exceeds 200 seconds, the practical ceiling imposed by overall Netalyzr session-length limits, which the paper states leads to underestimating the true number of stateful NATs present (30.9% of tests in Table 7 found no expired mapping, some unknown fraction of which are undetected long-timeout NATs rather than true absences). The enumeration technique also cannot by itself distinguish a NAT from any other stateful middlebox, such as a stateful firewall performing no address translation, though the paper states this ambiguous case affects fewer than 0.5% of tests and those cases are excluded from the reported figures. The paper states it remains out of scope for this work to precisely measure the effect of CGNs on end-user application performance; that measurement is left to future work.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Neither detection method requires cooperation from the ISP or CGN operator: the BitTorrent method depends only on the DHT protocol's existing peer-list propagation behavior and a crawler that can issue DHT queries and record replies; the Netalyzr method depends only on end users voluntarily installing and running the tool, and on the tool's own server distinguishing the packet's observed source address from the address the client software reports locally. The TTL-driven enumeration technique specifically depends on both the client and the measurement server being able to send TTL-limited packets and observe whether a given hop still forwards traffic after an idle period, which requires a stable number of hops on the path for the duration of one test (the paper filters out results from paths whose hop count changes mid-test). The STUN-based mapping-type test depends on a publicly reachable STUN server capable of replying from a different port and/or address than it received the probe on, per the STUN protocol (RFC 3489, already in this batch as ROSENBERG-RFC-03), and reports only the most restrictive behavior among all on-path NATs, so it cannot by itself attribute a given restriction to a specific hop; the paper compensates for this by taking the least-restrictive AS-wide result as its estimate of CGN-specific (as opposed to CPE-specific) behavior. Any design relying on NAT mapping type or timeout to predict connectivity (for example, a peer-to-peer hole-punching scheme) must treat the CGN's own restrictiveness as independent of, and potentially more restrictive than, the customer's own CPE NAT, since the paper measures CGNs frequently applying stricter (for example, symmetric) mapping and shorter timeouts than commonly deployed home routers.

### Contradicts
None found in this batch. The paper's own comparison (Section 6.5) states CGN mapping is frequently more restrictive than CPE-router mapping, a claim the paper backs with its own STUN measurement rather than a comparison against an external source's figure.
