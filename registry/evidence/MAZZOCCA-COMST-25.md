## [MAZZOCCA-COMST-25] A Survey on Decentralized Identifiers and Verifiable Credentials

**Citation:** Carlo Mazzocca, Abbas Acar, A. Selcuk Uluagac, Rebecca Montanari, Paolo Bellavista, Mauro Conti. "A Survey on Decentralized Identifiers and Verifiable Credentials." IEEE Communications Surveys & Tutorials, 2025 (arXiv preprint 2024). DOI 10.1109/COMST.2025.3543197.
**Retrieved:** full text
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02455
**Domain:** E

### What it does
The survey documents Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs, a W3C-standardized identifier scheme independent of any centralized registry) and Verifiable Credentials (VCs, a W3C-standardized data model for cryptographically signed claims) as the two components of Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI, an identity model giving the identity owner direct control over their own identity data rather than a third party). A DID resolves to a DID Document, a JSON-LD document holding the subject's public keys, service endpoints, and authentication parameters, published through a Verifiable Data Registry (VDR), the paper's general term for whatever system stores and serves DID Documents, most commonly a distributed ledger. Resolution of a DID method-specific identifier to its DID Document happens through a universal resolver, an interface that dispatches to a method-specific DID adapter, so a verifier need not implement every DID method itself. A VC is issued by an issuer to a holder's DID, presented to a verifier as a Verifiable Presentation (VP) bound to a challenge/nonce to block replay, and verified against the issuer's public key retrieved from the VDR; the holder proves control of the credential by signing with the private key corresponding to the DID inside it. The paper catalogs nine threat categories against this architecture (key/credential compromise, credential-validity drift, several privacy-linkage threats, and man-in-the-middle/replay) each with a corresponding mitigation drawn from the cited literature, compares five open-source implementation toolkits (DIDKit, IOTA Identity Framework, Hyperledger Aries, Microsoft Entra Wallet Library, Veramo) on language, credential format, proof types, and VDR backend, and reviews VC revocation mechanisms, principally the W3C's bitstring-based "Revocation List 2020," in which each bit position corresponds to one credential's revocation status.

### Measured results
This is a survey; the authors ran no experiment, implementation benchmark, or simulation of their own, and no throughput, latency, or scalability figure in the paper is a measurement performed by these authors. The one concrete numeric example in the paper is illustrative, drawn from the cited W3C Revocation List 2020 specification rather than measured by this paper's authors: a bitstring covering revocation status for a credential population, at 16 KB uncompressed, compresses to 135 bytes via ZLIB, attributed to the property that most credentials remain valid so most bits share a value and compress well. No sample size, credential-population size, or bit-density condition is stated for this figure, so it is recorded here only as an illustrative example from the cited specification, not as an independently reproducible measurement.

### Parameters
The paper states no protocol parameters with numeric defaults of its own (it is not proposing a new mechanism); it reports parameters others' systems use as descriptive facts:
- DID types per the DID specification: Anywise (usable with an unbounded set of parties), Pairwise (known only to the subject and one counterparty, reducing cross-context correlation), N-wise (known to exactly N parties, generalizing Pairwise at N=2).
- Revocation List 2020 bitstring: one bit per credential index; bit=1 denotes revoked, bit=0 denotes valid; compressed with ZLIB before distribution.
- Table IV (implementation comparison) records categorical, not numeric, parameters per toolkit: DIDKit (Rust core, JSON-LD/JWT credential formats, RSA/EdDSA/ECDSA/EIP712/JWS2020 proof types, no built-in key/wallet management, SD-JWT selective disclosure); IOTA Identity Framework (Rust/Node.js, JWT format, EdDSA/ECDSA proof types, Stronghold key management, Tangle-based VDR, SD-JWT and Zero-Knowledge Selective Disclosure); Hyperledger Aries (Python/JavaScript/Go/.NET, JSON-LD format, BBS+/EdDSA proof types, Indy Ledger VDR, SD-JWT, described as steepest learning curve of the five); Microsoft Entra Wallet Library (Android/iOS only, JWT format, EdDSA/ES256K/ECDSA P-256 proof types, Azure Key Vault key management, SD-JWT); Veramo (JavaScript, JSON-LD/JWT format, EdDSA/ECDH/ECDSA proof types, selective disclosure through a plugin interface).

### Stated limitations
The paper states that W3C's DID and VC standards do not specify how a VDR must be implemented, leaving DLT choice, indexing, and access-control design entirely to each deployment. It states that DID method specifications vary in format, completeness, information density, and versioning across implementations, which it identifies as an open interoperability problem, not a solved one. It states that only one revocation mechanism (Revocation List 2020) is close to W3C standard status at the time of writing, and that only one other work in the literature it surveyed (its own authors' prior EVOKE paper) proposes an efficient alternative revocation mechanism, specifically for IoT (Internet of Things) networks; it states the scalability of current revocation approaches in high-throughput or constrained-device settings remains underexplored. It states DID key rotation, the mechanism for recovering from a compromised private key by publishing a new public key signed with the old one, reduces but does not eliminate the vulnerability window between compromise and detection, and that frequent rotation introduces its own usability burden. It states that granting a third party (a DID controller distinct from the DID subject) authority to manage a DID document introduces an impersonation risk if that controller generates a new key pair and substitutes it into the document, and that robust authorization/auditing mechanisms to detect unauthorized DID Document modification are called for but not yet standardized. It states selective disclosure (revealing only a subset of a VC's claims) does not by itself prevent a verifier from linking repeated presentations of the same credential to one individual, and that Pairwise DIDs reduce, without eliminating, that linkage risk.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
The architecture requires a VDR that is available whenever a verifier needs to resolve a DID or check an issuer's public key; the paper states verifiers can reduce live-lookup dependence by locally caching the public keys of issuers they already trust, but this is described as a mitigation, not a removal, of the availability requirement. It requires a functioning DID method adapter registered with the universal resolver for every DID method a verifier might encounter, since the universal resolver itself only dispatches to method-specific adapters rather than resolving any method natively. It requires a revocation-status distribution channel (a registry, a bitstring endpoint, or equivalent) that both issuers and verifiers can reach, since a VC's stated validity period alone does not account for early revocation due to loss of privilege. It requires each identity owner to hold and operate a digital wallet capable of managing private keys, constructing VPs, and performing key rotation, placing key-management and backup obligations onto the end-user device or an escrow service the paper describes only as a named mitigation option (Trusted Third Party Key Recovery), not a specified mechanism. It requires an out-of-band accountability layer (Know-Your-Customer / Anti-Money-Laundering–style verification) wherever regulatory identity screening is required, since the paper states DID/VC privacy preservation and regulatory screening are in tension and unresolved by the DID/VC data model itself.
