## [UNGER-SP-15] SoK: Secure Messaging
**Citation:** Nik Unger, Sergej Dechand, Joseph Bonneau, Sascha Fahl, Henning Perl, Ian Goldberg, Matthew Smith. "SoK: Secure Messaging." IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (S&P), 2015. DOI 10.1109/SP.2015.22.
**Retrieved:** full text
**Source URL:** https://www.ieee-security.org/TC/SP2015/papers-archived/6949a232.pdf
**Domain:** H

### What it does
This paper is a Systematization of Knowledge (SoK): it does not propose a new mechanism, and its contribution is an evaluation framework that decomposes secure messaging into three near-orthogonal problems and scores every known approach to each against a fixed set of properties, so a reader can compare mechanisms on a common basis rather than each paper's own claims. The three problems are trust establishment (verifying that a long-term cryptographic key is actually associated with the intended real-world party — for example, key directories, Trust-On-First-Use, or out-of-band fingerprint verification), conversation security (protecting message confidentiality, integrity, and authentication once trust is established — for example, static asymmetric encryption, Diffie-Hellman ratcheting, or deniable group key exchange), and transport privacy (hiding who is communicating with whom from network and server observers — for example, onion routing, Dining Cryptographer networks (DC-nets), broadcast, or Private Information Retrieval). For each problem the paper defines security/privacy properties, usability properties, and adoption properties, then rates every catalogued approach (and named example tool or protocol) against every property using a three-value scale (provides / partially provides / does not provide), assembled into three tables (Tables I, II, III). The threat model considers a local network adversary, a global network adversary (each active or passive), and service-provider operators as potential adversaries, all of whom are also assumed to be legitimate participants in the messaging system able to start conversations and send messages; endpoint compromise (malware, hardware attacks) is explicitly out of scope. Usability scoring is derived from expert cognitive walkthroughs of actual implementations against Nielsen's usability principles, not from user studies with human subjects.

### Measured results
This paper reports no quantitative measurements from an experiment, testbed, or implementation the authors ran. Its evaluation is a qualitative property-satisfaction rating (full / partial / none) assigned by the authors' own expert review to each catalogued protocol design, published as Tables I (trust establishment), II (conversation security), and III (transport privacy). No node counts, latencies, message sizes, or other quantities with stated experimental conditions appear; per the extraction rule that a figure without experimental conditions is not usable evidence, no numeric result from this paper is recorded here as measured fact. Where the paper does state comparative outcomes for named systems (for example, that a broadcast transport system, Bitmessage, uses proof-of-work or fees to limit spam), these are qualitative design descriptions the authors extracted from those systems' own specifications, not measurements this paper's authors performed.

### Stated limitations
Endpoint security (malware and hardware attacks) is explicitly excluded from the threat model. The paper does not audit implementations for software exploits and assumes cryptographic primitives are correctly implemented, only penalizing a scheme if it lets end users misuse primitives in ways the design permits. Higher-level group-chat features — invitation mechanisms, removing users, chat-room moderation — are stated as out of scope because they operate above the secure-messaging protocol layer. The paper's own conclusion states that no conversation security protocol in Table II provides all desired properties simultaneously, and that no transport-privacy approach in Table III provides strong privacy against global network adversaries while remaining practical for synchronous use. The authors state group conversation security is unresolved: "the situation is less clear for secure group conversations; while no approach is a clear answer." Multi-device support is identified as broadly unaddressed: only the least secure protocols in Table II support multiple devices without a pairing procedure. The authors state it "remains unclear exactly what consistency properties are required to match users' expectations" for group protocols, and call for usability research to guide future group-protocol design — an open question the paper does not resolve. Usability results come from expert cognitive walkthroughs of the authors' own choosing, not controlled user studies; the authors distinguish this from — and cite as inspiration but not replacement for — prior user studies of specific tools (Whitten and Tygar's "Why Johnny Can't Encrypt" and its several follow-ups).

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
As a systematization, this paper does not itself impose runtime requirements on a system; it states, as a finding rather than a design requirement, what other papers' mechanisms need. Its most load-bearing structural claim for downstream design is that the three problems (trust establishment, conversation security, transport privacy) are "nearly orthogonal" and their solutions "can largely be chosen independently" — a composition claim that licenses selecting one mechanism per layer, but the paper simultaneously cautions that layering an anonymity-preserving transport under a conversation-security layer that leaks key identifiers "undermines" the transport's anonymity guarantee (the paper's own "Anonymity Preserving" property), so a selection at one layer can still destroy a guarantee claimed at another and the two layers are not fully independent in practice. The paper's recommended trust-establishment combination — central key directories, transparency logs for global consistency of directory entries, and optional out-of-band verification for security-conscious users — requires an operational key-directory service and a transparency-log infrastructure that neither this paper builds nor specifies in implementable detail; it is presented as the authors' judgment of the best available trade-off, not a proven or measured result, and must be attributed to the authors as a value judgment rather than treated as an established property of any single mechanism in the corpus.

### References worth retrieving
- Foundational: Whitten, Tygar, "Why Johnny Can't Encrypt: A Usability Evaluation of PGP 5.0," USENIX Security Symposium, 1999 — the seminal usability study the paper's own usability framework is built in response to.
- Foundational: Wendlandt, Andersen, Perrig, "Perspectives: Improving SSH-style Host Authentication with Multi-Path Probing," USENIX Annual Technical Conference, 2008 — cited as the origin of Trust-On-First-Use (TOFU), a trust-establishment baseline this paper's Table I evaluates.
- Competing: the DGKE-based mpOTR protocol (deniable group key exchange, cited as references [67],[68]) — a group conversation-security approach the paper evaluates in detail against Table II's properties, a direct comparison point for any deniable group key agreement mechanism considered in this corpus's synthesis.
- Competing: GOTR (2013), a "hot-pluggable" group key agreement protocol using circle keys (cited as reference [69]) — evaluated as achieving message unlinkability and flexible group membership, properties the paper's mpOTR evaluation finds lacking; worth retrieving to check its own measured costs.
- Competing: Dissent and Verdict, shuffle-based DC-net systems providing group anonymity with global-adversary resilience (cited as references [88]-[91]) — the paper's transport-privacy comparison identifies these as addressing DC-nets' collision/denial-of-service weakness through a verifiable shuffle, worth retrieving for their own measured scalability figures.
- Attack/critique: unspecified statistical deanonymization analyses of onion routing by global network adversaries referenced in Section V — the paper states such analysis can use content size, transmission direction, packet counts, and timing, but does not itself cite a single paper by name for this claim in the passage extracted; a full-text pass over the reference list is needed to identify the specific citation.
- Foundational: the FS-IBE (Forward-Secure Identity-Based Encryption) scheme discussed in the paper's Section IV-C3, cited as [57] — stated by the paper to resolve server-complexity and denial-of-service problems the TextSecure prekeys approach has, at the cost of new scalability and performance issues, and to have "received a considerable amount of follow-up research," making it a high-value forward-mining target.
