## [WEI-INFOCOM-12] SybilDefender: Defend Against Sybil Attacks in Large Social Networks
**Citation:** Wei Wei, Fengyuan Xu, Chiu C. Tan, Qun Li. "SybilDefender: Defend Against Sybil Attacks in Large Social Networks." IEEE INFOCOM, 2012. DOI 10.1109/INFCOM.2012.6195572.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.cs.wm.edu/~liqun/paper/infocom12_1.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.cs.wm.edu/~liqun/paper/infocom12_1.pdf
**Domain:** F

### What it does
SybilDefender decides whether one specified suspect node in a social graph is a Sybil identity, and separately finds the whole cluster of Sybil nodes surrounding a known Sybil node, using only a bounded number of random walks rather than by processing the entire graph. It also proposes two measures for limiting how many attack edges (a link between a real user's node and a Sybil node) a real-world social network permits.

Mechanism, sybil identification: the social graph has a small cut of attack edges separating a large honest region from a smaller Sybil region. A random walk of a fixed length that starts inside the Sybil region tends to stay trapped inside it, so it visits (traverses) fewer distinct nodes than a same-length walk starting in the honest region. A pre-processing phase (run once per graph) picks a known honest node h, performs f short random walks of length log(n) from h to obtain f additional nodes that are honest with high probability under a fast-mixing assumption (defined below), and treats h plus those f nodes as judge nodes. For each candidate walk length l from a minimum l_min to a maximum l_max in steps of 100, the algorithm performs R random walks of length l from every judge node, counts for each judge node the number of nodes visited at least t times (t a small constant), and records the mean and standard deviation of that count across judges — producing one tuple (l, mean, stdDeviation) per length. To then test a suspect node u, the algorithm performs R random walks of length l starting from l_0 and doubles l on each iteration up to l_max; at each length it compares the suspect's own count of nodes visited at least t times against the judges' recorded mean for that length, and declares u a Sybil the first time the shortfall exceeds stdDeviation times a scaling constant alpha; if no length up to l_max triggers this, u is declared honest.

Mechanism, sybil community detection (finds the whole cluster around one already-known Sybil node s, rather than testing nodes one at a time): first estimate a suitable partial-walk length by repeatedly doubling a starting length and running R partial random walks from s at each candidate length until the fraction of "dead" walks (walks that exhaust the graph's reachable local region before reaching the target length) reaches a threshold beta close to 1. Then perform R partial random walks of that length from s, tally how often each node is visited, sort nodes by visit frequency, and greedily grow a candidate set S by adding nodes from the sorted list whenever doing so does not increase the set's conductance (the fraction of edge-endpoints in S that cross to outside S, a measure of how tight the boundary cut around S is); repeat sweeping the list until conductance stops improving across two full passes, and output the resulting S as the detected Sybil community.

Mechanism, limiting attack edges (two proposed techniques, evaluated only by a user survey, not by an attack simulation): (1) let users rate each of their existing relationships as "Friend" or "Stranger" through a purpose-built application, and drop "Stranger"-rated edges before running Sybil defense; (2) restrict the graph used for defense to an "activity network," where an edge exists only between users who have actually interacted (not merely declared a relationship), which is argued to naturally exclude unused attack edges.

### Measured results
All experiments use two full real-world social-graph data sets: Orkut (3,072,441 nodes, 117,185,083 edges, average degree 76.28) and Facebook (3,097,165 nodes, 28,377,481 edges, average degree 18.32) — described in the paper as the largest data sets ever used to evaluate topology-based Sybil defenses at the time. Sybil regions were synthetically attached to these real graphs using either a Preferential Attachment (PA, scale-free) model or an Erdos-Renyi (ER, random) model; the PA sybil region's average degree matches the host data set's average degree, while the ER sybil region's average degree ranges from 8 to 11 (a sparse topology by comparison). All experiments used the full published data sets with no small-degree-node pre-filtering (unlike some prior evaluations the paper cites).

### Stated limitations
SybilDefender is explicitly a centralized mechanism: "the administrator knows the social network topology," justified only by the observation that all current online social networks are already under centralized administrative control — it is not designed to run without a central party with full graph visibility. It assumes at least one node in the network is already known with certainty to be honest, as the starting point for the whole identification algorithm; the paper does not test what happens if that assumption is violated. It assumes the size of the Sybil region is not comparable to the size of the honest region, and states this is reasonable only by appeal to the large user bases of networks like Facebook, Twitter, and Orkut at the time and to account-creation friction (email verification, CAPTCHAs); the paper provides no bound on how close the two region sizes can get before the identification algorithm breaks down. It assumes the honest region is fast-mixing (a random walk of length Theta(log n) converges to the graph's stationary distribution with probability at least 1 - 1/n), citing prior work (Yu et al.) as having shown real social networks are fast-mixing, without re-validating that claim itself. It assumes the number of attack edges is limited, and the paper's own survey data shows this assumption is not automatically satisfied on real online social networks (Bilge et al.'s prior finding that roughly 20% of bogus friendship requests on Facebook are accepted, cited by the authors, and the authors' own survey finding 19.8% of rated relations are "Stranger"). Nodes that lie directly on the small cut (compromised nodes connected by attack edges) are the ones the algorithm systematically fails to identify correctly, in both the identification and community-detection algorithms — this is stated as the source of essentially all observed errors, not merely a hypothetical edge case. Finding the true minimum-size cut between honest and Sybil regions is NP-hard, which the paper states as the reason the community-detection algorithm approximates the cut with the greedy conductance heuristic instead of solving it exactly.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Requires a single administrative party (or a coalition acting as one) with complete, current knowledge of the social graph's full edge set; the algorithm cannot run against a graph only partially visible to the party running it. Requires that party to already hold, from some external source, at least one node identity it is certain is honest, since the whole judge-node bootstrap in the pre-processing phase is derived by walking outward from that single node — the mechanism supplies no way to establish that certainty internally. Requires the graph to remain fast-mixing on the honest side, which depends on the honest population not being fragmented into many small, weakly-interconnected communities (the paper notes but does not test this failure mode; it is the same failure mode later measured directly against a related family of methods in GAO-CNS-18). Requires that the number of attack edges stays small relative to network size for the small-cut/conductance signal to exist at all — a network that does not maintain this (as the paper's own survey suggests real online social networks may not) removes the basis for both the identification and the community-detection algorithms. Supplies, but does not itself enforce, two techniques (manual relationship rating, or restricting to an interaction-derived activity network) intended to keep the attack-edge count low; each requires either a separate user-facing rating application or an independently available interaction log, neither of which SybilDefender's core algorithms provide.

### Contradicts
None found within this corpus regarding SybilDefender's own reported figures. This paper's own re-implementation and measurement of SybilLimit produce substantially higher false-negative rates for SybilLimit (up to 85.3% at low sybils-per-attack-edge) than SybilLimit's asymptotic O(log n)-sybils-per-attack-edge claim might suggest in isolation — a case where an independent re-measurement (by a competing paper's authors) diverges sharply from the originating paper's own framing, illustrating exactly the kind of re-measurement disagreement this corpus is built to preserve; no direct textual figure from the original SybilLimit paper is quoted here to compare against, so the two cannot be set side by side numerically without retrieving SybilLimit (Yu et al., IEEE S&P 2008) directly.
