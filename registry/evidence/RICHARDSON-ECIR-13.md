## [RICHARDSON-ECIR-13] Ranked Accuracy and Unstructured Distributed Search
**Citation:** Sami Richardson, Ingemar J. Cox. "Ranked Accuracy and Unstructured Distributed Search." ECIR, 2013. Pages 171-182. DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-36973-5_15.
**Retrieved:** full text via http://www0.cs.ucl.ac.uk/staff/ingemar/Content/papers/2013/ECIR2013.pdf
**Source URL:** http://www0.cs.ucl.ac.uk/staff/ingemar/Content/papers/2013/ECIR2013.pdf
**Domain:** B

### What it does
The mechanism defines a rank-weighted correctness metric, rank-accuracy, for probably-approximately-correct (PAC) search over an unstructured peer-to-peer network, and derives a document-replication policy that raises this metric above what document-popularity-based replication alone achieves. PAC search finds documents by sending a query to z nodes sampled uniformly at random out of n total network nodes, each node holding a fixed-size local index (rho documents per node), and combining whatever matching documents those z nodes report into a top-k result list. Prior work (Cohen and Shenker) measured only rank-unaware accuracy: the fraction of an exhaustive search's result set that a constrained z-node search recovers, treating every recovered document as equally valuable regardless of its position in the ranked list. Rank-accuracy instead assigns each document d_i in the exhaustive top-k list a rank-dependent weight w_j(i), summing to 1 across the k positions, and scores a query's constrained-search result as the sum of weights of the documents it actually recovered; when every weight equals 1/k, rank-accuracy reduces to the original rank-unaware accuracy measure. The paper adopts a rank-biased-precision-style weight, W = (1-p)*p^(y-1) for a document at rank y, where p is a persistence parameter modeling the probability a user examines the next-ranked result; smaller p concentrates weight more heavily on the top-ranked positions. The replication policy computes, for each document, a weighted retrieval rate v_i that sums, over every query that returned that document in its top-k list, the query's issue rate times the document's rank weight in that query; three replication rules are then compared: uniform (equal replica count per document), rank-aware proportional (replica count proportional to v_i), rank-aware square-root (replica count proportional to the square root of v_i, echoing the square-root-optimal rank-unaware result from Cohen and Shenker), and rank-aware optimal (an integer-programming solution obtained by the same convex-optimization method a prior PhD thesis, Fu 2012, used for the rank-unaware case, with v_i substituted for plain retrieval rate).

### Measured results
Theoretical analysis (closed-form, not simulated): parameters n=10,000 nodes, rho=500 documents/node, m=47,480 distinct documents, 4,748 distinct queries following an inverse power-law query-rate distribution with exponent theta (query rate of query j proportional to j^-theta), total query volume 10,000, query fanout z=100, disjoint top-10 result lists assumed per query. Average expected rank-accuracy A was computed across theta in [0, 1.5] for three rank-biased-precision persistence values, p=0.3, 0.6, 0.9 (Figure 1). Findings: the gap between rank-aware and rank-unaware replication policies is largest at low p (heavily skewed rank weighting) and shrinks as p rises toward 0.9; the gap between replication policies also shrinks as theta rises, because a highly skewed query distribution lets a few popular queries dominate rank-accuracy regardless of policy; even at a uniform query distribution (theta=0), all three rank-aware policies (proportional, square-root, and optimal) show a rank-accuracy improvement over uniform replication, attributed to the disjoint-top-10 assumption giving documents at rank 1 systematically higher replication than documents at rank 2, etc.; the rank-aware square-root policy outperforms the rank-aware proportional policy, and the rank-aware optimal policy performs best of all three, consistent across all three p values tested. Per-query analysis at theta=0.7, p=0.6 (Figure 2) showed the rank-unaware proportional policy gave the best rank-accuracy for the handful of most popular queries but left many other queries performing worse than plain uniform replication, while the rank-aware optimal policy was the only one to keep every query's rank-accuracy above the uniform baseline. Simulation Experiment 1 (idealized, matching the theoretical assumptions exactly: query distribution known in advance, top-10 result lists disjoint, correct replication pre-applied before search): m=47,480 documents, rho=500 documents/node, n=10,000 nodes, 10,000 queries issued of which 4,748 were distinct, power-law theta=0.7, z=100 nodes per query. Average rank-accuracy, rank-unaware / rank-aware: 0.71/0.93 at p=0.3 (+31.0%), 0.71/0.83 at p=0.6 (+16.9%), 0.71/0.72 at p=0.9 (+1.4%). Simulation Experiment 2 (real query data: first 1,000,000 queries from an anonymized Yahoo! web search log, non-disjoint top-10 result sets, m=461,788 distinct documents, rho=1,500 documents/node, R=15,000,000 total network storage capacity, query-distribution knowledge and pre-applied replication still assumed): rank-unaware / rank-aware average rank-accuracy 0.72/0.86 at p=0.3 (+19.4%), 0.69/0.76 at p=0.6 (+10.1%), 0.64/0.65 at p=0.9 (+1.6%); the paper attributes the smaller gains relative to Experiment 1 to the Yahoo! query distribution being more heavily skewed toward extremely popular queries than the idealized theta=0.7 power law used in Experiment 1, despite both having theta approximately 0.7. Simulation Experiment 3 (same Yahoo! data and network parameters as Experiment 2, but no prior knowledge of the query distribution: documents start uniformly distributed and each query's returned top-10 documents are replicated onto up to 20 additional nodes according to the policy under test as queries are issued): rank-unaware / rank-aware average rank-accuracy 0.62/0.78 at p=0.3 (+25.8%), 0.58/0.69 at p=0.6 (+19.0%), 0.54/0.56 at p=0.9 (+3.7%); absolute rank-accuracy for every policy was lower than in Experiment 2 because rank-aware and rank-unaware proportional replication are only approximated as queries accumulate, rather than pre-computed exactly.

### Parameters
- n (network size): 10,000 nodes in all theoretical analysis and simulations.
- rho (per-node storage): 500 documents/node in the theoretical analysis and Experiment 1; 1,500 documents/node in Experiments 2 and 3.
- z (nodes queried per search): 100.
- p (RBP persistence parameter): 0.3, 0.6, 0.9 tested; the paper cites a prior study finding p around 0.6-0.7 approximates typical user behavior.
- theta (query power-law exponent): swept 0 to 1.5 in the theoretical analysis (Figure 1); fixed at 0.7 for the per-query analysis (Figure 2) and all three simulation experiments; the paper cites prior web-search-log studies finding theta typically between 0.7 and 1.5.
- Document collection size m: 47,480 in the theoretical analysis and Experiment 1; 461,788 (extracted from the Yahoo! log) in Experiments 2 and 3.
- Replication-onto count in Experiment 3's incremental scheme: up to 20 additional nodes per query-triggered replication event.

### Stated limitations
The paper states the analysis assumes an idealized network in which all nodes operate correctly with no malicious behavior, explicitly following the same assumption used in the original PAC search framework, and states that node failure and security threats are beyond its scope. The paper states that bandwidth and storage overhead of the search and replication mechanisms are outside its scope, following the same exclusion made in the PAC framework it builds on. Experiment 1's disjoint-top-10-result-list assumption is stated by the authors to be "a rather artificial environment"; results using real, non-disjoint result sets (Experiments 2 and 3) show smaller rank-accuracy gains. The paper states that computing the exact document distribution required by rank-aware proportional or square-root replication needs prior knowledge of the full query distribution, which the authors state may be infeasible to obtain in practice for a large-scale system with a large document collection and many queries; this is why Experiment 3 (incremental, query-triggered replication with no prior knowledge) is presented, and the authors state that improving that incremental technique is left as future work. Due to space constraints, the paper states that it does not report simulation results for the square-root and optimal rank-aware replication policies (only for proportional), though it states rank-accuracy for both can also be improved by making them rank-aware.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Rank-aware proportional and square-root replication (Section 3.2, Equations 11-12) require the system to compute, per document, the weighted retrieval rate v_i, which requires observing which query results (with their ranks) each document appeared in and at what query rate; this is the same "learn what a search found" signal square-root replication (Cohen and Shenker; Lv et al.) needs, extended here to also require the returned document's rank position, not just its identity. Experiments 1 and 2 additionally require the full query-rate distribution to be known before replication is applied; Experiment 3 relaxes this to an online scheme that instead requires each querying node to actively push copies of the top-10 documents it retrieves onto up to 20 additional randomly selected nodes after every query, so the replication mechanism needs write access to other nodes' local indexes, not only read access via search. The rank-accuracy metric itself is independent of any specific information-retrieval ranking model, but assumes the ranking model used to compute the exhaustive top-k list is identical to the one applied at each queried node; the paper states this assumption "may not always be valid" when a ranking model requires collection-wide statistics unavailable to individual peers, in which case rank-accuracy becomes an upper bound rather than an exact prediction of achievable performance.

### Contradicts
None found against other entries in this evidence corpus. The paper states an internal contrast with Cohen and Shenker's finding that square-root replication (rank-unaware) is optimal for minimizing expected search length: the rank-unaware PAC framework instead found square-root replication is not optimal under a fixed search length, with the true rank-unaware optimum given by a convex-optimization solution from a separate PhD thesis (Fu, 2012); the paper does not treat this as an error in either source, only as two different objective functions (minimizing search length versus maximizing accuracy at fixed search length) giving different optimal replication policies.

### References worth retrieving
- Cox, Fu, Hansen, "Probably Approximately Correct Search," ICTIR 2009 — foundational; defines the PAC search framework, its independence and non-disjoint-indexing assumptions, and the rank-unaware accuracy metric this paper extends.
- Cohen, Shenker, "Replication Strategies in Unstructured Peer-to-Peer Networks," SIGCOMM 2002 — foundational; source of the square-root-replication result already in this evidence corpus, cited here as the rank-unaware baseline this paper's rank-aware square-root policy is built in analogy to.
- Fu, "The Quality of Probabilistic Search in Unstructured Distributed Information Retrieval Systems," PhD thesis, University College London, 2012 — foundational; source of the convex-optimization method for the rank-unaware optimal replication policy this paper reuses (with the weighting term added) to derive its rank-aware optimal policy (Equation 14).
- Neumann, Bender, Michel, Weikum, "A reproducible benchmark for P2P retrieval," ACM Wkshp. Exp. DB, 2006 — foundational; a P2P-IR benchmark framework cited as an example of comparing distributed search results against a centralized baseline.
- Witschel, Holz, Heinrich, Teresniak, "An Evaluation Measure for Distributed Information Retrieval Systems," ECIR 2008 — competing rank-aware P2P evaluation metric (ARRR), cited as similar in purpose to this paper's rank-accuracy metric.
- Lu, Callan, "User modeling for full-text federated search in peer-to-peer networks," SIGIR 2006 — competing rank-aware P2P evaluation metric (MAOP), cited alongside ARRR as a prior rank-sensitive P2P-IR measure.
- Chapelle, Metzler, Zhang, Grinspan, "Expected reciprocal rank for graded relevance," CIKM 2009 — foundational; source of the p approximately 0.6-0.7 user-persistence estimate this paper uses to justify its RBP parameter range.
- Baeza-Yates, Gionis, Junqueira, Murdock, Silvestri, "The impact of caching on search engines," SIGIR 2007 — foundational; cited as the source for the typical 0.7-1.5 range of the query power-law exponent theta used in this paper's experiments.
