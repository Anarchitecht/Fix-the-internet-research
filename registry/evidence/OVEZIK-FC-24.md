## [OVEZIK-FC-24] SoK: A Stratified Approach to Blockchain Decentralization
**Citation:** Christina Ovezik, Dimitris Karakostas, Aggelos Kiayias. "SoK: A Stratified Approach to Blockchain Decentralization." Financial Cryptography and Data Security (FC), 2024. DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-78676-1_8.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01291
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01291
**Domain:** J

### What it does
The paper gives a methodology for locating single points of failure in a blockchain system by decomposing the system into layers, and for each layer stating which resource, held by which kind of party, must stay distributed for the system's security properties to hold.

Four properties are defined as the things centralization can put at risk: safety (all honest participants hold the same settled view of the ledger), liveness (the settled view keeps being updated as new transactions arrive), privacy (a user's actions stay dissociated from their real-world identity and unlinkable to each other), and stability (the ledger's native asset's supply and market price stay predictable, operationalized as low short-term price volatility). A single point of failure is defined as one party whose compromise by an adversary would violate one or more of these properties.

The methodology decomposes a blockchain system into eight layers, modeled on the OSI network stack: hardware (physical machines and cloud capacity running consensus software), software (which client implementations run the protocol and which wallet software manages keys), network (peer-discovery bootstrapping and inter-instance topology), consensus (who holds the power that determines block production, both owned power and power delegated to pools), tokenomics (who owns tokens, from initial distribution onward), client API (which services applications and wallets depend on to read and submit to the ledger), governance (who decides protocol changes and who funds development), and geography (a cross-cutting dimension: physical concentration by country or jurisdiction, applicable to every other layer). For each layer, the paper's methodology asks the analyst to name the resource that is the layer's basic unit (for example, hashing power on the consensus layer), name the parties that control that resource (miners), and name which of the four properties are at risk if that resource distribution becomes concentrated.

Built on this decomposition, the paper defines the Minimum Decentralization Test (MDT): a system fails the MDT if there exists any one layer in which a single legal person (an individual or an organization, treated as a unit for legal-liability purposes rather than as a count of the human beings involved) controls enough of that layer's relevant parties to violate one of the four properties on its own. The MDT is deliberately a binary layer-by-layer test rather than a single continuous decentralization score; the paper does not propose a metric that aggregates across layers into one number, and states this as future work rather than as part of the current contribution.

Case studies applying the binary MDT (not a continuous metric): fiat-backed stablecoins (Tether/USDT, USDC, Gemini Dollar, TrueUSD, Binance USD) are stated to fail the MDT at the governance layer, because in each a single legal entity is the sole party that can issue new tokens against escrowed currency and redeem them; wrapped-token bridges (Wrapped Bitcoin, custodied by BitGo; Huobi Bitcoin, custodied by Huobi) are stated to fail the MDT at the governance layer for the same reason, a single custodian being the sole party able to mint and burn the wrapped token.

### Parameters
The paper is a systematization and case-study methodology, not a parameterized experimental system; it defines no tunable protocol parameters. The one formal definition with an internal threshold is the Minimum Decentralization Test (Definition 1), whose only "parameter" is the existence quantifier over layers: the system fails if such a single controlling legal person exists in even one layer, with no numeric threshold stated for "a sufficient number of relevant parties" beyond "able to violate a property of interest" — left to case-by-case legal and technical judgment for each layer and system.

### Stated limitations
The paper states explicitly that its own contribution is a framework for analyzing decentralization, "not specific quantitative metrics," and states that identifying which specific metric to use per layer is left as future work. It states that it does not treat off-chain Layer-2 protocols (for example the Lightning Network) as a distinct layer of its own stratification, though it states the existing layers could in principle be applied to a Layer-1-plus-Layer-2 composite system, and that doing so is a promising but unexplored research direction. It states that analyzing decentralization of infrastructure the ledger stack depends on but does not itself define, such as physical network links, internet routing, or operating systems, is explicitly out of scope. It states that no aggregation methodology across layers into a single system-wide decentralization score is proposed, calling this a compelling direction for future work. It states that natural-disaster and similar exogenous physical hazards are outside the paper's scope, even though it notes such hazards could matter more when a resource is geographically concentrated. For several claims in the Bitcoin case study, the paper states explicitly that no concrete data could be found (for example, on the distribution of Bitcoin token ownership by wallet software type, and on the distribution of hashing power by specific hardware product), and reports the corresponding layer as inconclusive rather than filling the gap with an estimate.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A layer's decentralization analysis under this methodology requires that the resource being tracked at that layer be identifiable and attributable to a controlling party; the paper's own Bitcoin case study reports several layers (Bitcoin hardware-by-manufacturer share, token ownership by wallet-software type) as inconclusive precisely because no such attribution data exists, which means any later synthesis step drawing on this paper's Bitcoin figures for those specific sub-claims has no basis to cite. Applying the Minimum Decentralization Test to a component requires the analyst to first map that component's participants onto legal persons rather than technical identities or addresses, since the MDT's failure condition is specifically about legal-entity control, and the paper's own worked examples (stablecoin issuers, bridge custodians) are single named companies rather than technical roles. Any system aiming to pass the MDT at the governance layer specifically must avoid vesting sole minting, redemption, or protocol-upgrade authority in one legal entity, since every MDT-failure example the paper gives is a governance-layer single point of control. The paper's Gini-coefficient figures for token wealth are stated to be sensitive to whether multiple addresses controlled by one real-world entity are merged (which the paper's own Table 2 data does not do, since it counts by address); a synthesis step citing these figures as a measure of decentralization across people rather than across addresses would need that clustering step performed first, which this paper states was not done.

### Contradicts
None found within this corpus. No other entry in this batch measures Bitcoin's own hardware, wealth, or geography layers with figures that disagree with the ones reported here; OVEZIK-ACNS-25 (this batch) is a later paper by an overlapping author set that measures related quantities (Gini coefficient, Nakamoto coefficient, HHI) on Bitcoin's consensus and tokenomics layers using the authors' own five-year historical pipeline rather than the third-party point-in-time figures cited here, so the two papers' numbers are not directly comparable measurements of the same window and neither entry states the other's number is wrong.
