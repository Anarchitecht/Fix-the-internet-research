## [WALLEZ-USENIXSEC-23] TreeSync: Authenticated Group Management for Messaging Layer Security
**Citation:** Théophile Wallez, Jonathan Protzenko, Benjamin Beurdouche, Karthikeyan Bhargavan. "TreeSync: Authenticated Group Management for Messaging Layer Security." USENIX Security Symposium, 2023. Pages 1217-1233.
**Retrieved:** full text
**Source URL:** https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec23fall-prepub-372-wallez.pdf
**Domain:** H

### What it does
TreeSync guarantees that every honest member of a Messaging Layer Security (MLS) group holds a consistent, authenticated view of who is in the group and which cryptographic keys they hold, so that an attacker cannot silently add, remove, or move a participant without every honest member's next processed commit detecting the inconsistency. The paper identifies TreeSync as one of three independent sub-protocols composing MLS — TreeSync (group state, membership operations, consistency/integrity/authentication), TreeKEM (group key agreement, already analyzed in this corpus as WALLEZ-SP-25), and TreeDEM (message encryption) — and proves TreeSync's guarantees hold independent of whatever TreeKEM and TreeDEM do, so a broken or replaced key-agreement or encryption layer cannot undermine TreeSync's membership-authentication property, though TreeSync's guarantee is in turn a stated precondition both other layers need. Each MLS group maintains a binary tree whose non-empty leaves hold member credentials and public keys; group membership operations (add, remove, update) modify this tree, and every modification a committing member makes is bound together by a parent-hash mechanism: each internal node's hash covers the content of its child subtrees, so tampering with any node's content or position changes the hash chain up to the root and is detected by any member re-deriving it. Each leaf's credential — the cryptographic binding between a member's identity and their signing key — is issued out-of-band by a trusted Authentication Service (AS), and the tree operations rely on a separate, only mostly-trusted Delivery Service (DS) that stores and relays messages and defines one global order for group modifications; a malicious DS can withhold or selectively deliver messages (partitioning the group) but cannot forge or read them. The paper's formal specification is written in F* (a dependently typed proof-oriented programming language) at byte-level precision — covering exact message serialization, not an abstract protocol model — verified with the DY* symbolic (Dolev-Yao) protocol-analysis framework, and the specification is also directly executable, compiled through F*'s OCaml extraction into a runnable reference implementation tested against the RFC's own test vectors and against two independent industrial MLS implementations for interoperability.

### Parameters
Not a system with tunable numeric parameters — this is a formal specification and proof of a fixed protocol version (MLS draft 16, the version nearing IETF standardization at the time of writing). The one architectural choice the paper states explicitly is to materialize TreeSync and TreeKEM as two separate trees internally, "favoring clarity and readability over conciseness," a design trade-off the authors state recurs throughout the specification wherever readability was chosen over a more concise or optimized implementation.

### Stated limitations
The paper's security proof covers only TreeSync; TreeKEM and TreeDEM are formally specified (so the interaction between all three is accounted for structurally) but their own comprehensive security analysis is explicitly left as future work — the paper states "we do not analyze TreeKEM and TreeDEM in this paper." The TreeSync authentication guarantee holds only as long as enough honest, uncompromised members continue creating commits — a group with too few active honest members does not receive the guarantee, though the paper does not quantify "enough" as a numeric threshold. The proof assumes leaf credentials have already been validated and issued out-of-band by a trusted Authentication Service before a leaf enters the tree; this issuance process itself is outside what TreeSync specifies or proves. The Delivery Service is assumed only "mostly" trusted: it can withhold messages or partition the group by selective delivery, and the paper's guarantees do not cover recovery from or detection of such partitioning beyond what the underlying commit-ordering property provides. The performance comparison against mlspp and OpenMLS is stated by the authors to support only a broad, order-of-magnitude conclusion, not a precise ranking, because the three implementations use different languages and toolchains with no controlled-for confound removal. The Skype integration is described as a one-time proof-of-concept "tested and benchmarked... on small groups exchanging a handful of messages," not a scaled evaluation, and the paper reports no numeric figures from it beyond the qualitative outcome. The authors state that MLS's growth into "a large protocol standard" makes even understanding the full protocol "a challenge... even for cryptographic developers and protocol experts," and suggest — as their own retrospective judgment, not a measured fact — that TreeSync/TreeKEM/TreeDEM's modular separation should have been built into the standard from the start.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Requires a trusted Authentication Service (AS) external to TreeSync that attests to the binding between a member's real-world identity and their authentication credential, issued out-of-band before that member's leaf enters the tree — TreeSync's proof assumes this binding already holds and does not itself establish it. Requires a Delivery Service (DS) that stores and relays messages between participants and enforces one globally unique order for all group modifications; the DS need not be fully trusted (it may withhold or selectively deliver messages) but must not be able to forge or read message content, and TreeSync's guarantees depend on this ordering property being supplied by the DS rather than by TreeSync itself. Requires TreeDEM (or whatever protocol occupies that role) to use signature formats disambiguated by purpose-specific labels from any signatures TreeSync itself produces under the same signing key — the paper's own authentication proof could not complete until this labeling fix (adopted in MLS draft 13) was in place, so any composition reusing a signing key across sub-protocols needs an equivalent disambiguation mechanism. Requires every honest group member to re-derive and check the parent-hash chain on receiving a commit, since the integrity guarantee is enforced by members performing this check, not by any property the delivery mechanism verifies on their behalf. States as a structural fact — not merely an assumption of this paper but something the paper proves as a dependency — that TreeSync's authentication guarantee is "a necessary precondition" for both TreeKEM and TreeDEM, because both layers rely on group members agreeing on the tree; a system substituting a different, weaker membership-authentication layer beneath a TreeKEM-like key-agreement mechanism inherits this same precondition and needs an equivalent guarantee from whatever layer replaces TreeSync.

### Contradicts
None found regarding this paper's own measured results. The paper cites Unger et al. (already in this corpus as UNGER-SP-15) as concluding that, as of that 2015 survey, "conversations between larger groups still lack a good solution" — this is quoted here as an attribution of Unger et al.'s own stated conclusion, not a finding of this paper, and is consistent with, not contradicted by, this paper's own scope (TreeSync addresses group membership authentication specifically, not the full space of group-conversation-security properties Unger et al. survey).

- Attack/critique: reference [5]'s "tree signing attack," where an attacker changes leaf positions in the tree to fool a new member — a second named prior attack this paper's integrity theorem is stated to prevent.
