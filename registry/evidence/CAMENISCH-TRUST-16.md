## [CAMENISCH-TRUST-16] Anonymous Attestation Using the Strong Diffie-Hellman Assumption Revisited
**Citation:** Jan Camenisch, Manu Drijvers, Anja Lehmann. "Anonymous Attestation Using the Strong Diffie-Hellman Assumption Revisited." International Conference on Trust and Trustworthy Computing (TRUST), 2016. DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-45572-3_1.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://eprint.iacr.org/2016/663.pdf
**Source URL:** https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-45572-3_1
**Domain:** E

### What it does
Direct Anonymous Attestation (DAA) lets a platform — a secure hardware element (a Trusted Platform Module, TPM) paired with a host computer — sign a message so a verifier is convinced an authorized secure element produced the signature, without the signature revealing which specific platform signed. This paper identifies proof errors in the qSDH-assumption-based (q-Strong Diffie-Hellman) DAA schemes standardized in TPM 2.0 and ISO, and constructs a repaired scheme.

The paper states three flaws in prior qSDH-based DAA schemes. First, the most efficient prior schemes use a simplified membership credential that is not proven existentially unforgeable under adaptive chosen-message attack, because their security proof issues a specially crafted credential only to a randomly chosen honest platform and then incorrectly assumes any forgery reusing part of an issued credential has a 1/q chance of reusing that specific one — a claim the paper states is false when no honest platform joins or when the adversary disregards credentials issued to honest platforms. Second, the zero-knowledge proof-of-knowledge these schemes use to prove possession of a credential proves knowledge of secret values a, b and the platform secret gsk, but never proves the required relation b = a * gsk holds between them, so a signature that passes verification cannot always be used to extract a genuinely valid credential — which the paper states blocks any unforgeability proof. Third, every existing security proof for these schemes uses an isomorphism from G2 to G1 (present only in type-1 and type-2 pairing settings), but the TPM 2.0 standard is deployed using type-3 pairings, where no such isomorphism exists, leaving the standardized scheme's security unproven in its actual deployment setting. The paper separately reports that Chen and Urian's LRSW-based DAA-with-attributes instantiation is forgeable outright by the trivial credential A=B=C=D=E_1=...=E_L=1 (the group identity element), which signs any attribute set.

The paper's repaired scheme, Pi_daa+, uses a BBS+ signature (a signature scheme on a vector of messages, introduced by Au, Susilo, Mu, SCN 2006) as the membership credential, and gives a new proof of knowledge of a BBS+ signature that runs entirely in group G1, avoiding the less efficient G2 and GT groups and their pairing operations during proof generation; verification needs two pairing operations, with the remainder of the computation in G1. As a side result the paper proves the BBS+ signature scheme itself existentially unforgeable under adaptive chosen-message attack in the type-3 pairing setting, without assuming an isomorphism between G2 and G1 — a proof the paper states did not previously exist, since the original BBS+ proof by Au et al. used the Eurocrypt version of the qSDH assumption, which does assume that isomorphism. The scheme adds support for signature-based revocation (revoking a platform from a prior signature, without knowing its secret key) via mandatory basenames — a per-verifier-context value B = H1(bsn) that the platform's pseudonym nym = B^gsk is computed from — required because a revocation-inequality check performed jointly between a corrupt host and its TPM could otherwise let the host learn B'^gsk for an adversarially chosen B', turning the TPM into a static Diffie-Hellman oracle; requiring bsn to be a hash of a public per-context string removes that leak in the random-oracle model. The scheme also supports attribute-carrying credentials, storing every attribute except the platform's own secret key on the host rather than the TPM, for efficiency, without weakening unforgeability under an honest-TPM, corrupt-host threat model.

### Parameters
- Pairing type: type-3 (G1 not equal to G2, no efficient isomorphism between them), chosen because it allows the most efficient operations in G1 among the three pairing types the paper cites (type-1, type-2, type-3).
- Curve: 256-bit Barreto-Naehrig curve, the one specified by the TPM 2.0 standard, used for all benchmark figures above.
- L, D, U: total attribute count, disclosed-attribute count, and undisclosed-attribute count in a credential; set to zero in the efficiency comparison to isolate the base-scheme cost from attribute handling.
- Join-session concurrency bound: when the scheme uses extraction-by-rewinding instead of online-extractable proofs, the paper states the issuer must limit the number of simultaneous join sessions to be logarithmic in the security parameter, to avoid an exponential blow-up in the security proof's rewinding argument; no concrete bound is given, only the asymptotic requirement.

### Stated limitations
The paper states it remains future work to revisit the standards it identifies flaws in (TPM 2.0's DAA schemes) to eliminate those flaws and establish provable security in deployment. The fully anonymous signing option (an empty basename, bsn = bottom) supported by prior DAA schemes is not supported by this scheme, because it is required for the mandatory-basename fix that closes the static-Diffie-Hellman-oracle leak during revocation checks; the paper argues this does not reduce achievable privacy, since a platform can instead choose a basename it uses only once to obtain the same anonymity property. Extraction by rewinding, an alternative to the scheme's online-extractable proofs that would be more computationally efficient, is stated to require the issuer to cap simultaneous join sessions at a logarithmic bound in the security parameter to keep the security proof's running time from blowing up exponentially, and the paper states this instantiation's security proof holds only in a stand-alone setting, not under the universal-composability framework's composability guarantees, because that framework does not permit rewinding at all.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A verifier checking a non-revocation proof must supply a fixed, application-specific basename bsn for every verification context, since the platform's pseudonym is computed as a hash of that basename raised to the platform's secret key; a system that omits basenames (fully anonymous verification) is not supported by this scheme and must instead have platforms generate a single-use basename per signature to obtain equivalent anonymity. Signature-based revocation requires every verifier to hold the current revocation list of prior signatures and requires the platform, during signing, to prove in zero knowledge that its own secret key differs from the key underlying every revoked signature; this proof must be re-run by the platform for every entry on the revocation list at signing time, so the revocation list's size directly bounds signing cost, though the paper gives no measured figure for this cost as a function of list size. The DAA credential-issuing party (the issuer) must generate a BBS+ key pair honestly and issue credentials through the paper's Join protocol; the security proof assumes static corruption, meaning the adversary's set of corrupted parties is fixed before the protocol run begins, not adaptively during it. Attribute-carrying deployments must place every attribute except the platform's own secret key on the host rather than the TPM for the stated efficiency gain; the paper states this preserves unforgeability under an honest TPM with a corrupt host, but a system whose threat model instead assumes a corrupt TPM with an honest host would need a different attribute placement, which the paper does not analyze.

### Contradicts
The paper does not itself introduce the BBS+ signature scheme; it cites Au, Susilo, Mu (ASM06, SCN 2006) as the source of BBS+ and states its own contribution is a new proof that BBS+ is existentially unforgeable specifically in the type-3 pairing setting, without assuming an isomorphism between G2 and G1 — the paper labels this "a side-result of independent interest" within a paper whose primary construction is the DAA scheme Pi_daa+. A synthesis step citing this source as the origin of BBS+ itself, rather than as the source of its type-3 security proof, is not supported by this paper's own framing.

### References worth retrieving
- Au, Susilo, Mu, "Constant-size dynamic k-TAA," SCN 2006 — foundational (the original BBS+ signature scheme construction and its type-2 security proof, which this paper extends to type-3).
- Camenisch, Drijvers, Lehmann, "Universally composable direct anonymous attestation," (cited as CDL16b) — foundational (the security model, including its ideal functionality, that this paper extends to support attributes and signature-based revocation).
- Brickell, Camenisch, Chen, "Direct anonymous attestation," (BCC04) — foundational (the original DAA scheme designed for the TPM, based on strong-RSA, that this line of qSDH-based work replaces for efficiency).
- Brickell, Li, "Enhanced privacy ID from bilinear pairing," (BL11) — competing (the EPID scheme this paper's revocation extension is compared against, including the static-Diffie-Hellman-oracle leak the paper's basename requirement is designed to close).
- Chen, Urian, "DAA-A: direct anonymous attestation with attributes," (CU15) — competing (the prior attribute-carrying DAA scheme this paper both compares against in Table 1/2 and reports a forgery attack against, via the trivial all-identity-element credential).
- Chen, Plessl, Steedman, (CPS10) — competing (an LRSW-based DAA scheme with faster host-signing than this paper's qSDH-based scheme, per Table 1).
