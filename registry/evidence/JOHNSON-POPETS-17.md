## [JOHNSON-POPETS-17] PeerFlow: Secure Load Balancing in Tor
**Citation:** Aaron Johnson, Rob Jansen, Nicholas Hopper, Aaron Segal, Paul Syverson. "PeerFlow: Secure Load Balancing in Tor." Proceedings on Privacy Enhancing Technologies (PoPETs), 2017(2):74-94. DOI 10.1515/popets-2017-0017.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://petsymposium.org/popets/2017/popets-2017-0017.pdf
**Source URL:** https://petsymposium.org/popets/2017/popets-2017-0017.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
PeerFlow assigns each relay in the Tor anonymity network a voting weight proportional to the traffic it actually carries, so that Tor clients route traffic across relays roughly in proportion to real capacity — the load-balancing function — while bounding how much a dishonest relay can inflate its own weight relative to the traffic it truly relays, which is the security function. Tor clients pick relays for each three-hop circuit (guard, middle, exit) with a probability proportional to the relay's consensus weight; a relay carrying a disproportionate share of circuits gains a disproportionate ability to observe or manipulate client traffic and, when it colludes with the other relays on a circuit, to deanonymize the client with a first-last correlation attack. PeerFlow computes weight from peer measurement rather than either self-report or the centralized probe design TorFlow uses: a subset of relays (the largest fraction `µ` of relays by capacity in each circuit position, `µ=0.75`), called measuring relays, each keep an application-layer byte count of traffic exchanged with every other relay while occupying that position, over a measurement period whose length depends on the measured relay's inferred bandwidth. A relay's inferred total traffic `ρ` is derived from these peer byte-counts and reported to the Directory Authorities as an estimate, with two protections against manipulation: (1) a "liar" check comparing a relay's self-reported capacity against what its peers measured, trimming the fraction `λ` of highest-disagreement measurements; (2) calibrated Laplace-distributed noise (differential privacy, parameters `δnoise`, `ϵnoise`) added to protect the link-level privacy of which relay pairs exchanged how much traffic, since raw peer measurements would otherwise reveal circuit-level traffic patterns. A relay whose measured traffic falls below its previously-inferred capacity beyond a threshold (`ϵdec`) is placed in "probation," a weight-reduction penalty, unless it sends compensating dummy traffic. Weight changes are further bounded across successive voting periods by a maximum per-period increase factor `ϵinc = 0.25`, limiting how fast an adversary can ramp up an inflated weight even if a single measurement period is gamed.

### Measured results
Three separate empirical studies, each with its own setup:

**TorFlow liar and selective-DoS attack (Table 1).** Implemented in Tor and the authors' own Shadow discrete-event network simulator plug-in (2128 lines of code) modeling TorFlow's Python bandwidth-scanning scripts, on a private simulated Tor network of 498 relays, 4 directory authorities, 7500 clients, and 1 bandwidth authority. One arbitrarily chosen exit relay (300 Mbit/s access link) ran 3 baseline (honest) trials and 6 attack trials combining a liar attack (falsely reporting 125,000 KB bandwidth) with a selective denial-of-service attack (forwarding traffic only for TorFlow's known measurement circuits, dropping all other traffic). Median goodput fell from 22.5 MiB/s (baseline) to 0.2 MiB/s (attack) while median consensus weight share rose from 7% to 11% — a measured bandwidth-inflation factor of 177 (ratio of consensus-weight-per-bandwidth-unit between attack and baseline conditions).

**EigenSpeed framing attacks (Tables 2 and 3), analytical, using the real Tor consensus of 2015-04-31 23:00 (5589 relays with positive selection probability) as input data, not a live or simulated network run.** In the "increase framing" attack (Table 2), varying the number of trusted relays from 280 (5% of honest relays) to 1677 (30%), an adversary using at most 3% of total network bandwidth could push each targeted honest relay's "increase metric" above 0.2 (exceeding EigenSpeed's suggested threshold `∆=0.1`), framing between 112 relays (at 30% trusted) and 1118 relays (at 10-20% trusted) using between 447 and 558 added adversarial relays. In the "targeted liar" attack (Table 3), reporting a false bandwidth 15 to 100 times the honest load-balanced rate let malicious relays obtain 7.4x to 28.1x weight inflation while keeping their liar and increase metrics below the honest non-trusted relays' own values, reaching adversary weight shares of 48.5% to 98.2% of the network across the four trusted-relay-fraction settings tested.

**PeerFlow load-balancing evaluation (Shadow simulation), a private Tor network configuration built from Tor Metrics 2014-09 data at approximately 1/12 scale: 4 directory authorities, 498 relays, 7,500 clients, 1,000 servers, run for 5 virtual hours per experiment (first 2 hours discarded as bootstrapping), 3 repeated experiments per model, comparing an Ideal model (fixed weights set to true configured capacity), the authors' TorFlow simulator plug-in, and a PeerFlow prototype (1222 lines of code, forked from Tor 0.2.5.10, without probation implemented).**

**PeerFlow speed and efficiency, using CollecTor historical Tor network data from the January 31, 2015, 00:00 consensus and January 2015 extra-info descriptors, 5917 relays after excluding those with zero selection probability or incomplete byte histories.** At `µ=0.75`, this yields 400 measuring guards, 749 measuring middles, and 145 measuring exits. Estimated measurement-time distribution across relays weighted by capacity: 25th percentile 11.5 hours, 50th percentile 27.7 hours, 75th percentile 70.7 hours; 96.8% of capacity-weighted relays measured within 14 days, with the remainder (low-capacity relays) requiring longer, reducible to a maximum of 316.4 hours (13.2 days) by raising the minimum bandwidth thresholds for Fast (100 KB/s to 250 KB/s) and Guard (250 KB/s to 625 KB/s) flags by 150%. Reporting bandwidth overhead to Directory Authorities: 119.6 bytes/second average per Bandwidth Authority (using the same January 2015 consensus, 4-byte statistics, `O(mn)` total traffic for `m` measuring and `n` measured relays). Dummy-traffic overhead from probation avoidance, from 3 Shadow runs per `ϵdec` setting on the 498-relay network described above: at `ϵdec=0.05` (trusted fraction `τ=0.05`), median 14 and maximum 75 relays in probation per second, averaging 2.38 KiB/s (SD 0.677) total dummy traffic across the network; at `ϵdec=0.95` (`τ=0.30`), median and maximum 1 and 10 relays, averaging 0.00328 KiB/s (SD 0.00385) — small relative to the 428.0 MiB/s median network-wide PeerFlow goodput measured in the load-balancing evaluation above.

**PeerFlow's proven security bound (Theorem 1, analytical, not measured):** for an adversary controlling voting-weight fraction `α` below trimming threshold `λ=0.256`, with measuring-relay weight fraction `µ=0.75`, per-period increase cap `ϵinc=0.25`, and noise-loss parameter `ϵloss=0.01`, the worked numerical example gives an advantage factor `γ=4.52` at `α=0` (an adversary's relative consensus weight share can exceed its relative true-traffic share by at most this factor, or else it is pushed into probation). The paper states achieving even this bounded factor requires the adversary to either send traffic in only one direction (client-side or destination-side only, without relaying any real Tor traffic — "highly observable") or to concentrate its traffic exchange on a minority-weight subset of measuring relays (also observable to Directory Authorities and clients).

### Parameters
- `µ` (measuring-relay population fraction): 0.75, used throughout the deployed-scale analysis.
- `λ` (trim fraction for disagreeing measurements): 0.256, used in the Theorem 1 numerical example.
- `ϵinc` (max per-period weight-increase fraction): 0.25.
- `ϵdec` (probation-trigger decrease threshold): varied experimentally at 0.05, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.95 (Table 6); paired in the text with trusted-fraction values `τ` from 0.05 (giving `ϵdec=0.90`) to 0.30 (giving `ϵdec=0.2`) for `µ=0.75`.
- `δnoise` (traffic amount covered by differential privacy): 1 MiB.
- `ϵnoise` (differential privacy guarantee): 0.1.
- `ϵerr` (targeted fraction of traffic added as noise): 0.1.
- `qerr` (probability noise exceeds targeted fraction): 0.1.
- `ϵloss` (accuracy loss from taking the max of noisy values): 0.01.
- `τ` (trusted relay weight fraction per position), used in the EigenSpeed attack analysis at 5%, 10%, 20%, 30% of honest relays.
- EigenSpeed's own suggested thresholds, evaluated (not chosen by this paper): liar-metric threshold `L=10^-5`, increase-metric threshold `∆=0.1`; the paper's attacks push both metrics past these suggested values.

### Stated limitations
The bounded advantage factor `γ` in Theorem 1 is a worst-case bound the adversary can approach only by adopting an attack pattern the paper states is "highly observable" (one-sided traffic with no real relaying, or traffic concentrated on a minority-weight subset of measuring relays) — the paper does not claim these observable patterns go undetected, only that the mechanism does not itself block them beyond the stated bound. Probation was not implemented in the PeerFlow prototype used for the load-balancing (§7) evaluation; the authors measured only when probation would trigger and the compensating dummy-traffic cost separately (§8.2), so the load-balancing goodput and download-time results do not include probation's effect on relay behavior. The private Shadow network used for evaluation is built at approximately 1/12 the scale of the public Tor network from a single month's data (2014-09), and aggregate goodput distributions varied enough across repeated runs of the same model that the maximum Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic reached 0.393 for that specific metric, the paper's own signal of higher variance for that measurement. The paper explicitly declines to benchmark EigenSpeed in the load-balancing comparison, citing that it is not deployed in practice and that the vulnerabilities detailed in the paper's own §4 make its future adoption unlikely — this is the paper's stated reason, not an independently measured deployment fact. The measurement-time analysis (§8.1) is a projection from historical byte-history data onto PeerFlow's measurement-period procedure, not a live deployment measurement — relays with low observed capacity (below Tor's stated Fast and Guard bandwidth flag thresholds) have measurement times exceeding 14 days under the analyzed parameters, addressed only by proposing (not deploying) a 150% increase to those flag thresholds. The authors state possible future improvements to PeerFlow include improving scalability and further reducing the adversary's residual ability to inflate weight, implying the current bound is not considered final by the authors themselves.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
PeerFlow requires a functioning Directory Authority infrastructure that collects, trims, and aggregates peer-submitted measurement statistics into consensus weights, and that runs the probation and per-period weight-increase-cap logic — a design with no analogous centralized aggregation point cannot apply this mechanism as specified. It requires each relay in the "measuring" subset (the largest `µ` fraction by capacity per circuit position) to keep application-layer byte counts of every other relay it directly interacts with while occupying guard, middle, or exit position, which requires the transport layer to expose per-peer, per-position byte accounting at the relay — a relay implementation lacking this instrumentation cannot supply PeerFlow's measurement input. Peer measurements themselves must be treated as privacy-sensitive: raw measurement data would reveal which relay pairs exchanged how much traffic, so any composition of this mechanism with a client-anonymity requirement needs its own differential-privacy noise-injection step (parameterized by `δnoise`, `ϵnoise`) before publishing aggregated statistics — omitting this step reopens a traffic-pattern side channel. The security bound (Theorem 1) is stated only for an adversary whose voting-weight fraction stays below the trim threshold `λ`; a design that lets a single adversarial coalition exceed `λ` loses the bound "even without any trusted relays," per the paper's own statement, meaning some other component (for example a Sybil-resistance mechanism controlling how much aggregate weight one identity can acquire) must keep any single adversarial coalition under `λ` for this bound to hold at all. The mechanism assumes voting-weight periods and measurement periods are tracked and enforced consistently across all Directory Authorities and relays — the per-period increase cap `ϵinc` and the probation state both depend on comparing a relay's current-period statistics against its own prior-period state, requiring durable per-relay history, not just current statistics.

### Contradicts
This paper's measured attack against TorFlow (177x bandwidth inflation factor, Table 1) directly supports and quantifies observations made by Bauer et al. [7] and Thill [31] that TorFlow's design is vulnerable to relays recognizing and preferentially serving measurement circuits — the paper states it "confirms this observation by implementing and experimentally testing the attacks," rather than contradicting prior work. No other paper in this corpus was found to report Tor bandwidth-measurement or EigenSpeed figures to compare against. This paper is the direct evidentiary source for the honest-bandwidth-reporting attack surface that BRIEF.md's open-problem list (item 10) identifies as unaddressed by HSkip+'s bandwidth-ordering assumption: PeerFlow's peer-measurement design is one published mitigation for that attack surface, bounding (not eliminating) an adversary's achievable weight inflation via the factor `γ` under the stated conditions, rather than assuming honest self-report as HSkip+ does.
