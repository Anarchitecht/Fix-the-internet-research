## [DICURSI-BIGDATA-24] A Herd of Young Mastodonts: the User-Centered Footprints of Newcomers After Twitter Acquisition

**Citation:** Francesco Di Cursi, Chiara Boldrini, Andrea Passarella, Marco Conti. "A Herd of Young Mastodonts: the User-Centered Footprints of Newcomers After Twitter Acquisition." IEEE International Conference on Big Data, 2024. DOI 10.1109/BigData62323.2024.10825038.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16383
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16383
**Domain:** J

### Scope note

The registry record for this key states its purpose as measuring "where a large wave of new users actually settled inside the fediverse's instance topology" after the Twitter exit event. The retrieved full text does not measure this. Its own data collection is explicitly instance-agnostic: a snowball sample of 2,000 Mastodon users and their contacts, gathered by following user-to-user reply, mention, and boost interactions starting from one seed account, without tracking or reporting which Mastodon instance (server) each user belongs to. The paper's actual subject is whether Mastodon users who joined after the Twitter acquisition form Dunbar-model ego networks (personal social circles of bounded size, seen in offline human relationships and in prior centralized-platform studies), not how newcomers distributed themselves across instances. The instance-redistribution question the registry record describes is covered by three other papers this paper cites in its related-work section (He et al., Raman et al., Zignani et al., listed below); their instance-level findings are attributed to them, not measured by this paper, and are not independently verified full-text evidence in this entry.

### What it does

The paper tests whether Mastodon (a federated Twitter-like microblogging network, part of the "Fediverse" of platforms built on the ActivityPub protocol) users who joined after Elon Musk's acquisition of Twitter form personal social circles matching Dunbar's ego network model, the same layered structure of relationship intimacy previously found in offline human relationships and in centralized platforms such as Facebook and Twitter. An ego network is built around one user (the ego) and the other users that ego communicates with directly (alters), grouped into nested circles by contact frequency: from a closest "super-support clique" contacted about every 5 days, out through wider circles to an "active network" of roughly 150 people contacted at least once a year, the number known as Dunbar's number. The authors collected 2,000 Mastodon users and their direct communications (replies, mentions, and boosts, i.e. reposts) through snowball sampling via the public Mastodon API, starting from one long-active user on the main instance mastodon.social, then applied a Meanshift clustering algorithm (a non-parametric method that groups data points by iteratively moving them toward the nearest region of high density) to each user's annual contact frequencies with their alters, to find how many social circles that user's contacts naturally group into and how large each circle is.

### Parameters

- Snowball sample cap: 2,000 collected users, starting from one seed user active since 2016-2017 on mastodon.social.
- Alter-inclusion filter: an alter-ego relationship is retained only if it started more than 6 months before the observation end date (2023-12-31), the pair had at least 2 contacts, and the annual contact frequency exceeds 1 (i.e., more than once a year).
- Observation end date fixed at 2023-12-31 for all collected timelines, regardless of collection date (January-March 2024).
- Meanshift clustering bandwidth: not given a fixed numeric value in the paper; described as automatically optimized by standard library tooling (cited as scikit-learn) rather than set manually.
- User-type classification threshold: users are split into three categories by whether their activity falls before, after, or on both sides of the Twitter acquisition date; the "Others2" (post-acquisition only) group, 76.74% of the sample, is the one used for the ego-network analysis in Section VII.

### Stated limitations

The authors state their sample's ego networks are smaller than Dunbar's canonical model predicts (median 50 active alters against a canonical ~150), and attribute this to the short observation window of about 1.5 years rather than to a structural difference from offline or other online social networks, since 6-circle egos in their data do reach the canonical ~150 figure. They state the scaling ratio between the two outermost circles is higher than the canonical ratio found closer to the ego, and attribute this specifically to the short observation window making the lowest-frequency (about once-a-year) outermost relationships hard to characterize reliably in this time frame. They state single-contact alters are discarded because a contact frequency cannot be reliably computed from one observed interaction, which the paper does not quantify as a fraction of the discarded population. They state the study is a single snapshot and plan to replicate it later to test whether circle sizes converge toward the canonical values as the platform and its users' relationships mature, and to extend the analysis to Bluesky, a separate decentralized social network. They state that not all users who join after a migration event remain active, observing that user growth outpaces "alive"-user growth throughout the post-acquisition period.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system

None as a positive system mechanism — this is a measurement and social-network-structure study, not a protocol proposal. The methodological requirement worth noting for anyone using this paper's figures: the ego-network circle-size results are drawn only from the post-acquisition-only user subgroup (1,534 of 1,999 collected users) after the 6-month-duration and 2-contact-minimum filters, so the reported mean of 153.53 active alters and the per-circle sizes in Tables V-IX describe that filtered subgroup, not the full unfiltered dataset (whose unfiltered mean is 434.69).
