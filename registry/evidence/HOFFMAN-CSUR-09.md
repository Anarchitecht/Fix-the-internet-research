## [HOFFMAN-CSUR-09] A Survey of Attack and Defense Techniques for Reputation Systems
**Citation:** Kevin Hoffman, David Zage, Cristina Nita-Rotaru. "A Survey of Attack and Defense Techniques for Reputation Systems." ACM Computing Surveys, 2009. DOI 10.1145/1592451.1592452.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://cnitarot.github.io/papers/p2p-reputation-survey.pdf
**Source URL:** https://cnitarot.github.io/papers/p2p-reputation-survey.pdf
**Domain:** F

### What it does
The paper provides a common vocabulary for comparing published reputation systems, so that a reader can identify which design choice in a given system permits which attack, without re-deriving the analysis for each new system independently. It decomposes every reputation system into three dimensions: formulation (the abstract specification of how raw observations turn into a reputation metric — its information sources, information type, and the metric itself), calculation (the concrete algorithm computing the metric value, characterized by structure, determinism, and computational efficiency), and dissemination (how computed values reach the parties that need them, characterized by structure, determinism, storage durability, and redundancy). Reputation-system input falls into four source types: manual feedback, automatic indirect observation, automatic direct observation, and inferred data propagated from other identities' observations.

The paper classifies attacks into five categories by which goal of the reputation system the attack defeats. Self-promoting attacks falsely raise the attacker's own reputation, exploiting formulation dimensions that accept positive feedback without verifying it corresponds to a real interaction. Whitewashing attacks let an attacker who has damaged its reputation escape the consequence and restart with a fresh or repaired reputation, exploiting the formulation's failure to distinguish newcomers from previously misbehaving identities. Slandering attacks report false negative feedback to lower another identity's reputation, exploiting formulation dimensions that lack feedback authentication or that weight negative feedback heavily. Orchestrated attacks combine several of the above strategies across a coalition of colluding identities that vary their behavior over time (the paper cites the oscillation attack, where colluders split into teams that alternate between honest and dishonest behavior to sustain average reputation while extracting the benefit of misbehavior). Denial-of-service attacks target the calculation or dissemination dimensions directly, for example by overloading a centralized calculation node or exploiting an underlying distributed hash table's own vulnerabilities.

For each attack category the paper pairs the exploited component with a corresponding defense family: mechanisms limiting the acquisition of multiple identities (centralized certificate issuance, IP-address-to-key binding, network coordinates, web-of-trust propagation, or social-network-based limits) address self-promoting and Sybil-enabled attacks; direct-observation verification and pre-trusted identity sets address formulation manipulation generally; redundancy and randomization in calculation and dissemination address denial of service; and irrefutable cryptographic proofs of interaction address slandering and fabricated feedback.

### Measured results
This is a survey; it reports no original experiments. Its factual content is a structured comparison of 24 published reputation systems (Beth 94, Zimmermann 95, eBay 96, Yu 00, P-Grid 01, CORE 02, XRep 02, EigenTrust 03, Lee 03, TrustMe 03, Xiong 03, Buchegger 04, Feldman 04, Guha 04, Marti 04, ARA 05, Scrivener 05, Song 05, TrustGuard 05, Xiong 05, Credence 06, PowerTrust 06, P2PRep 06, Li 07) along two paired tables: one recording each system's formulation, calculation, and dissemination design choices (including the asymptotic computational cost to calculate the reputation metric for a single identity — values ranging from O(1) for Buchegger 04 and Scrivener 05 to O(n^2) for Guha 04 and Xiong 05, where n is the number of nodes), and one recording, on an ordinal scale (none, minimal, partial, full), each system's susceptibility to each of the five attack categories against the defense mechanisms it employs. EigenTrust is stated to reduce its per-identity calculation cost from O(n) to O(log n) without loss of accuracy by relying on pre-trusted identities, a specific claim traced to its own source (Kamvar et al. 2003) rather than independently re-derived here.

### Parameters
Not applicable — the paper defines an analysis framework and taxonomy, not a mechanism with tunable parameters of its own. Individual systems' parameters (e.g., EigenTrust's pre-trusted identity set) are described qualitatively within the framework rather than with specific numeric values the survey itself sets or varies.

### Stated limitations
The paper states that none of the 24 surveyed reputation systems defends against all five attack categories, and that most existing techniques address selfish rational attackers plus only a limited subset of coalition-based malicious attacks. The attacker model adopted treats all attackers as insiders (entities with legitimate, authenticated access to the system) on the stated grounds that reputation systems push trust decisions to network edges, where end-node compromise is common, and this scoping choice is stated explicitly rather than derived — outsider (unauthenticated) attackers are outside the paper's classification. The paper states that identifying orchestrated attacks is difficult because colluders performing different roles at different times may show no direct observable interaction within any given time window, so clique-detection approaches over an identity-interaction graph can miss coordinated collusion that is only indirectly observable. On formulation sensitivity to negative feedback, the paper states an inherent trade-off it does not resolve: lower sensitivity resists slander from malicious collectives but tolerates prolonged bad behavior for the same reputation decrease, while higher sensitivity punishes bad behavior faster but exposes honest identities to more effective slander — stated as a property of the design space, without recommending a specific point on this trade-off. Reputation-based systems that compute trust exclusively from direct information limit exposure to slander but cannot function in systems with sparse pairwise interaction, where the paper states trust inference (using indirect, propagated observations) becomes necessary and must itself be made robust to attack.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A reputation system built using this framework requires an authentication mechanism external to the reputation logic itself to bind feedback to the identity that produced it; the paper states systems lacking data authentication and integrity checks cannot distinguish fabricated from legitimate feedback and are vulnerable to self-promotion and slandering as a direct consequence. Systems relying on direct-information-only formulations to resist slander require that interactions between identities be frequent enough to produce sufficient direct observations; below that density, the system must fall back to indirect, inferred trust data, which reintroduces exposure to propagated false information. Distributed calculation and dissemination approaches that use redundancy to resist denial of service require an underlying storage or routing layer (the paper cites DHT-based storage as one option) that itself is robust, since the paper explicitly notes DHTs carry their own vulnerabilities that an attacker can exploit to deny service to the reputation layer built on top. Systems using pre-trusted identity sets (the paper cites EigenTrust) require that this set be established and its members remain trustworthy externally to the reputation calculation itself, since the calculation's probabilistic convergence guarantee depends on treating pre-trusted identities as fixed anchors.

### Contradicts
None found within this corpus. This paper is repeatedly cited elsewhere in Sybil- and reputation-defense literature as the canonical component/attack taxonomy; nothing in its text claims a quantitative result that a target-corpus paper here disputes.
