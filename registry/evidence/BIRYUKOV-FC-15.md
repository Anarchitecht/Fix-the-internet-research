## [BIRYUKOV-FC-15] Proof-of-Work as Anonymous Micropayment: Rewarding a Tor Relay
**Citation:** Alex Biryukov, Ivan Pustogarov. "Proof-of-Work as Anonymous Micropayment: Rewarding a Tor Relay." Financial Cryptography and Data Security, 2015 (this file is the authors' extended version of the FC'15 paper). DOI: 10.1007/978-3-662-47854-7_27.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://eprint.iacr.org/2014/1011.pdf
**Source URL:** https://eprint.iacr.org/2014/1011.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
The scheme rewards a Tor relay operator for providing improved service, using client-submitted proof-of-work as the unit exchanged instead of a measured-bandwidth currency or direct monetary payment. A relay operator selects a crypto-currency mining pool and a compatible proof-of-work algorithm, generates a ticket-signing key pair separate from its Tor identity keys, and publishes this choice in its relay descriptor. A client who wants improved service opens a circuit to that relay and sends a SUBSCRIBE message; the relay replies with a mining job (algorithm, per-share difficulty, job data). The client mines a share at that difficulty, generates a random value x and its hash H(x), and sends the share plus H(x) to the relay. The relay checks the share and forwards it to the mining pool, which credits the relay with a fraction of pool earnings once enough shares accumulate to solve a block at the pool's full difficulty. The relay then issues the client a partially blind signature over H(x), with the current calendar date as an included but non-blinded attribute; the tuple (signature, date, x, H(x)) is a priority ticket. The client later presents the ticket to the same relay (over the same anonymous circuit) to receive prioritized traffic scheduling for a fixed duration; the relay stores spent-ticket state only long enough for tickets to expire (the paper suggests 48 hours) to bound that state. Priority scheduling is implemented with a Hierarchical Token Bucket: bandwidth is split into a paid class and a free class in a tree, each class has a guaranteed rate and a ceiling rate it may temporarily borrow up to from unused sibling-class capacity, and higher-priority classes borrow first — a class with tickets can use its full ceiling when no other ticket-holding client contends, while the free class always retains its guaranteed floor rate. Because the client never transmits fiat or crypto-currency value directly to the relay, the relay's bandwidth-measurement mechanism is not part of the reward path — the paper's design goal states the scheme deliberately avoids any dependency on a secure bandwidth-measurement mechanism.

### Measured results
The paper does not run a network deployment or simulation; its numeric results are an analytical profitability calculation from measured mining-hardware hash rates and observed crypto-currency market prices, not a systems evaluation.

### Parameters
Ticket validity/expiry: suggested 48 hours, to bound the size of the relay's spent-ticket record. Ticket date granularity: full calendar day, so that all tickets issued on the same day by a relay are mutually indistinguishable. Priority duration per ticket: a fixed interval of Δt seconds of prioritized cell transmission (value left unspecified). Assumed real fraction of Tor's daily client population: 500,000 of 2,000,000 (the remainder attributed to botnets, citing a 2014 report of a Tor client-count spike from the Mevade botnet). Assumed mining session length: 1 hour/day at up to 100% CPU, with the paper noting in its appendix that 25% CPU would be more realistic in practice.

### Stated limitations
The authors state their own income projections are "very rough estimates," because it is not possible to learn Tor users' actual hardware, the real (non-botnet) fraction of the client population, the number of users willing to mine, or the number of new users a Bittorrent-over-Tor use case might bring. The paper states a curious relay can learn a client's hash rate from repeated interactions and could use it to recognize returning clients across sessions, and separately that a relay learns that the same client returned the same day if that client pre-mines tickets for later use; the paper's suggested mitigation, unverified experimentally, is that a client randomizes its hash rate. The paper states a powerful miner could attempt to deny paid traffic to other clients on a relay by consuming all its priority-serving capacity, but classifies this as economically irrational because the miner earns more by contributing the same work to the mining pool directly. The scheme is stated as requiring, but not providing, an anonymous crypto-currency (Zerocoin cited) if genuine payment anonymity from mining-pool records rather than only from ticket-signature blinding were later desired; the paper describes this only as a possible future extension. The paper states that CPU mining of Bitcoin or established Altcoins is not profitable on its own in 2014 because the difference between coin value and electricity cost is negative for consumer hardware, which is the premise the whole reward scheme is built to route around by aggregating many clients' otherwise-unprofitable CPU cycles into a pool.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Requires the relay to hold or obtain a crypto-currency mining-pool account and requires that pool to accept proof-of-work shares of the difficulty the relay assigns to clients; the relay's earned coins never touch the client. Requires a partially blind signature scheme (the paper specifies a WI-Schnorr-based partially blind signature construction) under which the timestamp attribute is visible to the signer but the message (H(x)) is not, so the relay can bind an expiry date to a ticket without learning which client holds it. Requires the relay to retain a per-client-visible record of spent ticket hashes only for the ticket-validity window, to detect double-spending; this requires clients and relay to agree on ticket expiry and requires the relay's local clock to be trusted for the date attribute. Requires the underlying Tor circuit to already provide anonymous transport between client and relay for every protocol message (SUBSCRIBE, JOB, share submission, ticket issuance, ticket redemption) since the scheme supplies none of its own; it inherits whatever traffic-analysis exposure the anonymity layer has. Requires a traffic scheduler at the relay implementing at least two priority classes with borrowable ceiling rates (the paper specifies Hierarchical Token Bucket) so that a ticket confers a real, differentiated increase in transmission priority rather than only an accounting entry. Explicitly does not require any bandwidth-measurement or bandwidth-audit subsystem elsewhere in the system, and does not require a central bank or distributed banking quorum — a design goal the paper states to distinguish itself from BRAIDS, LIRA, and TEARS.

### Contradicts
None found. This paper is a design and analytical-cost paper with no independent measurement to reconcile against another source in this batch; its critique of TorPath (Sybil vulnerability despite the TorPath mechanism, and that circuit-assignment servers ignore relay bandwidth, weakening anonymity) is the authors' own analysis of a design cited but not itself in this batch. Its critique of TEARS-style banking, that TEARS still relies on a new distributed bank entity and on an external secure bandwidth-measurement mechanism, is the authors' characterization; JANSEN-HOTPETS-14 (TEARS itself, already extracted in this batch) confirms both properties in its own text, so this is not a contradiction but an independent, consistent reading of the same source.

- Jansen, Miller, Syverson, Ford, "From Onions to Shallots: Rewarding Tor Relays with TEARS," HotPETs 2014 — competing: distributed-bank e-cash alternative, the direct trust-model contrast already in this batch (JANSEN-HOTPETS-14).
- Miers, Garman, Green, Rubin, "Zerocoin: Anonymous distributed e-cash from bitcoin," IEEE S&P 2013 — foundational: the anonymous-currency extension this paper proposes integrating if genuinely anonymous payment is later wanted.
- Ron, Shamir, "Quantitative analysis of the full bitcoin transaction graph," FC 2013 — attack: cited as the class of Bitcoin-anonymity-defeating analysis this scheme's ticket construction is designed to remain inapplicable against, since shares carry only a relay's or pool's Bitcoin address, never the client's.
- Ngan, Dingledine, Wallach, "Building Incentives into Tor," FC 2010 — foundational: earlier Tor incentive-scheme survey/proposal, cited among the four incentive lines the paper's related-work section positions itself against.
