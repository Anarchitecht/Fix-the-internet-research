## [WEI-PACMNET-25] An Empirical Analysis of the Nostr Social Network: Decentralization, Availability, and Replication Overhead

**Citation:** Yiluo Wei, Gareth Tyson. "An Empirical Analysis of the Nostr Social Network: Decentralization, Availability, and Replication Overhead." Proceedings of the ACM on Networking, 2025. DOI 10.1145/3768994.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05709
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05709
**Domain:** J+K

### What it does
The paper measures Nostr, a decentralized social network in which users publish signed posts to any number of independently operated relays (open storage-and-forward servers holding no shared state with each other), and clients connect directly to whichever relays they choose to read from and write to, with no server-to-server federation protocol between relays. Because a user is not bound to one server, the same post can exist as identical copies (replicas) on many relays simultaneously, and a client seeking a given user's posts must independently connect to and query every relay that user is known to use. The authors build two datasets over a six-month window: a relay dataset, giving each relay's metadata, hosting Autonomous System (AS, the routing-visible network block a server is hosted within), and 15-minute-interval availability snapshots; and an event dataset, obtained by connecting directly to every crawled relay and recording, for every unique post, users, and micropayment ("zap") record, exactly which relays hold a copy. From these they compute relay-level, country-level, and AS-level concentration of posts and users; relay uptime and outage-duration distributions; relay income from admission fees and zap payments against an estimated operating cost; per-post replication counts; simulated censorship-style relay and AS removal and its effect on the fraction of posts still reachable from at least one surviving relay; and, from a reconstructed follower graph, the volume of duplicate download traffic caused by a client independently fetching the same post from every relay it shares with the post's author. The paper then proposes two client-side-only mechanisms — capping the number of relays each post is pushed to (optionally weighted to spread across ASes) and a top-N relay-selection rule for retrieval — and evaluates each by replaying the same removal simulation against the capped dataset.

### Parameters
Relay-removal simulation swept top-X removed relays from 0 to 50 and top-X removed ASes from 0 to roughly 30, ranked separately by post count, user count, and (for ASes) relay count. Replication-cap evaluation swept N in {2, 5, 10, 15, 20}. Retrieval-side top-N relay selection swept N in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}. Relay availability measured every 15 minutes over a 3-month window (2023-10-01 to 2023-12-31); event and relay-list crawling ran over a 6-month window (2023-07-01 to 2023-12-31). A relay is classified "dead" once continuously offline for more than a week through the end of the measurement window; 132 relays met this criterion.

### Stated limitations
Excluding offline and paywalled relays from the dataset may skew results toward the behavior of publicly accessible relays only. Relay-availability findings depend on third-party monitoring data from nostr.watch rather than independent probing by the authors, though they state this also avoids bias from a relay banning their own probing infrastructure specifically. Geolocation and IP-to-AS mapping depend on the IP-API service and may carry mapping errors. The estimate of relay operating cost is derived by analogy to published Mastodon hosting prices, not from surveying Nostr relay operators directly. The censorship-simulation only models relay-level and AS-level attacks, not country-level censorship, which the authors state has been documented elsewhere (citing measurement studies of censorship in China and Turkmenistan). The proposed AS-spanning replication-cap strategy is stated to potentially create a new attack surface: a relay that is the sole relay hosted in its AS becomes a high-value, easily identified single point of failure for every post the AS-spanning strategy assigned to it, and the paper's own resilience evaluation only models whole-AS failure, not this more targeted single-relay attack, leaving Nostr's resilience under such targeted adversarial relay selection an explicitly stated open question. The evaluation of the client-side retrieval optimization measures bandwidth savings only and does not measure the resulting effect on user-perceived latency or on content consistency during a query fallback (when the top-N relays do not hold a wanted post). The simulation assumes uniform adoption of the proposed client behavior and does not model users adaptively circumventing the replication cap or strategically choosing which relay subset to use. The proposed mechanisms address only the operational-cost side of relay sustainability; the authors state they introduce no new revenue mechanism, and cite exploring micropayments or tokenization as future work to address the underlying lack of financial incentive. The six-month dataset (ending late 2023) represents one point in a rapidly evolving network, and the authors state relay stability, user base, and client behavior may since have changed; a longitudinal follow-up and a comparative study against Bluesky are cited as future work.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A content-location or discovery layer consuming Nostr-style multi-relay replication needs a mechanism for a client to learn which relays hold a given user's or a given post's replicas, since the paper's own duplicate-retrieval measurement shows that without such a mechanism a client defaults to querying every relay it shares with an author, producing the measured 98.2% wasted-traffic outcome. A replication-capping mechanism that targets AS diversity requires, before it can select a spanning subset, prior knowledge of each candidate relay's hosting AS; the paper computes this via the IP-API third-party service and states this dependency introduces potential geolocation error. Any component relying on the paper's censorship-resilience figures (near-90% availability after 50-relay or 10-AS removal) inherits an assumption that failures occur at the granularity of whole relays or whole ASes; the paper explicitly does not model or bound resilience against an adversary that targets specific relays chosen for their structural position (e.g., the sole relay in a small AS), and states this is an open question for its own proposed AS-spanning capping strategy. A relay-sustainability mechanism built on the paper's income analysis needs an external revenue source beyond admission fees and zap payments to keep free relays with more than roughly 10 users solvent, since the paper's own cost-vs-income comparison finds most such relays do not cover an estimated operating cost from those two revenue sources alone.

### Contradicts
Common misattribution: none identified in the retrieved text that the paper itself flags as a misreading of its own results.
Cross-paper: this paper's post-availability-under-AS-removal figure (>80% availability after top-10 AS removal, Nostr) directly contrasts with RAMAN-IMC-19's Mastodon figure (availability collapsing to <10% after top-10 AS removal), which the paper cites explicitly as the comparison baseline; both figures are measured, not claimed, and the difference is attributed by this paper to Nostr's more uniform relay distribution across ASes versus Mastodon's instance-hosting concentration in a few major providers.
