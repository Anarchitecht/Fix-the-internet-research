## [TRAUDT-ICDCS-21] FlashFlow: A Secure Speed Test for Tor
**Citation:** Matthew Traudt, Rob Jansen, Aaron Johnson. "FlashFlow: A Secure Speed Test for Tor." IEEE ICDCS, 2021. DOI 10.1109/ICDCS51616.2021.00044.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.robgjansen.com/publications/flashflow-icdcs2021.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.robgjansen.com/publications/flashflow-icdcs2021.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
FlashFlow measures the true forwarding capacity of a Tor relay so the Tor network can assign load-balancing weights that a relay cannot inflate by lying about its own bandwidth. Tor relays currently self-report bandwidth to Bandwidth Authorities (BW Auths), which combine the self-report with a small active download test (TorFlow); a relay controls the self-reported number and prior work showed this self-report can be forged to inflate a relay's assigned traffic share. FlashFlow replaces the self-report with a load test large enough to force the relay to carry all the traffic it claims to be able to carry. A FlashFlow team, which constitutes one BW Auth, has a coordinator and one or more measurers. The coordinator opens an authenticated connection (keyed by a public key distributed in the Tor consensus) to each measurer and to the target relay, then divides the total measurement traffic across m measurers, each running k_i = max(1, c_i/p_i) modified Tor processes, where c_i is the measurer's CPU-core count and p_i is its expected number of parallel measurements that period. Each measurer sends and receives normal-looking Tor cells to and from the target over measurement circuits; sent cell contents are randomly generated and a fraction p of returned cells are checked byte-for-byte, so a relay that forges a response (skips decryption, or replies before receiving) is caught with probability approaching 1 as more responses are checked, and a relay forging k responses evades detection with probability only (1−p)^k. The team measures a candidate capacity z; if z is small enough relative to the team's allocated capacity a_i (specifically z < Σ_i a_i (1−ε1)/g), the measurement is accepted as accurate because the true capacity x must then lie in the interval ((1−ε1)x, (1+ε2)x); otherwise the allocated capacity is at least doubled (z0 = max(z, 2z0)) and the relay is remeasured. New relays without a prior estimate start from the 75th-percentile capacity observed across the network in the preceding month. Because the target relay also carries its normal client traffic during the load test, the design caps the relay's self-reported normal traffic at a ratio r of the total measured traffic during the measurement window, so a relay cannot claim high capacity by counting client traffic it never actually forwarded beyond that ratio. Measurement timing per relay is drawn from a schedule known only to the BW Auths and generated from a shared random seed each 24-hour measurement period, so a relay cannot predict when it will be tested and inflate capacity only during that window; results from a majority of BW Auths are aggregated by taking the median, following Tor's existing directory-authority trust model.

### Parameters
- Excess capacity factor f = g(1+ε2)/(1−ε1); used value f = 2.84 for the July 2019 simulation, derived from g = 2.25, ε1 = 0.20, ε2 = 0.05.
- Number of measurement sockets s = 160 (value that maximized throughput on the slowest lab host; determined empirically, range not stated).
- Capacity-estimate multiplier g = 2.25 (smallest g found sufficient for accuracy in the paper's own tuning experiments).
- Measurement duration t = 30 seconds (chosen as, in the authors' words, a reasonable balance of time-to-result versus accuracy).
- Error-bound parameters ε1 = 0.20, ε2 = 0.05.
- Background-traffic ratio r: recommended value 0.25; range tested 0.1-0.5 in the client-background-traffic experiment.
- Measurement period p = 24 hours.
- Response-check probability p (fraction of returned cells verified byte-for-byte): a design variable, no single numeric default stated in the extracted text.
- Measurement team capacity in the simulation study: 3 measurers, 1 Gbit/s each (3 Gbit/s total).
- New-relay initial capacity estimate: 75th-percentile advertised bandwidth among all relays over the preceding month (51 Mbit/s in the July 2019 dataset).

### Stated limitations
The authors state a malicious relay controlling multiple IP addresses on one physical machine can be measured separately at different times and obtain a capacity estimate equal to the shared machine's full capacity for each of its aliases, a Sybil-style limitation FlashFlow shares with TorFlow; their proposed mitigation is to measure suspected co-resident relays simultaneously and average the result, not yet built into the described system. A second stated limitation: because measurement slots are short (30 seconds), FlashFlow may capture a host's burst throughput rather than its sustainable capacity where an ISP or hosting provider throttles sustained rates below burst rates; the authors argue the median of separately scheduled measurements from different BW Auths mitigates this when the burst is due to shared-resource congestion, and that a rate limit applied faster than 15 seconds would still be caught. The threat model explicitly excludes an adversary that controls an autonomous-system-level network position, an adversary that runs a Sybil attack, and an adversary that can change its relay's capacity quickly; the security analysis is stated to hold only under an honest majority of BW Auths (and, transitively, of Directory Authorities that each trust their own BW Auth). The authors state FlashFlow measures capacity, not whether a relay actually forwards real client traffic, and note a relay could send little to no client traffic on non-measurement circuits while reporting accurate self-measured capacity; they call this an additional limitation shared with TorFlow and leave detecting such misbehavior as future work. Future work sections list the unresolved problem of detecting co-resident relays automatically (by IP address or autonomous-system-number heuristics) and combining FlashFlow's secure capacity floor with additional, insecure dynamic measurements (per-relay CPU or network utilization) to further refine weights without reopening the inflation vulnerability.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
FlashFlow requires the same Directory Authority (DirAuth) and Bandwidth Authority (BW Auth) infrastructure Tor already runs: DirAuths distribute the coordinator's public key in the network consensus, and a majority of DirAuths (and, in the paper's recommended configuration, their associated BW Auths) must be honest for the security bound to hold. It requires the BW Auths to hold sufficiently synchronized clocks to coordinate a shared, secret measurement schedule generated from a jointly produced random seed (the paper points to Tor's existing secure-randomness protocol as the source of that seed). It requires the measurement team's aggregate bandwidth capacity to exceed the highest capacity among target relays by the factor f = g(1+ε2)/(1−ε1), meaning the rest of the system must provision and fund measurer bandwidth (3 Gbit/s per team in the deployed-scale simulation) as an ongoing operational cost, separate from relay bandwidth. It requires the target relay to run modified Tor software able to accept authenticated measurement connections and to be reachable simultaneously by the number of measurers a given test allocates. It requires an existing weight-consumption mechanism — Tor's v3bw file format and consensus-voting process — to accept FlashFlow's capacity and weight outputs unchanged, since FlashFlow only replaces how a BW Auth produces those numbers, not how DirAuths use them.

### Contradicts
None found within this corpus. Against the specific claim it is designed to supersede: the paper's own related-work section cites PeerFlow's demonstrated 177x weight-inflation attack against TorFlow and PeerFlow's own analytic self-inflation bound of 4.5x for its recommended parameters, both of which FlashFlow's inflation bound of 1.33x is measured against directly in Table II of the source; this is the authors' own comparison, not a disagreement between independent measurements of the same system.

### References worth retrieving
- **Foundational**: Tor project. "TorFlow" [28] — the currently deployed system FlashFlow replaces; described in the paper's Background section rather than cited as a separate retrievable paper.
- **Competing**: PeerFlow, cited as reference [23] — reports the 177x TorFlow weight-inflation attack and its own analytic inflation bound of 4.5x under recommended parameters and 2/τ in general (τ = trusted relay-weight fraction); produces capacity lower bounds via peer-reported byte counts, needs 14+ days to measure the largest 96.8% of relays.
- **Competing**: Snader, Borisov. EigenSpeed peer-measurement system, cited as reference [32], plus a demonstrated attack achieving 21.5x weight inflation with 20% trusted relays (by number or weight), and separately a "targeted liar attack" reaching 7.4-28.1x depending on trusted-relay fraction.
- **Competing**: Reference [33], Snader and Borisov — a simpler median-of-pairwise-speed variant of EigenSpeed, stated to be vulnerable to a Sybil attack because it uses an unweighted median.
- **Competing**: Reference [9], "SmarTor" — decentralizes BW Auth operation via blockchain and trusted execution environments but keeps TorFlow's file-download measurement technique, so it is stated to remain vulnerable to the same bandwidth-inflation attacks as TorFlow.
- **Competing**: Reference [25], "Simple Bandwidth Scanner" — an easier-to-maintain reimplementation producing the same results as TorFlow, addressing none of its security weaknesses.
- **Competing**: Reference [13], "TightRope" — assumes capacity weights already exist and instead optimizes path selection for load balancing using differential privacy over current relay load.
- **Competing**: Reference [36], Wang et al. — client-side lightweight active latency measurement to detect and avoid congested relays, an alternative mechanism to server-side capacity measurement.
- **Attack**: References [10, 12, 23, 34] — collectively cited as prior work demonstrating TorFlow's self-report and weight-inflation vulnerabilities; [34] and [23] specifically demonstrate 89x and 177x inflation attacks respectively.
