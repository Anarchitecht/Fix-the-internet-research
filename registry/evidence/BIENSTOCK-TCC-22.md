## [BIENSTOCK-TCC-22] On the Worst-Case Inefficiency of CGKA

**Citation:** Alexander Bienstock, Yevgeniy Dodis, Sanjam Garg, Garrison Grogan, Mohammad Hajiabadi, Paul Rösler. "On the Worst-Case Inefficiency of CGKA." Theory of Cryptography Conference (TCC), 2022. DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-22365-5_8.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/1237.pdf
**Source URL:** https://eprint.iacr.org/2022/1237.pdf
**Domain:** H

### What it does
The paper proves that no continuous group key agreement (CGKA) protocol built by treating public-key encryption (PKE) as a sealed component — using only its encryption and decryption interface, never its internal algebraic structure — can guarantee communication cost below linear in group size n in the worst case, and that no single such protocol is simultaneously optimal on every sequence of group operations.

The proof introduces a new primitive, Compact Key Exchange (CKE): n users each independently and non-interactively broadcast a public key, and one designated sender encrypts a symmetric key into one ciphertext that all n users can decrypt with their own private key. The paper first proves CKE and CGKA are equivalent in worst-case communication complexity — a CGKA protocol with small worst-case ciphertexts implies a CKE scheme with small ciphertexts, and a CKE scheme with small ciphertexts implies a CGKA protocol with correspondingly small worst-case ciphertexts. It then proves a black-box separation: any CKE scheme built by treating PKE as a sealed component must produce ciphertexts of size Ω(n) (growing at least linearly with the number of receivers), by an attack that shows a ciphertext smaller than this bound cannot "embed" enough independent decryption trapdoors for all n receivers to be extractable. Composing the two results yields the paper's Ω(n) lower bound on CGKA's worst-case communication.

The lower bound targets a specific pattern of group operations the paper calls a bad sequence: some set of users add k new members and go offline (become passive), while the remaining active users execute rounds of state updates (refreshes) among themselves. Because post-compromise security (PCS — the requirement that once a user is removed or refreshes, all group secrets become independent of any secret material that user previously generated or leaked) forbids reusing key material the passive users received only from the users now updating, each such round must re-communicate fresh secrets to all k passive users, at communication cost Ω(k) per round, even amortized over the whole sequence.

None in the empirical sense — every reported figure is an asymptotic complexity bound derived by cryptographic proof and construction, not a measured runtime, message count, or deployment statistic.

### Parameters
The paper's constructions and proofs are parameterized by the number of group members (n), the number of users added in one collective event (k), and the number of active users conducting rounds of updates (α) — none of these are tuned constants; they are variables the asymptotic bounds are stated in terms of. No concrete numeric parameter (key size, round count, timing) is given, consistent with this being a complexity-theoretic result.

### Stated limitations
The paper states its lower bound applies specifically to CGKA protocols making black-box use of PKE, and states explicitly that circumventing the bound with a strong primitive such as indistinguishability obfuscation (as used in prior multi-party non-interactive key exchange work) may be possible, though the authors describe finding a practical (non-black-box) construction that avoids the bound as "a challenging problem." It states its impossibility result targets the standard, strong form of post-compromise security in the literature, which bundles two properties — double-join prevention and resilience to randomness leakage — and that both properties individually already force the Ω(n) bound; it states, as an explicit open problem for future work, what efficiency guarantees are achievable if the randomness-leakage-resilience property is dropped and only double-join prevention is required. It states weakening PCS to assume randomness is always securely deleted after use (never leaked) permits trivial O(log n) protocols, such as Tainted TreeKEM without its tainting mechanism, but states most real-world implementations should not accept that level of trust. It states two further open problems: finding practical (non-PKE-black-box) building blocks that circumvent the bound, and identifying and forbidding all problematic operation sequences in a live protocol.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Any CGKA construction that wants to escape this paper's Ω(n) worst-case bound must not treat its underlying public-key encryption as a sealed black box — it must exploit PKE's internal algebraic structure directly, or substitute a non-black-box primitive, because the impossibility result covers every protocol that uses PKE only through its stated encrypt/decrypt interface. A system deploying a black-box-PKE-based CGKA (which the paper states covers all currently known practical protocols, including every TreeKEM variant and pairwise-Signal-based CGKA) must accept that some legitimate, non-adversarial sequence of adds followed by state refreshes forces Ω(n) communication in that round, and cannot assume amortization over a longer session avoids this cost, since the bound holds even amortized over the whole sequence. A deployment that wants to avoid the two-administrator degradation case must either accept only ever having one administrator (reducing to classical multicast encryption, achievable at O(log n)) or accept the Ω(n) cost once a second administrator with replacement rights is introduced. The bound's derivation assumes a non-adaptive, passive adversary and a common-reference-string model for CKE, which the paper states only strengthens (makes more general) the resulting impossibility, so a system facing a weaker or differently modeled adversary is not thereby exempted from the bound.

### Contradicts
None found against another corpus entry's measurement — no measured results exist to disagree with. This paper directly bounds the CGKA construction described by BHARGAVAN-HAL-18 (TreeKEM) and standardized in BARNES-RFC-23: it states TreeKEM protocols achieve O(log n) only in "fair-weather" conditions and degrade to Ω(n) worst-case, even amortized, once trees are forced to degrade by PCS requirements — consistent with, not contradicting, BHARGAVAN-HAL-18's own O(log n) fair-weather claim, since that paper never claims a worst-case bound. It states its own lower bound is proved by a method independent of, and not a strengthening or weakening of, the concurrency-specific lower bound in Bienstock, Dodis, Rösler (TCC 2020) — the two results hold under different conditions (this paper's bound needs no concurrency at all) and neither implies the other. BARTUSEK-EPRINT-26 (2026) states it narrows, without refuting, this paper's black-box impossibility result, by using a non-black-box lattice-structured primitive rather than PKE treated as a sealed component.
