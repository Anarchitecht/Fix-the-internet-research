## [PIETRZAK-ITCS-19] Simple Verifiable Delay Functions
**Citation:** Krzysztof Pietrzak. "Simple Verifiable Delay Functions." Innovations in Theoretical Computer Science (ITCS), 2019. DOI: 10.4230/LIPIcs.ITCS.2019.60.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/627.pdf
**Source URL:** https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/627.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
The construction makes the Rivest-Shamir-Wagner (RSW) time-lock puzzle publicly verifiable, producing a Verifiable Delay Function (VDF): a function whose output takes a fixed number of unavoidable sequential computation steps to produce, even with unlimited parallel hardware, but whose correctness any observer can check quickly. The RSW puzzle is a triple (N, x, T) where N = p*q is an RSA modulus, x is a random element, and T is a step count; its solution is y = x^(2^T) mod N, conjectured to require T sequential squarings by anyone who does not know the factorization of N (equivalently, the group order), while the party who set up N can compute the same y in two exponentiations. Pietrzak's protocol lets a prover convince a verifier that y was computed correctly without either party learning the factorization of N. The prover sends an intermediate value mu = x^(2^(T/2)); this splits verifying y = x^(2^T) into verifying mu = x^(2^(T/2)) and y = mu^(2^(T/2)), and the verifier combines these two half-size statements into one new statement (x', y') = (x^r * mu, mu^r * y), using a random challenge r, that holds with the same time parameter halved if the original statement was true, and almost certainly fails if it was false, regardless of what mu a cheating prover sent. Repeating this halving step log(T) times reduces the time parameter to 1, where the verifier checks the final statement directly. The interactive protocol becomes non-interactive (a proof the prover computes once and anyone can later check) by replacing each verifier challenge with a hash of the current transcript, an application of the Fiat-Shamir heuristic; the paper proves this transformation remains sound for its particular logarithmic-round protocol even though the standard Fiat-Shamir soundness argument covers only constant-round protocols. Computation runs over the group of signed quadratic residues (QR+_N, a group in which membership can be checked efficiently, unlike the plain quadratic residues) with N restricted to a product of two safe primes, which the paper states is what gives the protocol unconditional (information-theoretic) soundness rather than soundness resting on a computational hardness assumption.

### Measured results

| Result | Value | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Proof size | About 10 KB | log(T) <= 40 halving rounds, 2048-bit RSA modulus N, each proof element mu_i one 2048-bit group element (about 256 bytes), so t=40 elements give about 10,240 bytes |
| Verification cost | About 3*lambda*t = 12,000 modular multiplications, approximately 4 full RSA-style exponentiations | lambda = 100 (statistical security parameter), lambda_RSA = 2048, t = 40 halving rounds; the paper notes a standard RSA decryption or signature costs one full exponentiation for comparison |
| Proof-computation speedup over solving the puzzle | Over 2^13 (about 8,000-8,192) times faster than computing y itself, with no parallelism used | t = 40, lambda = 100; the paper's own example states that if computing y takes 1 hour, computing the accompanying proof pi takes about half a second on top |
| Memory required to compute the proof at this speed | About 2^27 bits, stated as about 8 MB | Same t=40, lambda=100 setting, using the paper's proof-computation strategy that stores 2^s intermediate group elements (s = t/2 - log(t*lambda)/2 in the worked single-core example) |
| Effect of truncating the halving protocol by Delta=10 rounds | Proof size falls about 25% (40 elements to 30); verification time falls about 15% | Trades 20 saved short exponentiations against 1,024 extra final squarings, for the same t=40, lambda=100 parameters |

These are the paper's own worked analytical estimates for stated parameter choices, not measurements from an executed implementation or benchmark run.

### Parameters
Statistical security parameter lambda: worked example lambda = 100. RSA modulus bit length lambda_RSA: worked example 2048 bits, required to satisfy lambda <= lambda_RSA/2 given that factoring is only subexponentially hard while the protocol's own soundness error is exponentially small in lambda. Time parameter T = 2^t: worked example t <= 40 (T <= 2^40), a power of two by construction; each unit increase in t doubles both the number of halving rounds and the size of the final proof. Truncation parameter Delta: worked example Delta = 10 out of t = 40 rounds, trading reduced proof size and verification cost against extra final squarings. Underlying group requirement: N must be a product of two safe primes (p and q where (p-1)/2 and (q-1)/2 are also prime), computation performed in the signed quadratic residue group QR+_N rather than plain Z*_N or QR_N.

### Stated limitations
The paper states the VDF's security requires that no party, including the prover, know the factorization of the public modulus N, so a real deployment needs either a trusted party to generate N or a multiparty-computation ("MPC ceremony") among participants of whom at least one is honest; the paper cites the Zcash parameter-generation ceremony as a precedent for the latter. This trusted-setup requirement is stated as absent from the earlier random-oracle-based Proof of Sequential Work constructions the paper builds on (Mahmoody-Moran-Vadhan; Cohen-Pietrzak), which need no such setup at all. The paper states that avoiding trusted setup by moving to class groups of an imaginary quadratic field (following Boneh-Bunz-Fisch and Wesolowski) converts the protocol's unconditional soundness into soundness resting on a computational hardness assumption (that finding a small-order group element is hard), a weaker guarantee than the RSA-safe-prime construction's own soundness. The paper states Dan Boneh raised the possibility of applying its proof technique to endomorphisms other than squaring, potentially toward post-quantum security, but states this remains unrealized: "currently we don't know of any such instantiations." The paper documents that its own Fiat-Shamir hashing must include the intermediate statement (x_i, T/2^(i-1), y_i) at every round, not only the prover's message mu_i, because Wesolowski privately communicated an attack on VDF uniqueness that succeeds if the y-values are omitted from the hash input; the paper's construction as specified includes them and the paper does not claim the omission-vulnerable variant is otherwise safe.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Requires a setup procedure — a trusted party or an honest-majority multiparty-computation ceremony — to generate the RSA modulus N without any party retaining or reconstructing its factorization; any subsequent use of the VDF for repeated challenges reuses the same N, so a single compromised setup compromises every future instance built on that N. Requires a cryptographic hash function to instantiate the Fiat-Shamir transformation, with the protocol's soundness proof holding only in the random-oracle model when that hash is treated as ideal; a real implementation using a hash like SHA-256 gets soundness resting on the hash's computational security, not the protocol's stated unconditional guarantee. Requires the challenge x to be a generator of QR+_N, which a randomly sampled x satisfies almost certainly but which the paper states cannot itself be efficiently verified, so an adversary who can choose x adversarially cannot be prevented by this protocol alone from ever picking a non-generator, though the paper's proof shows this does not open a soundness gap in the proof system in the parameter regime described. Requires whatever consumes the VDF output (the paper's own example is the Chia blockchain's use of a VDF as a core ingredient) to be able to run the O(3*lambda*t) group-multiplication verification cost and store or transmit the roughly 10 KB proof for its chosen t; a consumer with tighter proof-size or verification-latency budgets faces the paper's own stated trade-off against Wesolowski's construction, which the paper states produces a single-group-element proof about a log(T) factor smaller with correspondingly faster verification, at the cost of a larger (O(T/log(T)) versus this paper's O(sqrt(T)*log(T))) proof-computation overhead and reliance on a computational hardness assumption rather than unconditional soundness.

### Contradicts
None found. The paper positions itself explicitly against a concurrent, not conflicting, construction: Wesolowski's VDF (cited as [Wes18], eprint 2018/623), described by this paper as having shorter proofs and faster verification at the cost of larger proof-computation overhead and a computational (rather than unconditional) soundness assumption. No numeric claim in this paper is stated by the paper itself to overturn a prior published figure, and no other entry in this batch reports a conflicting measurement for VDF construction cost.

### References worth retrieving
- Boneh, Bonneau, Bunz, Fisch, "Verifiable delay functions," CRYPTO 2018 — foundational: the paper defining VDFs formally, whose construction and security definitions this paper's §5 (completeness, sequentiality, soundness) instantiates.
- Wesolowski, "Efficient verifiable delay functions," Cryptology ePrint 2018/623 — competing: the concurrent VDF construction this paper compares against throughout, holding the alternative proof-size/verification-time/proof-computation-cost tradeoff point.
- Boneh, Bunz, Fisch, "A survey of two verifiable delay functions," Cryptology ePrint 2018/712 — foundational: the cited survey directly comparing this paper's construction against Wesolowski's, and the source for the class-group trusted-setup-avoidance discussion.
- Mahmoody, Moran, Vadhan, "Publicly verifiable proofs of sequential work," ITCS 2013 — foundational: introduces Proofs of Sequential Work, the concept this paper's VDF specializes, and is cited as not requiring trusted setup unlike this paper's RSA-group construction.
- Cohen, Pietrzak, "Simple proofs of sequential work," EUROCRYPT 2018 — foundational: the same author's prior random-oracle-based Proof of Sequential Work construction, cited as leaving open the problem this VDF paper addresses (public verifiability tied to a concrete puzzle) and as also not needing trusted setup.
- Rivest, Shamir, Wagner, "Time-lock puzzles and timed-release crypto," 1996 technical report — foundational: defines the underlying RSW time-lock puzzle this entire construction makes publicly verifiable.
