## [SIDDARTH-FRONTIERS-20] Who Watches the Watchmen? A Review of Subjective Approaches for Sybil-Resistance in Proof of Personhood Protocols

**Citation:** Divya Siddarth, Sergey Ivliev, Santiago Siri, Paula Berman. "Who Watches the Watchmen? A Review of Subjective Approaches for Sybil-Resistance in Proof of Personhood Protocols." Frontiers in Blockchain 3:590171, 2020. DOI: 10.3389/fbloc.2020.590171.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://doi.org/10.3389/FBLOC.2020.590171
**Source URL:** https://doi.org/10.3389/FBLOC.2020.590171
**Domain:** E

### What it does
This is a review paper, not a single mechanism: it defines a taxonomy of proof-of-personhood (PoP) approaches — protocols that certify one human corresponds to one registered identity, so that a system built on top can prevent a Sybil attack (one actor registering many identities). It surveys seven deployed or prototype systems along that taxonomy and evaluates each on decentralization, privacy preservation, and scalability.

The taxonomy identifies two subjective-judgment primitives underlying every reviewed system. A reverse Turing test asks a human to complete a task easy for humans and hard for algorithms (the CAPTCHA family); the paper's cited example, Idena Network's FLIP test, asks the user to pick which of two orderings of images tells a coherent story, and this test alone stops automated bot registration but not one human passing the test multiple times to register multiple identities. A pseudonym party (or web-of-trust vouching, its asynchronous variant) certifies uniqueness by requiring one human to be physically or socially verifiable in only one place, either through synchronous group ceremonies or through existing members vouching for newcomers across a social graph.

Seven systems are classified by governance structure, size, blockchain, protocol, and substrate, then scored on decentralization, privacy, and scalability (Table 1): Idena Network (public network, ~4,012-4,012 users at time of writing, its own Idena chain, synchronous reverse-Turing-test ceremonies using FLIPs run roughly every two weeks, no personally identifying information required but node IP address observable); Humanity DAO (DAO governance, 640 approved members at peak, Ethereum, web-of-trust plus token-curated registry, required sharing a Twitter account, terminated January 2020 after a smart-contract change made joining prohibitively expensive for new applicants); Kleros / Proof of Humanity (DAO plus legal entity, Ethereum, web-of-trust plus token-curated registry with a distributed dispute-resolution court, requires photos, bios, and video selfies stored on IPFS, users vouch for each other with a financial stake forfeited if the vouched-for identity is later ruled duplicate or false); Upala (DAO, Ethereum, "price of forgery" identity model, verification groups assign each member a currency-denominated score that member can steal from the group's shared pool, an act that automatically deletes that member's identity); BrightID (DAO, 556 users with positive anti-Sybil rank at time of writing, Ethereum/IDChain, intersectional web of trust anchored to trusted seed identities, runs GroupSybilRank — a modification of the SybilRank algorithm — to score participants by graph affinity to seeds); Duniter (public network, 2,801 currency holders as of September 2020, its own Duniter chain, web of trust requiring five vouches from existing members and a maximum graph distance of five hops from designated referent members); Equality Protocol (DAO, 529 users, Ethereum, a meta-protocol scoring other DAOs' Sybil-resistance by combining a Quadratic-Voting-based subjective function with an objective Gini-coefficient measure of token distribution).

### Parameters
Not applicable in the sense of a tunable mechanism parameter — this is a comparative review, not an implementation. Structural parameters reported per system: Duniter requires 5 vouches from existing members and a maximum graph distance of 5 hops from a referent member; Idena's validation ceremonies recur approximately every 2 weeks; BrightID's Minimal Anti-collusion Infrastructure (cited from Buterin 2019) lets a voter switch voting keys at any time, so a previously issued voting receipt can be invalidated at will by the voter, preventing verifiable vote-selling short of the voter also giving up the private key itself.

### Stated limitations
The paper states BrightID's GroupSybilRank algorithm's "effectiveness ... in the presence of multiple attack vectors remains to be proved," and separately states there is "no evidence of the Web of Trust schemes' effectiveness for Sybil-resistance in the presence of multiple attack vectors" for the four of seven reviewed systems built on a web of trust. It states a specific attack vector: a bad actor can forge multiple real relationships under different names across enough non-intersecting small groups to grow a significant Sybil presence over time, and that preventing this requires the kind of sophisticated behavioral- and content-classifier modeling Facebook uses (SybilEdge), not a property the web-of-trust protocols themselves guarantee. Reverse Turing tests are stated to stop automated bot attacks but not "human-generated attacks, in which one individual passes the test multiple times and creates multiple different identities" — a mechanical-turk-style marketplace for completing the test on another person's behalf is named as an open, unresolved incentive-model gap for Idena specifically. Kleros's mechanism compromises biometric information (video selfie, personal data) as a structural requirement, which the paper states "may de-incentivize potential users." Upala's score-proportional-to-stake design is stated to more easily benefit capital-rich users, and separately carries a named "avalanche user exit" risk: an event that triggers loss of trust in the system can cause many users to simultaneously destroy their own identities to seize the underlying pooled assets before others do, regardless of the reputation cost. BrightID is stated to not yet be a fully self-sovereign solution because, as of the paper's writing, no established path exists for individuals or groups outside its existing small seed network to self-authenticate, and its social graph is public, which the paper states may compromise user privacy if a participant's real-world identity is separately revealed. The Equality Protocol is stated to lack any verifiable mechanism within the protocol itself to confirm whether a user's subjective rating of another DAO's Sybil-resistance is accurate, creating a named conflict-of-interest and misaligned-incentive risk. Duniter's five-vouch, five-hop requirement is stated to significantly restrict network growth as a direct consequence of the same design that gives it its Sybil-resistance property, and its web of trust is stated to be geographically restricted (concentrated in France and nearby countries), limiting it to local rather than global use.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A reverse-Turing-test-based PoP mechanism (Idena's FLIP) requires a separate mechanism elsewhere in the system to stop one human from completing the test repeatedly under different identities, since the test by itself only distinguishes humans from machines, not one human from many. A web-of-trust vouching mechanism requires an existing social graph with enough density and diversity of connection sources that an attacker cannot cheaply construct many non-intersecting small groups of colluding accomplices; the paper states no reviewed system provides this as a proven property. A vouching-with-financial-stake mechanism (Kleros/Proof of Humanity) requires a functioning, sufficiently liquid financial-stake market and a dispute-resolution process (the paper cites Kleros Court or Aragon's courts) capable of adjudicating disputed identities faster than the incentive to submit false vouches accrues. A trusted-seed-based web of trust (BrightID) requires that seed selection itself be trustworthy and either centrally curated (reintroducing a point of centralization the paper explicitly notes) or subject to a scalable, decentralized seed-selection governance process that, as of the paper's writing, does not yet exist. A score-proportional-to-stake mechanism (Upala) requires that groups independently design and enforce their own admission and verification standards, since the base protocol supplies no floor against capital-rich actors buying higher scores. Any of the reviewed mechanisms used as an input to a downstream system (universal basic income distribution, quadratic voting, quadratic funding, decentralized-oracle witness sampling) requires that downstream system to tolerate the specific residual Sybil-penetration rate the chosen PoP mechanism leaves, since the paper explicitly notes democratic governance is possible under "bounded Sybil penetration" but does not itself quantify what bound each reviewed system achieves.

### Contradicts
None found. The paper does not measure any single figure that another paper in this corpus disputes.

- **Attack-or-critique:** B. Viswanath, K. P. Gummadi, A. Post, A. - **Attack-or-critique:** B. Viswanath, M. Mondal, K. P. Gummadi, A. Post, A. Mislove, "Canal: Scaling Social Network-Based Sybil Tolerance Schemes," EuroSys 2012 — follow-up scaling analysis of the same social-graph Sybil-defense family.
- **Competing:** M. Borge, E. Kokoris-Kogias, P. Jovanovic, L. Gasser, N. Gailly, B. Ford, "Proof-of-Personhood: Redemocratizing Permissionless Cryptocurrencies," IEEE EuroS&PW 2017 — an earlier, more formal proof-of-personhood proposal the paper's taxonomy descends from.
- **Competing:** N. Immorlica, M. O. Jackson, E. G. Weyl, "Verifying Identity as a Social Intersection," SSRN 2019 — theoretical extension of web-of-trust identity verification cited as a proposed but not fully deployed alternative (partially implemented by the closed-source Identiq network).
