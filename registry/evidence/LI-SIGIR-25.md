## [LI-SIGIR-25] Unsupervised Corpus Poisoning Attacks in Continuous Space for Dense Retrieval
**Citation:** Yongkang Li, Panagiotis Eustratiadis, Simon Lupart, Evangelos Kanoulas. "Unsupervised Corpus Poisoning Attacks in Continuous Space for Dense Retrieval." ACM SIGIR, 2025. DOI 10.1145/3726302.3730110.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17884
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17884
**Domain:** B

### What it does
The mechanism inserts a small number of adversarially generated documents into a dense-retrieval corpus so that the injected documents rank above the genuinely relevant documents for a target ranking, without knowing the queries that will be issued against that ranking. Dense retrieval scores a query against a corpus by mapping both to vectors with a neural encoder and comparing the vectors (dot product, cosine similarity, or Euclidean distance); the encoder is denoted E(·). The attack requires white-box access to E(·) and the similarity function, and requires that existing corpus documents cannot be replaced, only that new documents can be added, matching how a search engine ingests newly crawled documents. Two conditions define a successful adversarial document: its embedding must be close to the target document's embedding (so it ranks highly), and its token content must be dissimilar to the target document's tokens (so a human reader who opens it perceives it as unrelated to the target document rather than as a duplicate). The method trains two components. A reconstruction model, a transformer encoder followed by a token-classification layer, is trained to recover the original document tokens from that document's contextual token-level embeddings, so that a chosen embedding can later be turned back into readable text; this model is trained once per target retriever with a cross-entropy loss over token predictions. A perturbation model, a three-layer perceptron, is then trained per target document to output a modified embedding that minimizes mean-squared-error distance to the target embedding while maximizing token-level cross-entropy loss against the target document (capped at a hyperparameter lambda = 5, to balance the two competing objectives). The reconstruction model turns the perturbation model's output embedding into an adversarial document, which is then inserted into the corpus.

### Measured results
Top-1 white-box attack on SimLM (a bi-encoder dense retriever), 100 randomly sampled test queries per dataset, results averaged over 3 runs with different random seeds. Perplexity computed with LLaMA-3.2 1B; BLEU-2 against the target document; ASR (attack success rate) is the fraction of rankings where an adversarial document outranks the lowest-scoring relevant document.

Across all six datasets tested (TREC DL 19, TREC DL 20, NQ, Quora, FiQA, Touché-2020), the paper's method and HotFlip-based word-substitution match on attack-success metrics, while the paper's method produces roughly 4x faster generation (119.9 s versus 512.1 s per document on TREC DL 19) and roughly 20-30x lower perplexity (188.9 versus 6032.6 on TREC DL 19), because HotFlip's token substitutions are gradient-selected in discrete lexical space and read as disordered text, while this method's perturbation happens in the continuous embedding space and the reconstruction model decodes each token conditioned on the whole adversarial document's context. Black-box transfer test: adversarial documents generated by attacking SimLM are applied unchanged against four other retrieval models (DPR, SimLM re-ranker, ColBERTv2, RankLLaMA), averaged over 3 runs; this paper's method's attack-success rate exceeds HotFlip-based transfer on most datasets, and DPR and RankLLaMA show lower transferred attack-success than SimLM re-ranker and ColBERTv2 across all methods. Corpus-poisoning task (no fixed target ranking): SimLM attacked on NQ, FiQA, and Touché-2020, adversarial-document count set as a percentage of corpus size via k-means clustering (one adversarial document per cluster centroid); attack success rate rises with cluster count for all methods, this paper's method and HotFlip-based method perform comparably, and on Touché-2020 a high attack-success rate is reached with only 0.01% of the corpus replaced by adversarial documents. Adversarial-training probe: fine-tuning SimLM with one adversarial document per positive pair from 7,000 MS MARCO training pairs, using contrastive loss with in-batch negatives, reduced this paper's method's Top-1 attack-success rate by an average of 7.9% relative across datasets, while raising average nDCG@10 by 0.002 across the six datasets. Reconstruction-model fidelity, tested out-of-domain on the NQ corpus after training on MS MARCO: SimLM reconstruction accuracy 0.9914, macro-F1 0.9463; averaged across six retrievers tested (SimLM, Contriever, E5-base-v2, TAS-B, DRAGON+, RetroMAE), accuracy is close to 0.99 and macro-F1 averages above 0.93.

### Parameters
- lambda (cross-entropy loss cap in the combined attack loss) = 5 by default; a hyperparameter study (Figure 7, not tabulated with specific values in the extracted text) reports that raising lambda lowers token overlap with the target document.
- Reconstruction model training: learning rate 1e-5, 5 epochs, batch size 128 x 4 GPUs, maximum text length 128 tokens, initialized from uncased BERT-large (30,522-token vocabulary).
- Attack optimization: learning rate 5e-4, run for 3,000 epochs (both this method and the HotFlip-based baseline).
- Random Noise baseline: Gaussian noise scale beta = 0.5 (following a prior paper's search method).
- Random Token baseline: token-replacement ratio p = 0.3.
- Hardware: NVIDIA L4 x 24 GB x 8 GPUs, AMD EPYC 9554P CPU, 384 GB memory, PyTorch 2.1 on Ubuntu.

### Stated limitations
The threat model assumes the adversary cannot edit or replace documents already indexed in the corpus, only add new ones. The paper assumes white-box access to the target retriever's encoder and similarity function for the primary attack; black-box results are obtained by direct transfer of documents generated against SimLM, not by training a surrogate model, because the authors state that distilling a surrogate LLM was too computationally costly for their experiments. The paper explicitly states it does not claim imperceptibility: a successful attack is one where the adversarial document is read by a user and perceived as nonsensical, not one where the user is deceived into thinking it relevant. Adversarial-training results are described by the authors as a preliminary experiment, not extensive.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A retrieval system's exposure to this attack requires that new documents can be added to the searchable corpus without a human or automated relevance check gating whether an embedding-similar, token-dissimilar document reaches a high rank. The attack requires white-box access to the encoder weights and similarity function; a system that keeps its encoder private denies the primary threat model tested here, though the paper's own black-box transfer results (Section 4.3.2) show attack success carries over to encoders the documents were not trained against. Any defense relying on perplexity-based filtering of injected documents is undermined by this mechanism specifically, because the reconstruction model produces token sequences with perplexity comparable to genuine top-ranked documents on the same query (Section 5.3), unlike prior discrete-substitution attacks.

### Contradicts
None found against other entries in this corpus. The paper itself states that its threat model, no query prior knowledge, differs from Zhong et al. 2023's corpus-poisoning attack, which does assume query access; the two are not compared as contradicting measurements but as different threat models.
