## [BRIGHTID-WP] Universal Proof of Uniqueness (BrightID Whitepaper)
**Citation:** BrightID project (collective; not peer-reviewed). "Universal Proof of Uniqueness." Project whitepaper, 2020.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.brightid.org/whitepaper
**Source URL:** https://www.brightid.org/whitepaper
**Domain:** E

Note on source status: this document is a project whitepaper, not a peer-reviewed paper. No figure it states is a measured result from a described experiment; every number below is project-stated design intent or a description of a method used elsewhere, and is recorded as such.

### What it does
BrightID lets a person prove to an application that they are not already using another account under that same application, without a central authority verifying identity. It builds a social graph — a network of cryptographically signed connections between people — and runs a ranking analysis over that graph to distinguish people from duplicate accounts (Sybils).

The basic unit is a connection: two people cryptographically sign a record of having connected, and each person's device stores its own connection list (names and photos of connections), never uploaded to a BrightID node. A BrightID node stores only the graph structure (which BrightIDs are connected) and, separately, a mapping between per-application user identifiers and BrightIDs, one such mapping per registered "context" (a paired identifier system plus verification system an application registers, so a person can hold at most one verified identifier per context). Analysis runs a rank-based Sybil-detection algorithm over the graph — the paper states its research was partly based on SybilRank, which was tested by its original authors on the Spanish social network Tuenti by ranking users by likelihood of being a duplicate and handing the ranked list to human reviewers, who found more duplicates that way than through user reporting alone (a result the whitepaper cites, not one it measured). The paper states nodes are free to run different or combined ranking algorithms and to weigh them differently by context; applications choose which nodes and which resulting verifications to trust.

The graph analysis is anchored by seed groups: pre-selected trusted starting points from which the ranking algorithm's notion of trust or reachability propagates. Where the underlying analysis operates over groups rather than individual connections, a seed is a group of people rather than a single person, so a seed can persist even as individual membership changes. A prospective member of an existing group must connect to more than 50% of the group's current members to join (an additional invitation or authorization step can be layered on top of this base rule). A hierarchy of accountability governs seed status: any member of a seed group's parent or ancestor group can revoke that group's seed status, which cascades to revoke every descendant group's status as well; the whitepaper does not resolve who initially holds the power to grant seed status to a group with no existing parent, stating this as an open question. Independent auditors — members of other seed groups, not the one under review — make "audit" connections to people in a region and record baseline and follow-up rank measurements to judge whether a seed group's presence is actually improving verification outcomes there.

Nodes propagate signed operations (connections, group actions, flags) to each other by a gossip protocol. Time is divided into fixed periods split into an analysis phase and a synchronization phase: during synchronization, nodes exchange missing operations, order the frozen operation set, compute a running checksum, and reach consensus on that set before it is applied to the graph and the following analysis phase begins; some operation types (creating users, founding groups, flagging users, authorizing primary groups) are instead applied immediately rather than waiting for the next synchronization phase.

A user's signing key can be replaced through social recovery: at account setup, the user designates at least three existing connections as "trusted connections." To recover a lost or compromised key, the user reconnects to two of those trusted connections, each of whom sees the connection flagged as a recovery request and confirms which person is recovering; once two such confirmations are recorded, every node substitutes the new signing key for the old, which becomes permanently invalid. A user's private connection data (names, photos) can separately be password-encrypted and held by a trusted connection or in cloud storage, and is handed back to the user during recovery if a trusted connection holds a copy.

A reputation mechanism ("stars") is layered over verification: a verified user accumulates stars over time and by making connections to people who go on to become verified; making a connection stakes some of the connecting user's future stars, forfeited if the connected person fails to become verified within a stated period, with the resulting reward or penalty propagating a few steps through the graph and weakening at each step. An unstaked-connection option exists so a person under social pressure to connect can do so without financial stake or without the risk being visible to the other party, at the cost that an unstaked connection contributes nothing toward either party's verification.

### Measured results
The whitepaper reports no experiment of its own. Every quantitative or evaluative figure in the text is either a design threshold BrightID's own designers chose, or a result the paper attributes to somebody else's prior work, cited without the conditions needed to check it here:
- The whitepaper cites that the SybilRank algorithm, tested by its original authors on the Tuenti social network, produced a ranked list of suspected duplicate accounts that let human workers find more duplicates than user self-reporting did. No number of accounts, ranks, or duplicates found is given in this whitepaper; the citation points to a separate SybilRank source not retrieved in this batch.
- The design threshold for joining an existing group is stated as connecting to more than 50% of current group members; this is a design choice, not a measured optimum.
- The design threshold for account-key recovery is stated as two confirmations out of at least three designated trusted connections; this is likewise a design choice, not a measured optimum.
None of these figures carries an experimental condition (graph size, attacker fraction, run count) that would let a reader use it as a measured cost or a security bound.

### Parameters
- Trusted-connection set size for recovery: at least three designated at signup; two of them must confirm to complete a key-replacement recovery. Values are project defaults, not derived or measured.
- Group-join threshold: connection to more than 50% of a group's current membership, before any additional invitation or authorization requirement is layered on.
- Seed-group audit process: no numeric threshold is given for when an audited region's rank measurements count as "improvement"; the whitepaper leaves this a qualitative judgment for auditors.
- Time-period structure: nodes operate on periods split into an analysis phase and a synchronization phase; no concrete period length is stated.
- Star-propagation depth: rewards and penalties from a connection's outcome are stated to propagate "several steps" through the graph, weakening at each step; no concrete step count, decay function, or weighting formula is given.

### Stated limitations
Seed selection is stated to be especially important, and especially hard, during the network's rapid-growth phase, when subgraphs of genuine users may exist that are not yet well connected to the rest of the graph — such a subgraph can be indistinguishable, from the ranking algorithm's perspective, from a Sybil region an attacker constructed to resemble an honest one. The whitepaper states an open question it does not resolve: who holds the authority to grant seed status to a new top-level seed group with no existing parent group. The whitepaper states that an ineffective seed group (one whose presence does not measurably improve verification outcomes in its region) can itself indicate the seed group's use by an attacker to manufacture Sybils, but gives no automatic detection mechanism for this beyond manual auditor review. Verifications returned by an untrusted node are stated to need independent auditing by an application before being relied upon; the whitepaper does not describe an automatic mechanism that performs this auditing, only that applications and other nodes are able to compare verification responses across multiple nodes.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
An application that wants Sybil-resistant per-user verification must register a context (an identifier system plus a chosen verification method) with one or more BrightID nodes, and must independently decide which nodes' verification responses to trust — nothing in the base protocol enforces node honesty for a single-node context; the whitepaper states this is a centralization risk the application either accepts or mitigates by using multiple independent nodes and comparing their outputs. The Sybil-detection ranking algorithm needs at least one seed (a preselected trusted point or group in the graph) from which to propagate its trust computation, and the quality of the result depends on that seed set's placement relative to genuinely well-connected honest users, which the whitepaper states is unresolved during rapid growth. The gossip-and-consensus protocol requires every participating node to receive the full stream of signed operations (or enough of them, corrected during the synchronization phase, to reach the same frozen operation set) before applying the next graph state; a node that misses operations without later reconciling them during synchronization would diverge from the rest of the network, though the whitepaper does not analyze what happens under sustained partition. Social recovery requires the designated trusted connections to remain reachable and honest; the whitepaper states this method reduces the effectiveness of bribery attacks against a single user (accepting a bribe and then recovering removes the briber's leverage) but does not analyze collusion among the trusted connections themselves beyond stating the user is trusting them "to not collude."
