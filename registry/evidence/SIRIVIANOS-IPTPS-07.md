## [SIRIVIANOS-IPTPS-07] Free-riding in BitTorrent Networks with the Large View Exploit
**Citation:** Michael Sirivianos, Jong Han Park, Rex Chen, Xiaowei Yang. "Free-riding in BitTorrent Networks with the Large View Exploit." Proc. 6th International Workshop on Peer-to-Peer Systems (IPTPS), 2007.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://users.cs.duke.edu/~xwy/publications/largeview-iptps.pdf
**Source URL:** https://users.cs.duke.edu/~xwy/publications/largeview-iptps.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
The large view exploit lets a BitTorrent client achieve a high download rate while uploading no data, by obtaining a swarm view (the tracker's list of active peers for a torrent) far larger than the roughly 50-peer view a compliant client requests and connecting to every peer in that larger view. Connecting to more peers than the protocol specifies raises the probability of being one of the peers a remote leecher optimistically unchokes (BitTorrent's periodic, unconditional grant of upload bandwidth to one connected peer regardless of reciprocation, intended to let newly joined peers with nothing to share begin downloading) and raises the chance of discovering seeders (peers holding the complete file, who upload to requesters in round robin regardless of reciprocation). The authors built this exploit by modifying an existing BitTorrent client (Enhanced CTorrent) rather than writing one from scratch, changing only three behaviors: it never uploads any file chunk to any peer; it re-contacts the tracker every 15 seconds, mimicking a client that keeps rejoining the swarm, to keep requesting fresh partial swarm views; and it connects to every peer named in every view it receives, accumulating a view much larger than the tracker's per-request limit. A fourth, separately tested variant additionally disconnects from a leecher immediately after that leecher has unchoked it, uploaded data, and choked it again, then reconnects to the same leecher under a fresh connection, erasing that leecher's record of past reciprocation with this client (the authors term this whitewashing). The paper proposes, but does not implement or measure, a countermeasure: synchronize all clients and the tracker to a shared time interval T, give every newly joined client the complete swarm membership as of the end of the prior interval, and have each client use a pseudorandom function seeded by its own IP address and the current interval to compute which N peers it is entitled to connect to; a peer receiving a connection request recomputes this function for the requester's claimed IP and refuses the connection if the requester's IP does not appear among its own valid N targets, with N set larger than the standard 50-peer view specifically so that multiple compliant clients behind one NAT (Network Address Translator, a device that lets several devices share one public IP address) can still be assigned disjoint peer sets.

### Parameters
- Rate limit applied to all PlanetLab clients (compliant and free-rider), both directions: 30 KB/s, chosen to give all clients equal resources.
- File size for PlanetLab experiments: 12 MB, shared across all PlanetLab trials (Figures 1 and 3).
- Free-rider tracker re-announce interval: every 15 seconds.
- Free-rider average acquired swarm view: approximately 250 clients, reached after approximately 150 seconds into the download, against a compliant client's standard limit of connecting to at most 50 peers.
- Measurements per PlanetLab node: 8 to 10, varying with node availability.
- Countermeasure design parameters T (interval duration), a (post-interval connection delay, stated only as "much less than T"), and N (per-client allowed peer-connection count, stated only as "greater than 50"): none given numeric values; the paper states determining good values for T, a, and N empirically is future work.

### Stated limitations
The authors state the proposed countermeasure (synchronized interval-based pseudorandom peer assignment) is unimplemented and unmeasured: assessing its added load on the tracker, and empirically determining working values for its T, a, and N parameters, are both named explicitly as future work. The authors state their technique for identifying a peer by IP address is undermined by clients sharing one IP behind a NAT, and their proposed fix (setting N above the standard 50-peer view so NAT-sharing compliant clients can still receive disjoint peer assignments) is a design sketch, not a validated solution with a chosen N. The paper explicitly separates its own single-run, unaveraged public-torrent measurements (one run per torrent, 15 torrents) from its averaged PlanetLab measurements (8-10 runs per node), so its 12-of-15 public-torrent win count for the free-rider does not carry the same statistical confidence as the PlanetLab means. The authors state they observed no correlation between file size and the exploit's effectiveness, without further quantifying that null finding. The authors state whitewashing (disconnect/reconnect to erase reciprocation history) reduces overall swarm throughput and degrades even the whitewashing free-rider's own completion time relative to a free-rider that does not whitewash, concluding a rational free-rider has no incentive to whitewash, a conclusion the authors state follows directly from their own measured 1% and 10% comparisons above.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
The exploit as measured requires only the standard tracker announce protocol and the standard practice, which the authors note is already implemented in mainstream compliant clients for tracker-failure resilience, of accepting and merging additional peer lists from other peers, so no protocol change or credential is required to mount it. The authors' proposed countermeasure requires every client and the tracker to share a synchronized time reference (interval boundaries of duration T) and requires the tracker to actively push membership-change updates (joins and departures) to all currently connected clients at each interval boundary, which the paper states may be assessed for tracker load but is not measured in this paper. The countermeasure requires clients to authenticate connection requests by IP address as the sole identifier, so it provides no defense, and the paper does not claim one, against a client that can present multiple distinct IP addresses. The countermeasure's disjoint-peer-set guarantee for NAT-sharing compliant clients depends on the tracker choosing N larger than the standard 50-peer view; the paper does not state a lower bound relating N to the number of clients expected behind a single NAT.

### Contradicts
None found in this paper's own claims. Cross-reference within this batch: LOCHER-HOTNETS-06 (BitThief) and this paper both report free-riding constructions against BitTorrent's tit-for-tat mechanism using materially different swarm compositions and metrics (BitThief reports relative download-time multiples against a reference client across 7 named public torrents and one controlled testbed; this paper reports absolute mean completion times across a controlled PlanetLab population sweep and a 15-torrent public sample with a win-count metric), so the two papers' numbers are not directly comparable as a single figure, though both independently conclude that a client that uploads nothing can match or beat a tit-for-tat-compliant client under common conditions. This paper explicitly distinguishes its own free-rider definition (zero upload) from Jun and Ahamad's cited PlanetLab study (approximately 150 leechers, 75 free-riders capped at 4 KB/s upload against a 100 KB/s cap for others, so a "free-rider" there still uploads at reduced rate), so a synthesis must not merge the two papers' free-rider population figures as measuring the same condition.

### References worth retrieving
- Cohen, "Incentives build robustness in BitTorrent," P2PEcon 2003 -- foundational (the original description of BitTorrent's tit-for-tat mechanism this exploit targets).
- Axelrod, "The Evolution of Cooperation," Basic Books, 1984 -- foundational (the iterated-prisoner's-dilemma game-theoretic framework the paper uses to explain why tit-for-tat is exploitable when the game has a finite, client-controlled number of rounds).
- Jun, Ahamad, "Incentives in BitTorrent induce free riding," P2PEcon 2005 -- competing (an independent PlanetLab measurement of BitTorrent free-riding under a weaker, reduced-upload-rate definition of free-rider, explicitly contrasted against this paper's zero-upload definition).
- Liogkas, R., Kohler, Zhang, "Exploiting BitTorrent for fun (but not profit)," IPTPS 2006 -- competing/foundational (shows peers that connect only to discovered seeders can raise download rate without contributing bandwidth; cited as related prior evidence for this paper's design rationale).
- Locher, Moor, Schmid, Wattenhofer, "Free Riding in BitTorrent is Cheap," HotNets 2006 -- competing (the near-concurrent BitThief paper, retrieved in this same batch as LOCHER-HOTNETS-06; this paper explicitly enumerates four ways its own experiments differ from and complement BitThief's).
- Piatek, Isdal, Anderson, Krishnamurthy, Venkataramani, "Do incentives build robustness in BitTorrent?," NSDI 2006 -- competing (shows careful peer and per-peer-upload-rate selection can raise download rate while substantially cutting uplink use, a related but distinct exploit from the large-view technique).
- Andrade, Mowbray, Lima, Wagner, Ripeanu, "Influences on cooperation in BitTorrent communities," P2PEcon 2005 -- competing (shows free-riders can attain better sharing ratios than compliant peers in seeder-heavy swarms).
- Shneidman, Parkes, Massoulie, "Faithfulness in internet algorithms," PINS 2004 -- attack (conjectures a Sybil-based optimistic-unchoke-frequency attack via multiple presented identities, explicitly distinguished by this paper from the large-view exploit, which requires no multiple identities).
- Douceur, "The Sybil Attack," IPTPS 2002 -- foundational (cited for the multiple-identity attack the paper explicitly notes its own exploit does not require).
- Li, Clement, Wong, Napper, Roy, Alvisi, Dahlin, "BAR Gossip," OSDI 2006 -- foundational (source of the verifiable pseudorandom peer-selection technique this paper's proposed countermeasure adapts, originally assuming certifiable per-client key pairs and a leave-only churn model this paper explicitly relaxes).
- Feldman, Papadimitriou, Chuang, Stoica, "Free-riding and whitewashing in peer-to-peer systems," IEEE JSAC 2006 -- foundational (source of the term "whitewashing" this paper adopts for its disconnect-and-reconnect technique).
