## [AGARWAL-ICWSM-24] Decentralised Moderation for Interoperable Social Networks: A Conversation-based Approach for Pleroma and the Fediverse

**Citation:** Vibhor Agarwal, Aravindh Raman, Nishanth Sastry, Ahmed M. Abdelmoniem, Gareth Tyson, Ignacio Castro. "Decentralised Moderation for Interoperable Social Networks: A Conversation-based Approach for Pleroma and the Fediverse." Proceedings of the International AAAI Conference on Web and Social Media (ICWSM), 2024. DOI 10.1609/ICWSM.V18I1.31293.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03048
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03048
**Domain:** K

### What it does
The paper measures how a reply thread (called a conversation) on a federated social network fragments as it spreads across independently operated servers (instances), and evaluates whether a machine-learning toxicity classifier that uses conversational context can still work when confined to the partial view any single instance has of that conversation. The paper works on Pleroma, an ActivityPub-based fediverse microblogging server implementation. A conversation is represented as a tree: each post (toot) is a node, and a directed edge from a reply to the post it replies to encodes the reply relationship, so every node has exactly one outgoing edge but can have many incoming edges. Because a toot's local database identifier and parent-post identifier are assigned per-instance and are not globally unique, the authors reconstruct cross-instance conversation trees by using each toot's globally unique URL as the join key across instances, then attach a toxicity score to every toot using Google's Perspective API. The paper adapts an existing graph-based toxicity/polarity model, GraphNLI, which walks the conversation tree starting from a target node and biases the walk toward the root (parent, grandparent, and other ancestor nodes are sampled with higher probability than sibling or child nodes) to gather conversational context before scoring the target toot's toxicity as a 0-to-1 regression value, thresholded at 0.5 to call it toxic. The paper trains and evaluates four combinations of where training data and inference-time context come from — globally across all instances or locally on one instance only — and, for instances too small to train an effective local model, evaluates three federation strategies that share either full conversation data, selectively federated toots, or trained model weights from a larger instance to improve a small instance's local moderation without requiring a global data view.

### Parameters
GraphNLI training: 3 epochs, Adam optimizer, single 24GB Nvidia Titan RTX GPU, roughly 1 hour per epoch on the full training set, 80:20 train/test split used throughout. Toxicity threshold: Perspective API score > 0.5 classified as toxic (primary threshold used for all reported results); a stricter 0.8 threshold was also tested and produced similar per-instance toxic-toot proportions, but no separate full result table at 0.8 is given in the retrieved text. Instance-size buckets: Small under the 25th percentile of toot count (1,290 toots), Medium between the 25th and 75th percentile (7,020 toots median, up to 26,960 toots), Large above the 75th percentile. Toot Federation strategy parameter: conversations of length greater than 5 toots are the ones federated in full from the largest federating instance.

### Stated limitations
The paper defines a "complete conversation" as the tree of every toot that is a reply-descendant of its first toot, and defines fragmentation as an instance seeing less than 100% of that complete conversation; it does not claim to observe every conversation that exists in Pleroma, only those reconstructable from the crawled 713-instance dataset, and toot identifiers usable for reconstruction are limited to matching by toot URL because local database identifiers are not globally unique. Toxicity ground truth comes entirely from Google's Perspective API rather than from human-annotated labels; the authors state this choice was made because human labeling was cost-prohibitive at this scale, and note the proposed approach could equally be implemented with an alternative labeling source. The Local-training/Local-inference configuration, the most realistic fully decentralized setting, produces a macro-F1 of exactly 0.0 for Small instances, meaning the model detects zero toxic toots correctly in that setting; the Full Conversations federation strategy does not fix this and also produces macro-F1 0.0 for Small instances, because the underlying data volume available locally remains too small even after adding missing conversation context. The Model Sharing strategy, which performs best for Small instances, requires a communication channel with high bandwidth between Pleroma instances to exchange trained model weights; the paper states the Toot Federation strategy is more immediately feasible because it reuses the toot-federation channel instances already use with each other. Medium and Large instances show only marginal (Medium, roughly 5%) or negligible (Large) improvement from any federation strategy over local-only training, because they already have enough local data; the paper concludes such instances may lack a strong incentive to participate in federated moderation sharing at all. The Global-training/Global-inference configuration is presented explicitly as an infeasible benchmark rather than a deployable option, since it requires every instance to have full global visibility into every other instance's data at inference time, which the paper states violates the fediverse's premise of independent, self-governing instances.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A cross-instance conversation-reconstruction mechanism, as used here, requires every reply-bearing post to carry a globally unique, instance-independent identifier (this paper uses the toot's URL) because per-instance database identifiers for a toot and its parent are not consistent across the instances that hold copies of the same conversation. A Model Sharing moderation-federation strategy requires a high-bandwidth communication channel between instances capable of transmitting trained model weights, distinct from and in addition to the existing toot-federation channel; it does not require sharing any toot content, which the paper states gives it better privacy properties than the Toot Federation or Full Conversations alternatives. A Toot Federation moderation strategy requires only the federation channels instances already use to exchange content with each other, and works by an administrator federating (e.g., via following) additional accounts on a specific already-federating instance to increase locally available conversational context; it does not require any new protocol or channel. Any moderation mechanism relying on conversational context for classification accuracy needs access to ancestor nodes (parent, grandparent, and further up the conversation tree) specifically, not merely sibling or child context, since the paper's own root-seeking random walk is deliberately biased toward ancestors and its measured toxicity distribution shows toxic toots concentrate nearer the conversation root. A Small instance (under roughly 1,290 toots in this dataset) cannot produce an effective local toxicity classifier from its own data alone under any of the four training/inference combinations tested without either full centralization (which the paper treats as infeasible) or one of the three federation strategies; a system relying on fully local, unfederated moderation for small servers should expect near-zero detection of toxic content specifically at that scale, per the measured Local-Local macro-F1 of 0.0 for Small instances.
