## [WANG-USENIXSEC-14] Effective Attacks and Provable Defenses for Website Fingerprinting
**Citation:** Tao Wang, Xiang Cai, Rishab Nithyanand, Rob Johnson, Ian Goldberg. "Effective Attacks and Provable Defenses for Website Fingerprinting." 23rd USENIX Security Symposium, 2014. Pages 143-157.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity14/sec14-paper-wang-tao.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity14/sec14-paper-wang-tao.pdf
**Domain:** G

### What it does
This paper does two separate things: it presents a website fingerprinting (WF) attack based on a k-nearest-neighbors (k-NN) classifier, and it presents a family of provably private WF defenses built on packet-sequence supersequences. It is not the Walkie-Talkie paper — the registry's own retrieval-justification note misattributes this citation as "the Walkie-Talkie paper"; that paper is a separate 2017 USENIX Security publication by Wang and Goldberg (see Contradicts, below), which this paper cites only as a related technical report ("Comparing website fingerprinting attacks and defenses," CACR 2013-30) predating the actual Walkie-Talkie publication. The k-NN attack works as follows: for each candidate site, the attacker collects packet-sequence traces and computes distances between traces using a large feature set covering timing, packet-count, and packet-size statistics; feature weights are adjusted iteratively (over R rounds) to shrink the distance between traces of the same page and grow the distance between traces of different pages, and classification requires all k nearest neighbors to agree on a page label before that label is assigned, otherwise the trace is classified as non-monitored — a design choice the paper states reduces false positives at small cost to true positives. The provable defense works as follows: a defense is "simulatable" if it can be written as a function from a packet sequence to a padded output sequence with no side channel beyond that function, and "deterministic" if the function has no randomness; the paper proves that a simulatable, deterministic defense that pads every packet sequence in an anonymity set to a single shared supersequence (the shortest sequence that contains every member sequence as a subsequence) gives a formal upper bound on any attacker's classification accuracy over that set, because every output the defense produces observationally overlaps across every sequence in the set. Because computing an exact shortest common supersequence (SCS) over more than two sequences is NP-hard, the paper gives a polynomial-time approximation: at each step, an outgoing packet is emitted if more than one quarter of the remaining sequences have an outgoing packet next, otherwise an incoming packet is emitted, and each sequence's position pointer advances only when its next element matches the emitted direction. Anonymity sets are chosen by clustering packet sequences under one of four information levels available to the client at send time (no information, sequence-end information, class-specific/page-identity information, or full information), trading a smaller number of shared supersequences (lower client-side foreknowledge, higher padding overhead) against a larger number (more foreknowledge required, lower overhead).

### Stated limitations
The attack assumes an oracle that identifies the start and end of a single page-load's packet sequence, and assumes the user does not interrupt the load or interleave other web activity; the paper offers heuristics (an initial burst of outgoing packets roughly three times the later rate) rather than a solved general mechanism. The paper states the attack is not effective at finding one sensitive page access among millions without a very low false-positive rate, and that attack usefulness depends heavily on the base incidence rate of monitored-page visits; at incidence below 0.1%, false alarms would overwhelm true positives with this classifier. It states that no attack is known to beat the accuracy upper bound of the new defense on this specific data set, but explicitly does not claim optimality against defenses not evaluated or against future attacks: "we do not know if there exist better attacks for these defenses." The polynomial-time supersequence approximation is stated to admit unboundedly poor cases in the worst case, citing a hardness result for approximating shortest common supersequences. Anonymity-set selection under partial information (sequence-end or class-level) is stated to be less efficient (higher bandwidth overhead) than full information, and the paper states its clustering strategy for class-level information may not be optimal — "a clever clustering strategy... could achieve lower bandwidth overheads" is left open.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
The provable defense requires the client to determine, before or during a page load, which anonymity set (which shared supersequence) a load belongs to; the paper's four information levels bound what is realistic (full information, the strongest guarantee, requires knowledge of the load's entire future packet sequence in advance, which the paper states is "not generally of practical interest except for serving as a bound"). It requires the client to avoid storing extraneous browsing history to assist set selection, because on Tor Browser (no disk cache) such storage is itself a privacy risk distinct from the WF threat. It requires an attacker-side classifier trained on the specific defended distribution to be evaluated meaningfully — the paper always retrains and re-tests its k-NN attack directly against each defense's simulated output rather than assuming transferability across defenses. The defense's formal privacy bound holds only under the paper's simulatable/deterministic defense model; it does not extend a guarantee to random or non-simulatable defenses, and the paper explicitly separates BuFLO (not provably private on its own) from its own construction.

### Contradicts
This paper is not the Walkie-Talkie paper. The retrieval registry's justification field for this key states "This is the Walkie-Talkie paper," which this full text does not support: Walkie-Talkie is Wang and Goldberg, USENIX Security 2017, cited within this paper's own bibliography only as an earlier 2013 CACR technical report ("Comparing website fingerprinting attacks and defenses"), not as this paper's own contribution. Any figure in this corpus attributed to "Wang, Walkie-Talkie, USENIXSEC-14" under this key is misattributed; the actual Walkie-Talkie defense numbers (49.7% accuracy under symmetric collisions, 31% bandwidth / 34% latency overhead) appear as externally-cited results inside SIRINAM-CCS-18 in this batch, sourced to the true 2017 Walkie-Talkie paper, not to this one.
