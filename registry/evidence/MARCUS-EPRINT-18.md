## [MARCUS-EPRINT-18] Low-Resource Eclipse Attacks on Ethereum's Peer-to-Peer Network
**Citation:** Yuval Marcus, Ethan Heilman, Sharon Goldberg. "Low-Resource Eclipse Attacks on Ethereum's Peer-to-Peer Network." IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive, 2018/236.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/236.pdf
**Source URL:** https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/236
**Domain:** A

### What it does
The paper isolates a victim node from every other participant on Ethereum's peer-to-peer network, using only two attacker-controlled machines, each with one IP address, and without controlling any path the victim's traffic travels (an off-path attack). It exploits the deployed geth (Go Ethereum) client's node-discovery protocol, a modification of Kademlia in which every node holds a persistent on-disk database (`db`) of every node it has ever contacted plus a short-term, memory-only routing table (`table`) that is empty on every reboot and rebuilt from the `db` through a seeding process.

Three separate attacks are presented. Connection monopolization exploits the absence, before geth 1.8.0, of any limit on unsolicited incoming TCP connections: the attacker waits for the victim to reboot and immediately opens incoming connections from many self-generated node identities (ECDSA key pairs, free to generate) until every one of the victim's connection slots is filled. Table poisoning exploits the fact that the mapping from a node identifier to its target routing-table bucket, `logdist`, is a public deterministic function (a SHA3 hash distance, inherited from Kademlia's public distance metric) with no secret salt: the attacker generates node identities that map to the router table's most heavily weighted buckets, inserts them into the victim's persistent `db` before the attack (via ping/pong bonding, refreshed at least once every 24 hours so entries do not expire), and then, at the instant of the victim's reboot, floods pings from those pre-registered identities so they fill the empty `table` before the victim's own seeding process or its `lookup(self)` call can insert legitimate node identifiers from the `db`. A third attack manipulates the victim's local clock: because query/response freshness is checked only via a timestamp with a 20-second tolerance window, forcing the victim's clock more than 20 seconds out of sync with the network causes it to reject legitimate messages and empty its `db`, then to accept only attacker messages carrying matching (attacker-controlled) timestamps.

### Parameters
- maxpeers = 25 (default, geth v1.6.6, used for all reported experiments).
- Outgoing TCP connection cap: floor((1+maxpeers)/2) = 13, by default (pre-1.8.0).
- Post-countermeasure incoming-connection cap (Countermeasure 1, live in geth 1.8.0): floor(maxpeers/3) = 8.
- Kademlia-derived routing table: 256 buckets pre-1.8.0 (reduced to 17 in geth 1.8.0 "in recognition of the fact that most of the lower-number buckets are empty"), bucket capacity k=16.
- `db` eviction: nodes older than 1 day (no last-pong response) are evicted once per hour.
- Table eviction on new-node insertion: pings the oldest bucket entry; pre-1.8.0, if that ping is unanswered, the new node replaces it.
- Table eviction on failed lookup: a node is removed after more than 4 failed findnode responses.
- Timestamp freshness tolerance exploited by the time-manipulation attack: 20 seconds.
- Countermeasure 2 as implemented in geth 1.8.0 (partial, not the paper's full recommendation): at most 2 nodes per /24 IP subnet per bucket, at most 10 nodes per /24 subnet in the whole table.
- Attack scaling parameter n=17 buckets targeted, yielding 16 x 17 = 272 crafted node IDs, chosen because a victim's table under normal operation was observed to hold only "a few hundred nodes" even after days online.

### Stated limitations
The authors state their attacks require the victim to reboot, and list causes: outages, power failures, host-OS attacks, "packet of death" crashes, DDoS-induced resource exhaustion, or ordinary client software updates; they quote the prior Bitcoin eclipse-attack authors' statement that "the security of the peer-to-peer network should not rely on 100% node uptime." They state the table-poisoning attack, as described, is "tailor made for a particular victim," so scaling it to attack many victims simultaneously would need a precomputed lookup table (which they construct and size at ~67 MB) rather than per-victim crafting. They state their measured success rates undercount honest-network interference: in the 33-day experiment, the attack failed in 15 of 49 tightly-measured non-seeded reboots because honest nodes made unsolicited incoming connections to the victim before the attacker filled all slots, a race condition inherent to the fact that geth (even after Countermeasure 1) still permits some honest incoming connections. They state a residual vulnerability after their own recommended Countermeasure 2 (one-to-one IP-to-key mapping) is only partially implemented in geth 1.8.0: with the partial subnet-based limit, an attacker restricted to /24-subnet limits can still associate up to 10 node IDs per IP address with a victim's table, which "does not raise the bar for attackers quite as high as we had hoped." They state Countermeasures 3 and 4 (making the node-ID-to-bucket mapping and the self-lookup target non-public via a locally-random secret) were not adopted in geth 1.8.0, so the public-mapping vulnerability those countermeasures would close remains open post-patch. They state Countermeasure 7 (replacing timestamp-based freshness with nonces) was not implemented because it breaks backward compatibility with the existing RLPx v4 packet format. They state a residual "low-resource eclipse attack" (Section VI-C) that combines the clock-manipulation attack with table poisoning can succeed with very few attacker node identities and can circumvent most of their proposed countermeasures, because a victim whose clock has been skewed empties its own `db` and rejects legitimate neighbor messages, eliminating the protective effect Countermeasures 3-6 depend on.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A Kademlia-derived node-discovery layer that (like Ethereum's) does not require the "well-formedness" property of iterative content lookup — because content is replicated at every node rather than discovered by address — gains no benefit from keeping the node-ID-to-bucket distance mapping public, and a public mapping is what both the table-poisoning attack and, per the related-work section, the Steiner/Kohnen/Locher-family Kademlia-DHT eclipse attacks exploit; a system reusing Kademlia's routing-table structure without needing public-distance lookups should salt the distance function with a per-node local secret (the paper's Countermeasure 3) before any deployment claim of eclipse resistance is made. A node-identity scheme that allows unlimited free identity generation on one IP address (unlimited ECDSA key generation, as in pre-1.8.0 geth) requires a separate binding mechanism — the paper recommends a cryptographically-verified one-to-one IP-to-key mapping, proved by a signed pong response — before any k-bucket-style routing table can resist identity-flooding from a single physical machine; a subnet-count heuristic alone (2 per /24 per bucket, 10 per /24 total, as partially adopted in geth 1.8.0) leaves an attacker up to 10 identities per IP address, which the paper states is insufficient. Any client using timestamp-based freshness for its discovery-protocol UDP messages requires either a tight and enforced clock-synchronization guarantee or a nonce-based challenge-response scheme (Countermeasure 7) instead, because a timestamp check alone is defeated by manipulating the victim's own local clock (e.g., via NTP) rather than by any network-path attack.

- Maymounkov, Mazières, "Kademlia: A peer-to-peer information system based on the XOR metric," IPTPS 2002 — foundational (already in corpus per BRIEF section 7).
- Heilman, Kendler, Zohar, Goldberg, "Eclipse Attacks on Bitcoin's Peer-to-Peer Network," USENIX Security 2015 (cited as [23]) — foundational, the Bitcoin-eclipse-attack paper this work's Ethereum attack is modeled after and repeatedly compared against.
- Apostolaki, Zohar, Vanbever, "Hijacking Bitcoin: Routing Attacks on Cryptocurrencies," IEEE S&P 2017 (cited as [17]) — competing/related attack, a BGP-level eclipse mechanism contrasted with this paper's off-path, low-resource approach.
- Steiner, En-Najjary, Biersack (cited as [43]) — competing, prior Kademlia-DHT content-eclipse attack whose goal (isolating a content item) is explicitly contrasted with this paper's goal (isolating a node); the paper states [43]'s countermeasures (adopted in eMule 0.49a) are the model for its own Countermeasure 2.
- Kohnen, Leske, Rathgeb, "Conducting and Optimizing Eclipse Attacks in the Kademlia Peer-to-peer Network" (cited as [27]) — attack/critique, shown by this paper to circumvent the eMule-style subnet countermeasures using only a logarithmic number of IP addresses via Kademlia's iterative lookup, a mechanism this paper argues does not apply to Ethereum.
- Locher, Mysicka, Schmid, Wattenhofer (cited as [29]) — attack/critique, another Kademlia table-poisoning attack in the same family as [43],[27].
- Singh, Ngan, Druschel, Wallach, "Eclipse Attacks on Overlay Networks: Threats and Defenses" (cited as [40]) — foundational, an early general eclipse-attack paper for structured overlays.
- Gervais, Karame, Wüst, et al. (cited as [21]) — competing/attack, on eclipse attacks' role in optimal double-spending and selfish-mining strategies.
- Urdaneta, Pierre, Van Steen, "A Survey of DHT Security Techniques" (cited as [44]) — foundational survey (already flagged as a priority bibliography in BRIEF section 4.4).
