## [JANSEN-PAM-21] On the Accuracy of Tor Bandwidth Estimation
**Citation:** Rob Jansen, Aaron Johnson. "On the Accuracy of Tor Bandwidth Estimation." Passive and Active Measurement Conference (PAM), 2021. DOI: 10.1007/978-3-030-72582-2_28.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.robgjansen.com/publications/torbwest-pam2021.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.robgjansen.com/publications/torbwest-pam2021.pdf
**Domain:** I

### What it does
The paper measures, rather than proposes, the accuracy of the deployed Tor bandwidth-estimation pipeline that Tor uses to balance client traffic load across relays. Tor derives a relay's advertised bandwidth from the minimum of two relay-reported self-measurement values, average bandwidth and observed bandwidth. Observed bandwidth is the highest throughput a relay sustained over any 10-second window during the preceding 5 days; a new relay bootstraps this value by sending 125 KiB over each of four self-built circuits and computing 4 x 125 KiB / 10 s if that completes within 10 seconds. A separate measurement tool, TorFlow, builds two-hop circuits through each relay, downloads files of 16 KiB to 64 MiB through them, computes each relay's measured-speed ratio to the network mean, and multiplies that ratio by the relay's advertised bandwidth to produce the published consensus weight that drives client path selection. The paper runs two independent analyses of this pipeline's accuracy. First, a passive analysis computes the relative standard deviation of each relay's weekly sequence of advertised-bandwidth values over one year of published Tor consensus and descriptor data, as a proxy for estimation noise. Second, an active experiment adds a new SPEEDTEST Tor cell type to a modified Tor client and relay: a controlled client builds a three-hop circuit through a target relay to one of the researchers' own relays, sends a 20-second burst of SPEEDTEST cells at the maximum rate TCP and Tor's flow control allow, and the target relay's own subsequent server-descriptor publication reveals whether the added load raised its self-reported observed bandwidth above its pre-experiment level.

### Parameters
Observed-bandwidth window: highest sustained throughput over any 10-second interval within the preceding 5 days (Tor protocol specification, cited, not chosen by this paper). New-relay bootstrap self-test: 4 circuits, 125 KiB sent per circuit, yielding an initial observed bandwidth of 50 KiB/s (about 410 Kbit/s) if completed within 10 seconds. Server-descriptor publication interval: 18 hours. TorFlow probe file sizes: 16 KiB to 64 MiB. Speed-test experiment: 20-second SPEEDTEST burst per target relay, 10 parallel client-relay circuit pairs (20 parallel sockets) per target, relay's own MaxAdvertisedBandwidth throttled to the Tor-minimum 300 Kbit/s so the researchers' relays did not attract organic client traffic, 487 added lines of code to Tor v0.3.5.7. Analysis window for relative standard deviation: computed per relay per week over 52 weeks.

### Stated limitations
The authors state the 52.9% network-capacity underestimate is itself a lower bound, because their measurement machine was capped at a 1 Gbit/s network link and could not test all relays, so both the true network capacity and the true degree of estimation error may exceed what they measured. The paper states its analysis establishes correlation between relay characteristics (uptime, position, capacity) and estimation error, not causation. The paper does not build or propose a replacement measurement system; it states that TorFlow, SmarTor, and Simple Bandwidth Scanner share similar measurement designs and similar limitations, and cites PeerFlow and TightRope as prior proposed alternatives without independently evaluating either. The paper states that easier techniques for a relay to inflate its own measured bandwidth are already known from prior work, and that its own speed-test technique could itself be used maliciously by a network-level adversary (an ISP or nation-state observing target relays) to redirect client traffic toward relays it can observe, without needing to control those relays.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A capacity-ordered overlay that assigns relay selection weight from a relay's own self-reported throughput requires that a relay actually attract enough real traffic to reach its true capacity within the self-measurement window (5 days here); a relay that has not yet attracted proportional traffic reports a capacity below its true value, and the paper's central measured finding is that most relays across all positions had not. Any weighting or selection mechanism drawing on such a self-reported figure inherits a systematic bias toward underestimating large, low-uptime, or newly active relays, so consumers of the weight (client path-selection logic) must not treat it as ground truth capacity. Because the resulting bias makes it comparatively less costly for an adversary to acquire disproportionate selection weight with modest bandwidth spent on many long-lived, low-capacity relays (limited only by Tor's two-relays-per-IP-address rule), any component relying on this weighting for a security property (resistance to traffic-correlation or route-selection attacks) must budget for that gap rather than assuming the weight tracks true relative capacity.

### Contradicts
None found within this batch. The paper's own finding (roughly 50% network-wide underestimation, active speed-test measurement, August 2019, 4,867 relays) is the first published measured figure for Tor capacity-estimation error and does not itself contradict another paper in this corpus, though it is a direct measured counterpoint to any capacity-ordered-overlay design (for example HSkip+, already in this corpus) that assumes honest self-reported bandwidth without independent verification — HSkip+'s bandwidth-ordering property is stated by that paper's authors to assume honest reporting and is not defended against it, and this paper measures that the reporting is inaccurate even absent any adversary.
