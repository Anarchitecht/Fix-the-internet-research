## [THIEMT-CCS-25] Generic Anonymity Wrapper for Messaging Protocols
**Citation:** Lea Thiemt, Paul Rösler, Alexander Bienstock, Rolfe Schmidt, Yevgeniy Dodis. "Generic Anonymity Wrapper for Messaging Protocols." ACM SIGSAC Conference on Computer and Communications Security (CCS), 2025. DOI 10.1145/3719027.3765186.
**Retrieved:** full text
**Source URL:** https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/1619.pdf
**Domain:** H

### What it does
An Anonymity Wrapper (AW) hides the metadata a messaging ciphertext otherwise carries — sequence numbers, sender public keys, epoch identifiers — so that an observer of the wire, including the messaging server itself, sees only pseudorandom-looking ciphertexts, and this holds even after a temporary exposure of a user's stored secrets (the paper's forward and post-compromise anonymity, FA and PCA, mirroring forward secrecy and post-compromise security for confidentiality). AW wraps an existing two-party or group messaging protocol rather than replacing it: it consumes symmetric input keys the underlying protocol already derives (for example, Signal's Double Ratchet, DR, or Sender Keys, SK) and derives from them a chain key that both sender and receiver advance in lockstep. To anonymize one outgoing message, the sender uses the current chain key ck to derive the next chain key, a pseudorandom tag, and a per-message symmetric key, encrypts the payload under that key with authenticated encryption, and sends the ciphertext together with the tag; the ciphertext and tag together look uniformly random to anyone without the chain key. Because ciphertexts can arrive out of order, the receiver precomputes multiple future key-tag pairs in advance and, on receipt, matches the incoming tag against its precomputed set to find the decryption key. The receiver's local state is split into three constant-size lists — future ciphertexts of the current epoch, not-yet-received ciphertexts of the prior epoch (padded with indistinguishable dummy entries when the list is not full), and future ciphertexts of the next epoch — so that whichever ciphertexts a receiver has or has not yet processed is not revealed even if the receiver's device is later exposed; deletion during epoch advancement uses the position of the first not-yet-used precomputed tag as an implicit pointer rather than an explicit sequence number, which is the mechanical difference from the prior wrapper design (Bienstock et al., CCS 2023) that this construction is derived from and that leaked epoch/sequence numbers on exposure. An Authenticated variant, AAW, adds sender authentication for group settings with potentially malicious receivers: the sender signs each anonymized ciphertext with a key rotated every update, one-time-pad-encrypts the signature under a key derived from the chain key so the signature itself is not visible on the wire, and the receiver stores only a commitment to the sender's verification key alongside each precomputed key-tag pair, opened only upon receipt, so the relation between precomputed pairs and their signer stays hidden. A Counting Bloom Filter (CBF) — a probabilistic set membership structure supporting insertion, deletion, and false-positive-bounded membership testing — replaces most of the two future-facing precomputed-pair lists: only a small number of pairs are stored explicitly for the common case of little reordering, and the rest are represented implicitly in a per-session CBF, recomputed on demand when an incoming tag is found in the filter but not in the explicit list.

### Parameters
- Tag length: 16 bytes.
- Commitment length (AAW): 32 bytes of HKDF output.
- Authenticated encryption: AES-256-GCM.
- Signature scheme (AAW): EdDSA over Ed25519.
- Precomputed-list size fut = past = 2000, used for the reported storage-cost figures.
- Benchmark hardware: 32-core Intel Core i9-14900K.
- Security proofs given in the random oracle model (ROM), not the standard model — the paper states this was chosen "to simplify the proofs for better comprehensibility and compactness."

### Stated limitations
The construction hides metadata only at the protocol layer, not at the network/routing layer: "efficient (anonymous) routing of messaging traffic" is explicitly out of scope, and a deployment still needs the receiver identity attached to the ciphertext (or a separate anonymous-routing mechanism) for delivery, so AW alone does not hide who is communicating with whom from a routing observer. The paper states network-level anonymity techniques (onion routing, mix networks) and server-side techniques (Private Information Retrieval, Oblivious Message Retrieval) are complementary and orthogonal, not solved by this work. Anonymous contact discovery, anonymous communication initialization, anonymous session synchronization across a user's multiple devices, and plausible anonymity of the surrounding messaging application (including hiding entries in a user's address book) are stated as open directions the paper does not address. The proofs are given in the random oracle model; lifting them to the standard model is stated as future work. AW and AAW are wrapper protocols requiring an external supply of fresh symmetric update keys from an underlying (continuous) key-exchange protocol such as the Double Ratchet; the paper states that modifying such a protocol directly to provide anonymity, avoiding the redundancy of wrapping, "remains an interesting open problem." A full production integration of AW with the Double Ratchet is stated as "a task for deployment in practice," not performed in this paper — the evaluation instead samples the wrapper's update keys uniformly at random rather than driving them from a live DR session. The dominant storage cost (120,000 B per recipient for the tag-indexed hash table before Bloom-filter compression, and 288,096-480,096 B per session for key/tag lists) is stated by the authors as the construction's "main drawback."

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
Requires an underlying two-party or group messaging protocol that already derives fresh symmetric keys on a regular schedule (a continuous key-exchange protocol) — AW consumes these as its update-key input and does not itself generate long-term key material. Requires the sender and every receiver to advance their chain-key state in the same epoch sequence; a receiver that falls out of sync past the bounds of its precomputed future/past lists needs an out-of-band resynchronization the paper does not specify. Requires the delivery mechanism to attach a receiver identifier (or route the anonymized ciphertext through a separate anonymity-providing routing layer) since AW's own ciphertext carries no addressing information once anonymized. For AAW's sender authentication, requires each receiver to already hold or fetch the sender's rotated verification key at the point it opens the stored commitment — the paper does not specify verification-key distribution as part of AW itself. Requires storage proportional to the configured future/past list sizes (or, with the Bloom-filter extension, a smaller but still per-session-proportional amount) on every receiver device, which a resource-constrained client needs to budget for independent of message volume. Composing AW with a protocol whose own header already reveals structural metadata (for example, an unwrapped MLS Private Message, which still reveals group and epoch identifiers) does not remove that metadata — AW hides only what it wraps, so the underlying protocol's remaining visible fields must be addressed separately or not used.

### Contradicts
The paper's abstract states Signal "considers deploying" this construction; per the extraction rule to attribute value judgments to whoever made them, this is Signal's evaluation as reported by the paper's authors (one of whom, Rolfe Schmidt, is a Signal Messenger employee and paper co-author), not an independent finding — it must not be restated as a fact established by this paper's own experiments. ### References worth retrieving
- Foundational: Bienstock, Rösler, Tang, "ASMesh: Anonymous and Secure Messaging" (mesh-network anonymizing wrapper, CCS 2023) — the direct predecessor design this construction's receiver-state improvements are measured against; cited in-text as [BRT23].
- Competing: Hashimoto, Katsumata, Prest, "How to Hide MetaData in MLS-Like Secure Group Messaging" (CCS 2022) — a specialized anonymity extension for tree-based CGKA (Continuous Group Key Agreement) that this paper's generic construction is compared against for scope and generality.
- Competing: Dowling, Hauck, Riepel, Rösler, unidirectional two-party anonymous protocol using a pairing-based Lamport signature scheme — cited as theoretically hiding wire and state metadata but "too inefficient for real-world deployment," a concrete cost figure worth retrieving in full.
- Foundational: Perrin, Marlinspike, "The Double Ratchet Algorithm," Signal specification, 2016 — defines Header Encryption, the Signal-specification concept this paper states "is not suited for practice" because receivers cannot efficiently identify the decryption key without expensive trial decryption.
- Foundational: the MLS standard (Barnes, Beurdouche, Robert et al., RFC form cited as [BBR+23]) — defines Private Messages, the metadata-hiding mechanism for MLS ciphertexts that this paper states covers only messaging ciphertexts, not key-update or membership-operation traffic.
- Attack/critique: analyses of Sealed Sender demonstrating that communication-partner interaction patterns can reveal identity despite explicit-metadata hiding, cited as [MKA+21] and [BH23] — direct measured critiques of the deployed baseline this paper compares against.
- Foundational: Jaeger, Kumar, Stepanovs, "Symmetric Signcryption" fine-grained attacker-model formalization, cited as [JKS24]/[JK25] — the closest prior formal treatment of the confidentiality-plus-authenticity combination AAW extends with anonymity.
