## [TRAUTWEIN-ARXIV-26] Large-Scale Measurement of NAT Traversal for the Decentralized Web: A Case Study of DCUtR in IPFS

**Citation:** Dennis Trautwein, Cornelius Ihle, Moritz Schubotz, Corinna Breitinger, Bela Gipp. "Large-Scale Measurement of NAT Traversal for the Decentralized Web: A Case Study of DCUtR in IPFS." ACM Internet Measurement Conference (IMC), 2026. DOI 10.1145/3777912.3809138 (also arXiv:2604.12484; an earlier four-author preprint of the same underlying study is arXiv:2510.27500).
**Retrieved:** full text via https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12484
**Source URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.12484
**Domain:** L

### What it does
DCUtR (Direct Connection Upgrade through Relay) upgrades an already-established relayed connection between two libp2p peers behind Network Address Translators (NATs) to a direct connection, without a centralized STUN/TURN/signaling server. It runs over three precursor libp2p protocols: Identify (each peer learns its own externally observed address from a connected peer, replacing a separate STUN lookup), AutoNAT (a peer asks others to dial back its advertised address to learn whether it is publicly reachable), and Circuit v2 (a private peer holds a bounded, time- and data-capped reservation at a relay, so any public peer can serve as a relay without unbounded resource exposure — this is the mechanism VYZOVITIS-SPECS-23 specifies). One peer (the initiator, which accepted the relayed connection) first attempts Connection Reversal: if the other peer (the listener) appears publicly reachable from Identify data, the initiator dials it directly, skipping hole punching entirely. If that fails or the listener is private, the initiator sends a CONNECT message with its candidate addresses over the relay, measures the relayed round-trip time (RTT), and sends a SYNC message; the initiator then waits half the measured RTT before dialing while the listener dials immediately upon receiving SYNC, aiming for near-simultaneous packet arrival at both NATs so each side's outbound packet opens a NAT mapping before the peer's inbound packet arrives. The coordination phase exchanges under 500 bytes per direction over two round trips through the relay; if the first synchronized attempt fails, synchronization and dialing are retried up to two more times. DCUtR is shipped as a default component of go-libp2p, rust-libp2p, and js-libp2p, so it runs automatically in every application built on those stacks, including IPFS (InterPlanetary File System) and Ethereum nodes.

### Parameters
- DCUtR retry count: initial synchronized attempt plus 2 retries (3 total), each retrying both synchronization and synchronized dialing
- Coordination-phase message size: fewer than 500 bytes per direction over the relay
- Wait time before dialing after SYNC: half the measured relayed RTT (T_wait = 1/2 · RTT_relayed), a fixed synchronization strategy in the deployed protocol; the paper's own §7.2 proposes but does not deploy a refined formula, T_wait = 1/2(RTT_Listener-Initiator + RTT_Listener-NAT − RTT_Initiator-NAT)
- Hole-punch stream open timeout: 15 seconds (client reports NO_STREAM if the /libp2p/dcutr stream is not opened within this window)
- Network-inclusion threshold for the H1 success-rate analysis: client must have contributed more than 1,000 data points from a given network
- Default Hop Limit used in a birthday-paradox port-collision proposal (§7.1, proposed not deployed): 256 or 2,048 ports probed/opened, yielding respectively a proposed 64% or 99.9% success probability against one Endpoint-Dependent-Mapping (EDM) NAT and 0.01% when both peers are behind EDM NATs (source: Anderson 2020, cited, not independently measured by this paper)
- Endpoint-Dependent-Mapping NAT prevalence used to project the birthday-paradox improvement: approximately 11% of peers (Halkes et al. 2011, cited, not measured here); projected mixed EDM/EIM combination rate approximately 19.6%, both-EDM combination rate approximately 1.2%, yielding a projected overall DCUtR success-rate improvement of 12.5% if the birthday-paradox technique achieves 64% success on the mixed-combination subset — this projection is explicitly derived from the cited 2011 figures, not from this paper's own campaign

### Stated limitations
Client sampling is drawn from volunteers in the IPFS and libp2p communities, a group the authors state is likely more technically proficient and on more stable networks than the general P2P user base, which may overestimate the measured success rate. The honeypot discovery method only finds NATed peers reachable through a relayed connection, systematically excluding peers behind NATs or firewalls restrictive enough to block even relayed connections, which the authors state may inflate the observed success rate. The measurement observes only the outcome (success or failure) of a hole punch, not the internal NAT mapping/filtering behavior, so individual failures cannot be attributed to a specific NAT type (Cone, Symmetric, or Carrier-Grade NAT specifically); this is stated as a direction for future work. The reported 70% figure characterizes only the hole-punching stage conditional on relay reservation and address discovery already succeeding; the unconditional end-to-end success rate from scratch is lower once the roughly 29% prerequisite-stage failure rate is included. The Connection Reversal analysis (H4) is based on port-mapping self-reports from the 212 volunteer clients only, whose configurations the authors state may not be representative. The authors state the dataset cannot distinguish a single NAT layer from a Carrier-Grade NAT (an additional NAT layer some ISPs insert), because both classify identically under the outcome-only measurement.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
DCUtR requires that a relayed connection through a Circuit v2 relay already exists between the two peers before hole punching begins, so it depends on Circuit v2's own reservation and connection-establishment succeeding first (VYZOVITIS-SPECS-23); this paper's 29% prerequisite-failure figure is exactly the rate at which that dependency is not met. It requires the underlying transport to support both the initiator and listener attempting near-simultaneous outbound connection attempts to each other's advertised address (TCP simultaneous open, or QUIC's dummy-UDP-packet NAT-state priming), so a transport without a simultaneous-open-equivalent cannot use this synchronization mechanism as specified. It requires each peer to expose its externally observed address through Identify, so a peer that cannot establish even one connection to obtain this observation (i.e., is fully unreachable, not merely NATed) cannot participate. The RTT-based synchronization assumes approximately symmetric one-way network delay between the two peers' paths to each other; the paper states that when path asymmetry is significant, the synchronization margin shrinks, which the authors offer as a partial explanation for part of the residual approximately 30% failure rate. During the CONNECT exchange both peers disclose their public IP address and port to whichever peer initiates the exchange, and the paper states this creates a route for systematic address collection via repeated probing, plus a trust boundary at the relay, which can observe and correlate both parties' addresses and connection timing over time.

### Contradicts
This paper's own H3b result contradicts the commonly stated belief, which the paper itself attributes to the field's conventional understanding, that "UDP hole punching is easier than TCP" — DCUtR shows statistically indistinguishable success rates for TCP and QUIC (both approximately 70%), which the authors attribute to DCUtR's RTT-based synchronization removing the timing unpredictability that ordinarily disadvantages TCP hole punching. No other paper in this corpus reports a conflicting DCUtR-specific measurement as of this batch.
