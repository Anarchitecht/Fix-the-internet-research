## [WOLCHOK-WOOT-10] Crawling BitTorrent DHTs for Fun and Profit

**Citation:** Scott Wolchok, J. Alex Halderman. "Crawling BitTorrent DHTs for Fun and Profit." USENIX WOOT, 2010.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/woot10/tech/full_papers/Wolchok.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.usenix.org/conference/woot10/crawling-bittorrent-dhts-fun-and-profit
**Domain:** J

### What it does
The paper demonstrates that a single machine can reconstruct which peers are downloading which content from an unmodified, widely deployed distributed hash table (DHT), by inserting many fake identities into the DHT and reading the data that legitimate replication delivers to them. A distributed hash table is a decentralized key-value store built as a peer-to-peer network in which every node and every stored key share one numeric address space, and each node holds a replica of the key-value pairs whose keys are numerically close to its own address. The authors reuse an existing crawler, ClearView, a from-scratch reimplementation of the Vuze DHT's Kademlia protocol that performs a Sybil attack (inserting many falsely distinct identities into a system that assumes one identity per participant) by running thousands of DHT client identities inside a single process. After the DHT's own periodic replication delivers nearby content to these planted identities, the crawler moves its identities to a new region of the address space and repeats, so a small number of processes cover a large fraction of the keyspace over successive rounds. Applied to search, the crawler extracts "torrent description" records (metadata records some Vuze clients create linking a torrent's info hash to its name, size, and seeder/leecher counts) and peer-list records (which list of IP addresses is currently exchanging a given torrent, keyed by a hash of the info hash), and stores them in a relational database with a full-text inverted index, so a rebuilt torrent search site (the authors' SuperSeed prototype) can be brought back online after a legal takedown of a centralized search site. Applied to monitoring, the same crawl (the DHT does not expose a way to permit content location without also exposing peer identity) is run repeatedly over time and the resulting peer-list and torrent-description records are joined on info hash to build content-to-IP-address and IP-address-to-content mappings, giving a content owner a per-IP-address history of what that address downloaded, without touching any centralized tracker or torrent discovery website.

### Parameters
- ClearView hop interval: 30 minutes (increased from an original 3-minute interval after Vuze removed replicate-on-join behavior in response to a related earlier attack against the Vanish system)
- Sybil identities per hop: 4,000 (10x the identity count used in the earlier 3-minute-hop configuration, to compensate for the longer hop interval and preserve roughly the same coverage)
- Hops per crawl: 2, each 30 minutes, for a total crawl duration of about 1 hour of active hopping per crawl (81 minutes measured including startup)
- Crawl schedule: three crawls per day (5 AM, 1 PM, 9 PM EDT), aligned to the Vuze DHT's 8-hour key-value expiration time
- Database: PostgreSQL 8.3, used for storage and full-text search via its built-in keyword search facility
- Vuze DHT size relative to Mainline DHT: 25-50% as many nodes as Mainline, stated as background, not separately measured in this study

### Stated limitations
The authors explicitly did not measure what fraction of indexed torrents could actually be downloaded (some indexed torrents may have zero seeders). The coverage estimate of 13% of all torrents rests on an assumption that peer lists are uniformly distributed through the DHT keyspace and on an unverified analytic coverage model carried over from a prior paper, not on ground truth. The Vuze DHT is chosen over the larger Mainline DHT solely to avoid the added engineering complexity of supporting two DHT protocols in the crawler; the authors state the same technique should extend to Mainline but did not implement or measure it. Torrent description records exist only because a specific Vuze client feature creates them when a user downloads two torrents in the same session, so the search technique depends on that client feature's continued presence and is not a property of the DHT protocol itself. Peer identification from IP address is degraded by proxy and NAT aggregation, which the authors detect but cannot fully resolve into individual users. The paper is exploratory and offers no defense or countermeasure against the crawling attacks it demonstrates; the conclusion recommends increasing legal and content-poisoning pressure on individual infringers rather than proposing a technical fix to the DHT.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
A DHT that supports distributed content tracking by storing (info hash, peer address) pairs as key-value records cannot prevent a participant from reading those records once it holds a numerically nearby identity, because DHT replication delivers stored values to any node whose address is close enough in the keyspace, honest or not; the paper's central finding is that search-enabling replication and monitoring-enabling exposure are the same mechanism and cannot be separated ("BitTorrent DHTs cannot allow one and prevent the other"). Any system relying on this class of Kademlia-style DHT for content location must assume that any sufficiently resourced observer can enumerate a large fraction of active (content, IP address) pairs within days, using commodity hardware, without cooperation from any tracker or discovery site. A design that wants to prevent this needs either admission control that makes Sybil identity insertion costly, or a mechanism that does not deliver full peer lists to arbitrary nearby nodes.

### Contradicts
None found within this corpus. Cross-check candidate: WANG-GLOBECOM-12 measures Sybil identities actually observed in the live Mainline DHT (not Vuze) and should be checked against this paper's Sybil-insertion technique (which targets Vuze, not Mainline) for whether the two DHTs show comparable vulnerability, since they are different DHT instances with only architectural similarity.

### References worth retrieving
- foundational: Maymounkov, Mazières, "Kademlia: A peer-to-peer information system based on the XOR metric," IPTPS 2002, pp. 52-56 — the routing protocol both BitTorrent DHTs implement (already in corpus per BRIEF §7)
- foundational: Douceur, "The Sybil attack," IPTPS 2002, pp. 251-260 — defines the identity-forgery attack this paper's crawler performs
- foundational: Wolchok, Hoffmann, Heninger, Felten, Halderman, Rossbach, Waters, Witchel, "Defeating Vanish with low-cost Sybil attacks against large DHTs," NDSS 2010, pp. 37-51 — the authors' own prior paper introducing the ClearView crawler this paper reuses; contains the analytic coverage model this paper's 20%/13% coverage estimate depends on
- competing: Grunthal, "Efficient indexing of the BitTorrent distributed hash table," unpublished manuscript, Esslingen University of Applied Sciences — a concurrently developed Mainline DHT crawler using tens of low-footprint nodes rather than thousands of Sybils, reported by this paper as retrieving 500,000 new .torrent files in one week versus this paper's 400,000 in 16 days
- competing: Le Blond, Legout, Lefessant, Dabbous, Kaafar, "Spying the world from your laptop -- identifying and profiling content providers and big downloaders in BitTorrent," USENIX LEET 2010 — centralized-tracker-based monitoring the authors call most similar to their own work, collecting 148 million IP addresses and about 2 million torrents over 103 days from Pirate Bay and Mininova
- competing: Zhang, Dhungel, Wu, Ross, "Unraveling the BitTorrent ecosystem" — independent large-scale BitTorrent measurement via centralized sites; reported comparable content-category results and a 4.6-million-torrent crawl of five discovery sites used as this paper's coverage comparison point
- competing: Piatek, Kohno, Krishnamurthy, "Challenges and directions for monitoring P2P file sharing networks," USENIX HotSec 2008, pp. 1-7 — prior monitoring work via discovery sites and trackers
- attack/critique (self-referential): Geambasu, Kohno, Levy, Levy, "Vanish: Increasing data privacy with self-destructing data," USENIX Security 2009, pp. 299-314 — the system the authors' earlier Sybil-crawling technique was originally built to attack
