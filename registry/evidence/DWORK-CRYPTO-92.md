## [DWORK-CRYPTO-92] Pricing via Processing or Combatting Junk Mail
**Citation:** Cynthia Dwork, Moni Naor. "Pricing via Processing or Combatting Junk Mail." Advances in Cryptology -- CRYPTO 1992, Lecture Notes in Computer Science. DOI 10.1007/3-540-48071-4_10.
**Retrieved:** full text via https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~naor/PAPERS/pvp.pdf
**Source URL:** https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~naor/PAPERS/pvp.pdf
**Note on source text:** the extracted text on disk carries a slash character inserted after most letter pairs and inside most numerals (an artifact of the PDF-to-text conversion, not of the paper), so every quotation and numeral below was reconstructed by removing those slashes and cross-checked against the surrounding sentence for consistency.
**Domain:** I

### What it does
This paper achieves access control on a shared resource -- originally electronic mail delivery, generalized to any resource with a request identifier -- by requiring the party requesting access to first compute a function that is deliberately moderately expensive to evaluate but cheap for the recipient to check, so that sending one message costs an acceptable amount of computation while sending many messages in bulk costs proportionally more computation with no way to amortize the per-message cost across the batch. The paper calls such a function a pricing function and states three required properties: it must be moderately easy to compute (harder than a trivial computation, but not intractable); it must resist amortization, meaning computing it for many different inputs costs close to the sum of computing it separately for each one, not less; and given a candidate output y and input x, checking whether y equals f(x) must be easy. A pricing function may additionally have a shortcut: extra secret information that lets whoever holds it evaluate the function cheaply, bypassing the cost that a party without the secret must pay; the paper's application gives the shortcut to a pricing authority and its trusted agents, who use it to let a sender of a bulk mailing (a conference program committee, a product announcement) evaluate the function once cheaply for every recipient rather than paying the moderate per-message cost once per recipient. In the paper's mail system, a single global pricing function f_s with shortcut c and a hash function h are fixed and known to all users, with only the pricing authority and its trusted agents holding c; to send message m at time t to destination d, the sender computes y = f_s(h(m,t,d)) and transmits (y, m, t); the recipient's mail program accepts the message only if y = f_s(h(m,t,d)) verifies and t is close enough to the current time, and otherwise discards it. The paper presents three concrete candidate pricing functions, all built on number-theoretic hardness assumptions. The first, extracting square roots modulo a prime p, has the recipient check a candidate answer y by one multiplication (checking y^2 = x mod p), while no known algorithm computes a square root mod p in fewer than roughly log(p) multiplications, and it has no shortcut. The second, built on the Fiat-Shamir identification/signature scheme, sets a modulus N = pq for secret primes p and q and k public values that are squares mod N; verification and shortcut-based evaluation both cost about k multiplications plus one hash evaluation, while evaluation without the shortcut costs an expected 2^k iterations of guess-and-check, so k is the paper's single difference parameter and increasing k by 1 doubles the cost gap between evaluating with and without the shortcut. The third recycles a signature scheme by Ong, Schnorr, and Shamir that had been broken (in the specific, weaker sense that a moderately-expensive forging algorithm existed without recovering the private key): the pricing function's shortcut is the broken scheme's private key, verification costs about three modular multiplications, and evaluation without the shortcut uses Pollard's algorithm for forging Ong-Schnorr-Shamir signatures, which the paper states requires roughly log(N) iterations.

### Measured results
None usable as an empirical measurement -- this is a construction paper with proofs and asymptotic cost derivations, not an experimental paper; the specific operation counts recorded above are theoretical costs the authors derive from the underlying number-theoretic algorithms (multiplications, hash evaluations, algorithm iterations), not timings from a run on stated hardware.

### Parameters
- Square-root pricing function: prime modulus p, recommended length 1024 bits (stated as "a reasonable length" tied to the desired difference parameter, not derived from a specific attack cost).
- Fiat-Shamir-based pricing function: composite modulus N = pq, with p and q each recommended to be at least 512 bits at the time of writing, rising to 1024 bits each "if there is further progress in factoring algorithms" (a conditional recommendation, not a fixed value); k, the count of public squares mod N and the paper's named difference parameter, with a suggested value of k = 10. Verification and shortcut-based evaluation both cost "about k multiplications" plus one hash evaluation; the text gives a more exact expected-multiplication-count refinement of this figure immediately afterward, but the digits in that refinement are corrupted in the extracted text (the same font-encoding artifact noted above) and are not recorded here because they cannot be verified against the source PDF. Evaluation without the shortcut costs an expected 2^k iterations.
- Ong-Schnorr-Shamir-based pricing function: composite modulus N = pq (bit length not separately specified from the Fiat-Shamir case); verification cost 3 modular multiplications; evaluation-without-shortcut cost roughly log(N) iterations via Pollard's algorithm.
- Hash functions considered for both the message-hashing step and the Fiat-Shamir scheme's internal hash: DES-based constructions, MD4 and MD5 (128- or 256-bit output stated in the text, though MD5's actual output is 128 bits -- the paper's stated figure is recorded here as written), a subset-sum-based one-way hash function (Impagliazzo-Naor), and Snefru; the paper notes a then-known attack on Snefru required about 2^24 operations to find a colliding partner for a given message and states Snefru "may still be viable" at that cost, and separately notes attacks existed on simplified MD4 and pseudo-collisions in MD5 without a known full collision-finding algorithm for either at the time of writing.
- Message hash-down length for the junk-mail application: the paper suggests hashing messages down to "some reasonable length, say 512 bits" before applying the pricing function.

### Stated limitations
The paper states there is virtually no complexity theory of moderately hard functions at the time of writing, unlike the mature theory of one-way functions, and names developing such a theory as the paper's primary suggested open problem. The paper states explicitly that evaluating the pricing function serves no purpose beyond acting as a deterrent, and separately states it would be desirable, but is not solved here, to find a scheme in which the wasted computation also accomplishes something useful. The paper warns against choosing a pricing function whose apparent per-instance hardness collapses under batch precomputation, illustrated with a subset-sum-based candidate the authors themselves reject: an attacker holding a moderate amount of storage can preprocess it once (citing Schroeppel and Shamir's time-space tradeoff) and thereafter answer many instances far more cheaply than paying the per-instance cost k times, defeating the required non-amortization property; the paper does not claim any of its three adopted candidates share this weakness, only that this candidate does, and uses it as a cautionary example. The Fiat-Shamir-based scheme's disadvantage, stated by the authors, is that it requires an extra hash function beyond the number-theoretic core (unlike the plain square-root function). The paper states the consequences of a compromised (its word: "broken") pricing function are not severe in the deployed junk-mail scheme: a party that finds a cheaper way to evaluate the function either sends few messages, doing little harm, or sends many, at which point the authority can detect or suspect the compromise and change the function or its key. The paper states its frequent-correspondent list (a per-recipient local allowlist that bypasses verification entirely) lets senders known to a recipient circumvent the pricing mechanism completely, which is presented as an intended usability feature rather than a flaw, but is a complete bypass of the cost-imposition property for any sender on that list.

### Requirements it places on the rest of the system
The junk-mail application as specified requires a single pricing authority trusted by all participating users to select the pricing function and hash function and to set usage fees, and requires that authority (or its trusted agents) to be the sole holder of the shortcut secret; a design that wants shortcut-based bulk-sending without a component playing this trusted-authority role cannot use the shortcut mechanism as specified. The scheme requires every recipient's mail program to enforce a freshness check against the timestamp t bound into the pricing-function input, and requires loosely synchronized clocks between sender and recipient, since a message is discarded if t is "significantly different" from the recipient's current time; the paper gives no numeric bound for what counts as significantly different. Verification requires the recipient to already know (or be told) which pricing function f_s and hash function h were used, since both are treated as shared, publicly known system parameters distinct from the shortcut. Any component wanting to bypass the mechanism for trusted senders needs a per-recipient, locally maintained allowlist (the frequent-correspondent list) checked before the pricing-function verification step, since the paper's only stated bypass path is this list, not a property of the pricing function itself.

### Contradicts
None found. This paper is the proof-of-work construction that BACK-HASHCASH-02 (in this batch) cites as prior work its author was unaware of when hashcash was first proposed in 1997; BACK-HASHCASH-02's non-interactive hashcash construction and this paper's square-root and Fiat-Shamir-based pricing functions rest on the same general asymmetry (moderate-cost generation against cheap, typically single-operation verification) but use different underlying hard problems (partial hash-collision search here is absent -- this paper's three candidates are all number-theoretic, based on square roots, factoring-based Fiat-Shamir, and a broken Ong-Schnorr-Shamir signature scheme, none of them a hash-collision search), so the two papers' operation counts are not directly comparable figures for the same mechanism.

### References worth retrieving
- Fiat, Shamir, "How to prove yourself," CRYPTO 1986 -- foundational (the identification/signature scheme this paper's second pricing function is built on).
- Ong, Schnorr, Shamir, "An efficient signature scheme based on quadratic equations," STOC 1984, and "Efficient signature scheme based on polynomial equations," CRYPTO 1984 -- foundational (the signature scheme this paper's third pricing function recycles after it was broken).
- Pollard, Schnorr, "Solution of x^2 + ky^2 = m mod n," IEEE Trans. Information Theory, 1988 -- attack (the algorithm this paper reuses as the moderately-hard evaluation-without-shortcut procedure for its third pricing function; originally an attack breaking the Ong-Schnorr-Shamir signature scheme).
- Schroeppel, Shamir, "A T=O(2^(n/2)), S=O(2^(n/4)) algorithm for certain NP-complete problems," SIAM J. Computing 10, 1981 -- attack (the time-space tradeoff this paper cites as the reason a naive subset-sum-based pricing function fails the non-amortization requirement).
- Impagliazzo, Naor, "Cryptographic schemes provably secure as subset sum," FOCS 1989 -- foundational (source of the subset-sum-based one-way hash function candidate this paper considers for message hashing).
- Rivest, "The MD4 Message Digest Algorithm," CRYPTO 1990, and "The MD5 Message Digest Algorithm," RFC 1321, 1992 -- foundational (two of the four hash function candidates this paper considers).
- Merkle, "Fast Software One-Way Hash Function" (Snefru), Journal of Cryptology 3(1), 1990 -- foundational (a third hash function candidate this paper considers).
- Biham, Shamir, "Differential Cryptanalysis of Snefru, Khafre, REDOC-II, LOKI, and Lucifer," CRYPTO 1991 -- attack (the attack this paper cites against Snefru, noting it still costs about 2^24 operations per collision and so may remain usable for this application).
- den Boer, Bosselaers, "An attack on the last two rounds of MD4," CRYPTO 1991, and "Collisions for the compression function of MD5," EUROCRYPT 1993 -- attack (attacks this paper cites against simplified MD4 and against MD5's compression function).
- Rabin, "Digital Signatures and Public Key Functions as Intractable as Factoring," MIT/LCS Technical Memo TM-212, 1979 -- foundational (cited for the equivalence between breaking the Fiat-Shamir-style square-root construction and factoring N).
