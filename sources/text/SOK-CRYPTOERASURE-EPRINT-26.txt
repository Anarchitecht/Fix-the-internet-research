Abstract. Public permissionless ledgers are append-only by design, yet are increas-
ingly asked to host data subject to statutory erasure obligations (GDPR Art. 17,
EDPB Guidelines 02/2025). The prevailing cryptographic response, rewriting the
chain via chameleon hashes and redactable-blockchain constructions, is structurally
unavailable on Bitcoin and Ethereum mainnet. This SoK systematizes the or-
thogonal application-layer design space, in which the ledger is left untouched and
erasure is achieved by destroying the key material required to read the on-chain
bytes. We organise the literature along two axes, data locus (on-chain ciphertext,
anchor-only, or hybrid) and key custody (single-custodian, threshold, time-lock, or
witness-encryption), into a twelve-cell grid with canonical constructions from 2006–
2026. We extend standard IND-CCA security with a key-lifecycle adversary model
parametrised by HSM side-channel leakage, coercion fraction over the custody com-
mittee, and algorithmic-break horizon, and establish an equivalence proposition be-
tween the resulting Destruction-IND notion and the EDPB “render unrecoverable”
criterion.
Evaluating seven reference architectures against eleven engineering, cryptographic,
and regulatory criteria, including a production RegTech engine processing > 104
events/s, we ﬁnd that the literature has concentrated on chain-rewriting while the
application-layer branch lacks a common threat-model vocabulary. We close this
gap and identify ﬁve open problems: post-quantum equivalence, forward-secure de-
struction under leakage, decentralised lifecycle audit (with a multi-custody extension
covering encrypted mempools), composition with ZKP selective disclosure, and era-
sure under multi-regime retention conﬂict.

1 Introduction
Public permissionless ledgers are engineered to be append-only and non-redactable. This
is not an accident of implementation but a central design goal: the security of Bitcoin and
Ethereum rests on the property that history, once agreed upon, cannot be rewritten except
by violating the consensus protocol itself. Over the past decade, the same ledgers have
been re-purposed to store progressively richer data (asset provenance records, identity
credentials, supply-chain attestations, regulatory audit trails), much of which falls within
the deﬁnition of personal data under Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR). Application
contexts span KYC and self-sovereign-identity workﬂows [ 65], blockchain-anchored audit

logs [ 68, 45], and the broader question of how organisational accountability composes
with on-chain transparency [ 66]. Article 17 of GDPR grants the data subject a right to
erasure; Recital 66 and the EDPB’s Guidelines 02/2025 on processing of personal data
through blockchain technologies [31] make explicit that rendering data cryptographically
unrecoverable is an acceptable form of erasure, so long as the recovery bar is demonstrably
high.
A substantial line of cryptographic work addresses this tension by re-engineering the
ledger itself. Redactable blockchains replace the standard collision-resistant chaining hash
with a chameleon hash [10, 28] whose trapdoor allows authorised parties to recompute valid
predecessors after editing a block. This branch is now mature enough to have attracted its
own systematic literature review [ 52]. It is, however, structurally unavailable on Bitcoin
and Ethereum mainnet, where the chaining hash is ﬁxed by consensus and cannot be
replaced without a hard fork of the entire network. For the practitioner who has already
anchored data to L1 and must now respond to an Article 17 request, chameleon hashes
are not a tool; they are a diﬀerent chain.
What the practitioner does have is an orthogonal family of constructions: keep the
ledger untouched, and destroy the cryptographic material required to read the on-chain
bytes. This family is wide and old. It includes the crypto-erasure framing of Garﬁnkel
and Malan [ 36], the SoK of Reardon, Basin, and Capkun on secure deletion [ 62], threshold
secret sharing [ 67, 48], time-lock and delay encryption [ 64], witness encryption [ 38], and
a large grey literature of enterprise deployments. Yet from the point of view of a regu-
lator, a reviewer, or a cryptographer seeking to compare designs, these constructions are
rarely described in a common vocabulary: their adversary models diﬀer; their notions of
“destroyed” diﬀer; their compliance claims with Art. 17 diﬀer; and crucially, they almost
never state what a cryptographic break would have to look like for the claim of erasure to
fail.
This paper systematizes exactly that application-layer design space. We take as given
that the ledger cannot be rewritten, and we ask: under what cryptographic conditions
does destruction of key material constitute erasure of the plaintext it protects, in a sense
that survives regulatory scrutiny under the EDPB 02/2025 criteria? We answer along
two primary axes, data locus and key custody and destruction semantics , producing a
twelve-cell design grid (Figure 1) populated with representative constructions from 2006–
2026. We then introduce a key-lifecycle adversary model parameterised by HSM side-
channel leakage ε, coercion threshold k/n, and algorithmic-break horizon T . The model
admits a formal notion of Destruction-IND security and, via reduction to the EDPB
“render unrecoverable” test, a cryptographic equivalence theorem for Art. 17 compliance
(Section 5). A production reference implementation at > 104 events per second anchors
the evaluation.

2 Background & Related Systematizations
2.1 Cryptographic erasure: origins and assumptions
The idea that deletion of key material is equivalent to deletion of plaintext predates
blockchain by two decades. Boneh and Lipton’s 1996 work on revocation, and more
directly Garﬁnkel and Malan’s 2006 paper on free-space sanitisation [ 36], established the
framing that underpins this paper: rather than overwriting every copy of a ciphertext
(which is impossible once the ciphertext has been broadcast or replicated) one overwrites
only the key. NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 1 [ 57] later canonised the term cryptographic erase
and listed it as an acceptable sanitisation method for encrypted storage.
Reardon, Basin, and Capkun’s SoK on secure data deletion [ 62], subsequently ex-
panded into a monograph [ 61], remains the most complete systematization of the technique
at the storage-media layer; earlier work on regulatory-compliant storage architectures [ 21]
establishes the same key-destruction primitive in an enterprise compliance context. Their
adversary model assumes a single-device deletion primitive (physical destruction, overwrit-
ing, or key wiping) and reasons about the correctness of each under adversarial access to
residual data. The model does not anticipate data that has been intentionally and durably
replicated to an adversarial-accessible public ledger, and consequently does not supply the
vocabulary we need when the ciphertext has been broadcast to 10,000 nodes. Subsequent
work on cloud-storage assured deletion [ 60], plausibly deniable encryption combined with
deletion [ 76], memory-erasure protocols [ 22, 40], and the formal-veriﬁcation methodology
recently applied to deployed cryptographic systems such as WhatsApp group messag-
ing [ 6] and Telegram [ 8] reﬁnes the storage-layer model and the analysis toolkit, but the
underlying single-device-deletion assumption remains.
2.2 GDPR Article 17, Recital 66, and the EDPB 02/2025 guidance
Article 17 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 grants the data subject a right to erasure “without
undue delay. ” Recital 66 clariﬁes that the controller “should take reasonable steps” to
inform third parties processing the data of the erasure request, but stops short of specifying
a cryptographic criterion for what constitutes erased.
The EDPB’s Guidelines 02/2025 on processing of personal data through blockchain
technologies [31] are the ﬁrst regulatory instrument to address the tension directly. Two
passages are load-bearing for our work. First, the Guidelines acknowledge that erasure by
physical overwriting is “technically impossible” on a public permissionless ledger. Second,
and this is the opening this paper exploits, they accept rendering the data unrecoverable
through cryptographic means as an adequate substitute, provided the controller documents
the threat model under which the data is considered unrecoverable. The pre-Guidelines
legal scholarship, notably Finck’s foundational treatment of blockchains and EU data
protection [ 33] and Finck and Pallas on the personal-vs-non-personal data distinction
under GDPR [ 34], established the analytic vocabulary the EDPB text inherits; we draw
on their framing at several points below.
The Guidelines do not, however, specify what form that documentation takes, nor
how a court should evaluate it. A controller writing “we destroyed the key” today has no
formal yardstick against which to measure that claim. Supplying such a yardstick is the
role of our equivalence theorem (§ 5).

3 Design Space and Taxonomy
We organise the application-layer design space along two orthogonal axes: data locus
(where the bytes that must become unreadable physically reside) and key custody and
destruction semantics (how the capability to read those bytes is managed over its lifecycle).
The two axes together yield twelve cells, arranged as in Figure 1. In this section we
deﬁne each axis, enumerate the values it takes, and populate each cell with a canonical
construction drawn from the literature.

3.1 Axis 1: Data locus
We distinguish three loci, according to where the protected bytes are stored relative to
the ledger.
L1: on-chain ciphertext. The encrypted payload is written directly into the ledger,
typically as Ethereum calldata or smart-contract storage, where a single transaction can
carry kilobytes of payload at predictable cost. Bitcoin’s bandwidth is more constrained
(the OP_RETURN output is capped at 80 bytes; Taproot envelopes via Ordinals reach the
block witness limit but at a substantial fee premium), so on Bitcoin L1 this locus is realistic
only for short ciphertexts or via multi-transaction splitting. Rollups and L2 data blobs
that eventually settle to L1 fall under the same locus once their data is committed. Tamper
evidence is maximal: any modiﬁcation to the ciphertext is detected by any verifying node.
Erasure is maximally hard: the ciphertext remains, by consensus, for as long as the chain
exists. The entire burden of erasure falls on the key.
L2: anchor-only. The ledger stores only a commitment to the data: a cryptographic hash,
a Merkle root, a polynomial commitment, or an RSA/BLS accumulator. The ciphertext
itself lives in an oﬀ-chain encrypted object store (IPFS [ 16], S3, a self-hosted CAS); recent
work on data-availability sampling [ 44] bounds the assumptions the on-chain commitment
makes about oﬀ-chain retrievability. A practical deployment pattern is the OpenTimes-
tamps protocol [ 69], which submits SHA-256 Merkle roots to Bitcoin via public calendar
servers and produces upgradeable proofs veriﬁable from the Bitcoin block-header chain
alone; this is the anchor used by the reference implementation evaluated in § 6. Erasure
has two components: destroy the key, and delete the oﬀ-chain ciphertext if its availability
is in the controller’s power. On-chain tamper evidence remains, since the commitment is
permanent and any oﬀ-chain recovery must be matched against it.
L3: hybrid. A short header (itself possibly encrypted) is stored on-chain, and a larger
body lives oﬀ-chain, indexed by the header. The pattern is ubiquitous in decentralised
identity [ 54, 70]: a veriﬁable credential commitment lives on-chain, the credential body
oﬀ-chain. Erasure must reason about the header and the body separately; ﬁne-grained
erasure (e.g. “forget the income ﬁeld but not the passport hash”) becomes a distinct
design goal.
Design tension. L1 maximises tamper evidence but raises the recovery bar for erasure to
“the ciphertext is permanent forever. ” L2 eases erasure but relies on oﬀ-chain availability
guarantees that a regulator may ﬁnd diﬃcult to audit. L3 inherits the obligations of both
rows with additional composition risk between header and body.
3.2 Axis 2: Key custody and destruction semantics
We distinguish four custody models, enumerated by the mechanism through which the
decryption capability is destroyed.
K1: single-custodian irreversible destruction. A single key is held in a hardware security
module or an enclave with attestable destruction. “Destroy” means issue the HSM’s
zeroisation primitive and obtain an attestation. Standard in enterprise RegTech; it is the
custody model of the Nexum Ledger reference implementation evaluated in § 6.

K3: time-lock / delay encryption. The key becomes available only after a speciﬁed

drand [ 49] to release the key at a later time in a way the controller cannot pre-empt.

a key becomes derivable. We argue in Remark 3 that delay is not destruction; it is
a distinct guarantee. Recent work on timed secret sharing [46] bridges K3 and K2 by
combining a threshold committee with a time-lock on reconstruction; we treat such hybrids
as occupying the K2 column with an added timing predicate, not a ﬁfth custody model.
Concurrent work on scalable time-lock puzzles [ 2] and time-lock puzzles with veriﬁable
homomorphic operations [ 1] extends K3 along the throughput and composability axes.
K4: witness-encryption gated. The plaintext is recoverable only if a witness for some
NP predicate exists [ 38, 42]. Erasure is achieved by making the witness permanently
unprovable, e.g., destroying the one preimage that decides the predicate. Full witness
encryption for arbitrary NP today relies on indistinguishability obfuscation, which is not
engineering-ready; however, practical instantiations exist for restricted language classes,
notably Derler and Slamanig’s 2018 construction for algebraic languages under Groth–
Sahai proofs [ 27] and the recent signature-based witness-encryption with compact cipher-
text of Magri et al. [ 11]; the McFly veriﬁable encryption-to-the-future construction [ 29]
bridges K4 and K3 by gating decryption on a future signature event. K4 is therefore
not uniformly theoretical (one corner of the column is deployable) but the general case
remains out of reach. We map both the deployable corner and the ceiling in § 3.3.

3.4 The third axis: adversary parameters
The two-dimensional grid classiﬁes designs. It does not classify security claims . Two
schemes in the same cell, for instance two diﬀerent single-custodian HSM-based systems,
may make profoundly diﬀerent claims about what cryptographic break would violate the
erasure guarantee. Section 4 introduces a third, transverse axis: a three-parameter ad-
versary model (ε, k/n, T ) that captures side-channel leakage during the destruction event,
coercion resistance over the custody lifecycle, and the horizon against algorithmic break-
through. Each cell of the grid supports a range of (ε, k/n, T ) values; the equivalence
theorem in § 5 is formulated over the product.
4 Key-Lifecycle Adversary Model
4.1 Why IND-CCA is not enough
The standard IND-CCA game treats the key as a bit-string that is either in memory
or erased, and the erase step as an instantaneous, perfectly silent operation. Neither
assumption holds for key destruction on deployed systems. The destruction event itself
is the attack surface: empirical work since Garﬁnkel and Shelat’s 2003 study of disk
sanitisation [37] has documented that an issued “delete” rarely coincides with an attacker-
unrecoverable state, whether the medium is ﬂash, RAM, or HSM-resident key material;
a committee of custodians asked to erase their shares may be individually coerced; and a
ciphertext that is IND-CCA-secure today may not be IND-CCA-secure in 2045 against a
cryptographically relevant quantum adversary, while still being present on the ledger. A
meaningful deﬁnition of “destroyed” must quantify over all three.
4.2 Committee-based scheme structure
To make the coercion parameter k/n load-bearing in the security game, we model
the decryption capability as distributed across a custody committee of n parties with
reconstruction threshold t ≤ n. A scheme in our framework has the shape Π =
(Setup, Enc, Dec, Destroy):
• Setup(1λ, n, t) → (pp, sk1, . . . ,skn) produces public parameters and n share secrets.
n = t = 1 recovers single-custodian (K1); 1 < t≤ n models K2.
• Enc(pp, m)→ c uses only public parameters.
• Dec({ski}i∈S, c)→ m succeeds for any qualifying set S with|S|≥ t.
• Destroy(ski) is a per-party erasure procedure invoked locally by each honest party on
erasure request.
K3 (time-lock) is modelled by a beacon party releasing its share only after wall-clock
delay τ (distinct from the algorithmic-break horizon T in Def. 1); K4 (witness-encryption)
collapses the committee into a witness-gate. The deﬁnitions below cover K1 and K2
directly; K3/K4 extensions are discussed inline.

an adversary’s interaction with a scheme Π by three quantities:
1. ε∈ [0, 1]: the side-channel leakage bound : a per-party budget such that, across the en-
tire Destroy(ski) event, the adversary obtains at most ε·| ski| bits of information about

5 GDPR Art. 17 Equivalence
5.1 From regulatory text to cryptographic criterion
Article 17 of GDPR grants a right to erasure. Recital 66 clariﬁes the controller’s obligation
to “take reasonable steps” to propagate the erasure request. Neither the Article nor the
Recital gives a cryptographic criterion. The EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 [ 31] are the ﬁrst
instrument to do so; we extract the operative criterion below.
Deﬁnition 3 (EDPB “render unrecoverable” test, formalised) . Let a controller claim
erasure of personal data m at time t0 under a threat model T ∗ = ( ε∗, k∗/n∗, T ∗). We
formalise the EDPB 02/2025 criterion as the conjunction of three clauses, derived from
the Guidelines’ acceptance of cryptographic-erasure substitutes:
1. Ciphertext persistence admissibility . The continued existence of any ciphertext c
derived from m on the ledger is not, in itself, a violation, provided c is not decipherable
underT ∗.
2. Documented threat model. The controller documentsT ∗ in advance: the assumed
bounds on side-channel leakage during destruction, the fraction of trusted custodians,
and the horizon beyond which cryptanalytic progress is not guaranteed to be defeated.
3. Post-destruction indistinguishability . For any probabilistic polynomial-time ad-
versaryA operating within T ∗, the probability of recovering m from c after time t0
is cryptographically negligible (operationalising the Guidelines’ “render unrecoverable”
clause).
Def. 3 is our reading of the EDPB text, not a direct quote. The load-bearing move
is (3): “cryptographically negligible” is not the Guidelines’ word, but the phrase “render
unrecoverable” becomes operational only when we interpret it this way. The equivalence
theorem below formalises this move.
5.2 The equivalence theorem
Proposition 1 (Destruction-IND implies Art. 17 equivalence) . Let Π =
(Setup, Enc, Dec, Destroy) be a committee-based scheme in the sense of § 4.2, deployed
to erase personal data m under controller-declared threat modelT ∗ = (ε∗, k∗/n∗, T ∗). Let
(pp, sk1, . . . ,skn)← Setup(1λ, n, t) and c← Enc(pp, m). If Π is Destruction-IND secure
underT ∗ in the sense of Def. 2, then c, after every honest party i∈ [n]\ C has executed
Destroy(ski) at time t0, satisﬁes the EDPB 02/2025 “render unrecoverable” criterion of
Def. 3 from t0 onwards, for the duration of T ∗.

Proof (Proof sketch). We reduce each clause of Def. 3 to a property of Def. 2; full proof
in Appendix B.
Clause (1): ciphertext persistence admissibility. The EDPB admits persistence pro-
vided c is not decipherable under T ∗. Def. 2 quantiﬁes over adversaries that observe c
before and after the per-party destruction events; hence persistence of c on the ledger is
included in the adversary’s view by construction. The Destruction-IND guarantee bounds
any deciphering advantage to negl(λ).
Clause (2): documented threat model. EDPB 02/2025 requires the controller to ﬁx,
in advance, the assumed bounds on (a) information leakage during destruction, (b) the
fraction of trustworthy custodians, and (c) the cryptanalytic horizon under which recovery
is considered infeasible. Def. 1 exposes exactly three parameters (ε, k/n, T ) quantifying
(a)–(c), and Def. 2 proves security with respect to speciﬁc values of them. The mapping
from regulatory requirement to cryptographic claim is therefore an explicit functional
correspondence: the controller’s declared T ∗ is the same tuple that parametrises the
game.
Clause (3): post-destruction indistinguishability. An adversaryA operating withinT ∗
is admissible in the ExpDest-IND experiment. By Destruction-IND security, such an adver-
sary’s distinguishing advantage is negl(λ). “Cryptographically negligible” is, by standard
convention, any function of λ that vanishes faster than the inverse of any polynomial in λ.
We propose, as a framing claim rather than a legal conclusion, that this is the appropriate
operationalisation of the EDPB’s “render unrecoverable” threshold for cryptographic con-
structions, by analogy with how related EU instruments (NIS Directive technical guidance,
eIDAS regulatory standards) operationalise similar terms via quantitative cryptographic
thresholds. Whether a court would accept this identiﬁcation is outside the scope of this
paper and a question for the legal-technology community.
5.3 What the theorem does and does not say
What the theorem says. A controller who documents T ∗ and deploys a scheme with a
proof of Destruction-IND under T ∗ has, under our interpretation of the EDPB criterion,
a defensible claim of Art. 17 erasure.
What the theorem does not say.
1. It does not say that Destruction-IND is necessary for Art. 17 equivalence. Other
cryptographic or non-cryptographic routes to “render unrecoverable” are possible (e.g.
physical destruction of an air-gapped oﬀ-chain ciphertext store, which we scoped out
in § 1.1).
2. It does not give the controller a free choice of T ∗. A controller who documents ε∗ = 1
(unbounded side-channel leakage) technically satisﬁes Def. 3 vacuously, but a court
will not ﬁnd this defensible. The theorem is silent on what values of T ∗ are regulator-
defensible; Appendix A tabulates per-industry defaults as a conjecture.
3. It does not bridge the gap between proven Destruction-IND and deployed Destruction-
IND. Implementation bugs, misconﬁgured HSMs, and social-engineered coercion are
outside the model. Destruction-IND is a property of a scheme, not of a deployment.
4. It does not say that a cryptographically-erased ciphertext satisﬁes sectoral record-
keeping mandates. Art. 17 erasure (“can the data subject compel erasure?”) and
sectoral retention (“must the controller preserve a readable record?”) are distinct reg-
ulatory regimes. A destroyed-key ciphertext that satisﬁes Def. 3 is, by construction,
no longer a readable record, and therefore does not on its own discharge a retention
mandate such as the SRA Accounts Rules or anti-money-laundering retention under

2. DeclareT ∗ = (ε∗, k∗/n∗, T ∗) with reference to Table 3.
3. (Target.) Adopt a construction with a Destruction-IND proof underT ∗. Where no such

6.4 Walk-through
Three observations organise the table.
Observation 1: only A4 fully satisﬁes all three regulatory criteria; A2 and A3 satisfy
them subject to L2 availability and vendor-trust caveats. A0 trivially fails. A1 (hash-
only anchor) provides no cryptographic erasure guarantee, since it relies entirely on the oﬀ-
chain controller honouring the delete request, which the EDPB 02/2025 criterion explicitly
declines to accept as adequate when the data was committed on-chain. A5 (time-lock)
fails C10 because delay is not destruction (Remark 3). A6 (WE) has weak C9, since third-
party veriﬁcation that a preimage has been destroyed is an open problem and reduces to
proving a non-existence statement. A2 and A3 both sit at L2 and thus inherit the oﬀ-chain-
availability-failure mode (the encrypted store may become unreachable independently of
the on-chain anchor). A3 speciﬁcally uses Bitcoin via OpenTimestamps for its anchor and
an HSM-resident master key; its C9 entry reads “vendor” rather than “on-chain” because
HSM zeroisation attestation depends on trusting the HSM vendor and its certiﬁcation
chain (FIPS 140-3, Common Criteria), not on a primitive a third party can verify against
the ledger. A4 genuinely outperforms A3 on C9 by replacing vendor-trust with on-chain
quorum-refusal proofs.

release policies (reveal at τ ), not erasure policies (forget by τ ). Hybrid constructions that
combine time-lock with destruction (e.g., destroying the VDF input before the beacon
output is reached) recover the erasure semantics but are closer to K2 (committee-gated)
than to K3 (delay-only).

7 Open Problems
The two-axis taxonomy (§ 3), the destruction-oracle adversary model (§ 4), and the eval-
uation (§ 6) together expose ﬁve gaps. Each is a concrete research question with, in our
estimate, a multi-paper agenda.
7.1 OP1: Post-quantum Destruction-IND under long horizons
The T parameter of the destruction oracle (Def. 1) assumes the adversary’s computational
budget is bounded for T years by current best-known algorithms. Every row of Table 4
shows “partial” under C7 because AES-256-GCM ciphertext anchored today is exposed
to Grover speedup, which halves the eﬀective key length, while the secp256k1 anchor
signatures used by both Bitcoin and Ethereum are exposed to Shor. A controller who
declares erasure today under T ∗ =∞ is, strictly, making a claim that current primitives

Yitebeier Aikebaier 17
do not discharge. The open problem is: can a deployable scheme achieve Destruction-
IND for T =∞ against a quantum adversary? Plausible routes include (i) symmetric
encryption with≥ 384-bit keys (sized so that Grover-halved security still exceeds 192 bits);
(ii) hybrid encryption combining a NIST-standardised post-quantum KEM (e.g., ML-
KEM [56]) with an AEAD; (iii) committing the ciphertext under a post-quantum signature
scheme [7] or a post-quantum threshold ring signature [ 74] so that Shor on the anchor does
not enable retroactive forgery; (iv) using L WE-based updatable encryption [ 5] to refresh
ciphertext under post-quantum-secure key rotation, with destruction implemented as the
absence of further updates. The roadmap of post-quantum blockchain primitives more
broadly is surveyed by Fernández-Caramés and Fraga-Lamas [ 32]. No current public-
ledger deployment executes any of these; the engineering cost of migration is itself a
research question.
7.2 OP2: Forward-secure destruction under non-zero ε
The destruction oracle admits ε > 0, meaning the adversary observes some fraction of key
material during the Destroy call. Standard IND-CCA analysis breaks at ε > 0; we need
a composable notion of forward-secure destruction in which leakage during the destroy
event does not retrospectively compromise plaintext encrypted before the destroy. Par-
tial progress exists in the leakage-resilient cryptography literature; puncturable PRFs [ 14],
puncturable signatures with proof-of-stake applications [ 50], and key-extraction-aware sig-
natures [ 71] furnish primitives whose security deﬁnitions are dual to the destruction prob-
lem and may transfer with adaptation. Even so, none of this work targets the destroy
step speciﬁcally. A thesis-sized question: what is the minimum leakage budget ε un-
der which a given scheme’s Destruction-IND still holds, and how does it compose with
side-channel-resistant hardware?
7.3 OP3: Decentralised key-lifecycle audit
Criterion C9 in Table 4 shows that only K2 (threshold) architectures admit on-chain de-
struction audit without the custodian’s cooperation. K1 relies on HSM vendor attestation;
K3 relies on beacon veriﬁability; K4 reduces to proving a non-existence statement which
is not currently feasible. The open problem is: construct a destruction-audit protocol that
is sound without a trusted third party , applicable to any custody model. Recent work
on traceable threshold encryption without a trusted dealer [ 19] is a partial advance on
the dealer side but does not address destruction auditability. Orthogonal work on ex-
plainable deletion [ 59] characterises what a controller’s audit trail must communicate to
a non-expert data subject; a mature destruction-audit protocol will have to satisfy both
security (cryptographic soundness) and explainability constraints.
Multi-custody extension. Emerging deployment patterns multiply the custody sur-
faces a destruction-audit protocol must cover. Encrypted-mempool designs [ 35, 47], the
universally-composable order-fairness extensions of Kiayias and collaborators [ 26], and re-
cent measurements of mempool-based transaction auditing [ 4] introduce transaction-intent
ciphertexts whose decryption keys are held by a diﬀerent party set than the application-
layer keys; once the intent is included on-chain, its ciphertext persists, and an end-to-end
audit must reason about the intent key, the application key, and the block-producer key
as a single lifecycle. The destruction oracle of § 4.3 extends naturally to multi-custody
by parametrising the coercion budget per custody layer; we leave the formalisation as a
sub-problem of OP3.

7.4 OP4: Composability with ZKP selective disclosure
Modern identity deployments combine encrypted credentials with ZKP selective disclo-
sure: a holder proves a predicate (“I am over 18”) without revealing the underlying data.
C11 asks whether the erasure semantics compose with such disclosure. The problem is
non-trivial: a SNARK over an encrypted statement whose key has been destroyed may
be veriﬁable (if the statement is baked into the SNARK) while the plaintext is unrecov-
erable. Conversely, a SNARK instrument may inadvertently preserve information that
the plaintext destruction was intended to erase. Recent constructions of blockchain-based
threshold proxy re-encryption with zero-knowledge proofs [ 63] illustrate the engineering
shape of such compositions without a security reduction against destruction. The open
problem: deﬁne selective-destruction semantics that compose cleanly with ZKP selective
disclosure in veriﬁable-credential architectures.
7.5 OP5: Erasure under multi-regime retention conﬂict
The equivalence of § 5 concerns erasure under GDPR Art. 17. It is silent on the case where
the personal data is embedded within a record that a sectoral mandate independently
compels the controller to retain in readable form for a ﬁxed period: the SRA Accounts
Rules for UK legal practices, anti-money-laundering retention under the UK MLR 2017,
or insurer-driven limitation-period retention. Destroying the key satisﬁes Def. 3, but for
the sectoral regime a destroyed-key ciphertext is no longer “a record” at all. This is a
conﬂict of law, not a cryptographic ﬂaw, and the taxonomy exposes two sub-problems.
(a) Construct a scheme achieving Destruction-IND erasure of the personal-data subset of
a record while preserving its non-personal skeleton as a readable, sectorally-valid artefact:
an open question once one accepts that ﬁeld-level key partitioning may leave the residual
record incomplete for sectoral purposes. (b) Formalise a decision-audit alternative: a con-
struction that does not erase the mandatorily-retained record during its retention period,
but produces an immutable, independently-veriﬁable log of the controller’s per-record re-
tention basis and Art. 17(3) exemption rationale, with cryptographic erasure applied only
after the sectoral retention period expires. Sub-problem (b) admits a Destruction-IND-
style formal treatment of the post-retention erasure step; whether the decision-audit log
itself discharges the Art. 17 obligation is a question for the legal-technology community
in the sense of § 5.
Summary
Each open problem is directly addressable with the taxonomy and adversary model intro-
duced here. OP1 motivates a post-quantum follow-up paper. OP2–OP5 are candidates
for theory and systems papers respectively; they are named with cross-references so sub-
sequent work can cite this SoK as the framing.
8 Conclusion
Article 17 of GDPR and the EDPB 02/2025 Guidelines together ask a question that
cryptography has not yet answered in common terms: when is the destruction of a key
equivalent to the destruction of the plaintext it protects, on a ledger one cannot rewrite?
The answer depends on where the bytes live, who holds the keys, what can leak during
destruction, how many custodians can be coerced, and over what horizon the underlying
primitives remain secure.
This paper systematizes the application-layer design space in which the answer can be
given. A two-axis taxonomy (§ 3) organises twelve architectural patterns, each with canon-
ical instantiations, revealing empty cells and crowded ones. A three-parameter adversary

Yitebeier Aikebaier 19
model (§ 4) extends standard indistinguishability with a destruction oracle parameterised
by side-channel leakage, coercion fraction, and break horizon. An equivalence theorem
(§5) identiﬁes Destruction-IND under a declared threat model with the EDPB “render un-
recoverable” criterion, furnishing controllers with the ﬁrst formal yardstick against which
to measure their Art. 17 compliance claim. An evaluation of seven reference architectures
(§6) locates the practically defensible region of the grid.
We ﬁnd the literature crowded on chain-rewriting (chameleon-hash redaction) and
sparse on the application-layer branch most relevant to Bitcoin and Ethereum deploy-
ments. We ﬁnd that no published system currently executes the full procedure (pick a
cell, declare T ∗, prove Destruction-IND, ﬁle the declaration) with a formal proof. We
name ﬁve open problems (§ 7) that directly extend our framework: post-quantum equiv-
alence, forward-secure destruction under leakage, decentralised lifecycle audit (with a
multi-custody extension to encrypted mempools), composition with ZKP selective disclo-
sure, and the conﬂict between Art. 17 erasure and sectoral retention mandates.
Our reference implementation is a production RegTech engine anchored to Bitcoin via
OpenTimestamps. We hope it, and the framework in this paper, narrow the gap between
what regulators ask of controllers and what cryptographers can prove.
