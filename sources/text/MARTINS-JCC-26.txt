## Introduction

### Context and motivation

According to the Matrix.org Foundation, as a concept, _“the Matrix is an open protocol for decentralized, secure communications, developed to enable interoperability between different communication systems”_ [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")]. As digital communication continues to evolve, the importance of secure and private interactions has never been more significant [[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR2 "Schipper GC, Seelt R, Le-Khac NA (2021) Forensic analysis of Matrix protocol and Riot.Im application. Forensic Sci Int: Digit Investigation 36:301118. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsidi.2021.301118
                  
                ")]. Matrix offers a robust framework that prioritizes user control and data sovereignty, allowing individuals and organizations to maintain ownership of their communications. This is especially important in a world where centralized platforms often pose risks related to data breaches and unauthorized surveillance. The protocol’s unique ability to provide secure, real-time communication across a federated network of independent servers makes it highly adaptable to the needs of modern distributed systems. This adaptability is further enhanced by Matrix’s support for a wide range of communication types, including instant messaging, voice-over-IP (VoIP), and even video conferencing. Unlike traditional centralized messaging platforms, Matrix enables interoperability between different services, allowing users to communicate seamlessly across various networks [[3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR3 "Jacob F, Becker L, Grashöfer J, Hartenstein H (2020) Matrix decomposition: analysis of an access control approach on transaction-based DAGs without finality. This interoperability is a key differentiator that positions Matrix as a forward-thinking solution in the landscape of digital communication. By breaking down the silos typically created by proprietary messaging systems, Matrix fosters a more connected and inclusive communication environment, where users are not restricted by the boundaries of specific platforms.

As a result of these strengths, the popularity of the Matrix protocol is rapidly increasing, with its adoption spreading across a variety of applications, from secure messaging and VoIP services to decentralized collaboration tools [[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR4 "Alatawi M, Saxena N (2023) SoK: an analysis of end-to-end encryption and authentication ceremonies in secure messaging systems. ### Research problem

A federated architecture, like Matrix, enables decentralized and interconnected server operations. In this model, each server maintains autonomy while exchanging information with other network servers, creating a distributed system without a central control point. Combined with the necessity for interoperability across various platforms, makes it difficult to perform comprehensive evaluations that can guide the development and optimization of Matrix-based systems [[9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR9 "Weidner M, Kleppmann M, Hugenroth D, Beresford AR (2021) Key Agreement for decentralized secure group messaging with strong security guarantees. In Proceedings of the 2021 ACM SIGSAC Conference on Computer and Communications Security, CCS’21, New York, NY, USA, 2024–2045. Association for Computing Machinery. event-place: Virtual Event, Republic of Korea")]. Existing research often provides fragmented insights, lacking a unified framework for assessing the protocol’s performance under different conditions and usage scenarios. As organizations increasingly adopt Matrix for critical communication needs, the absence of a structured approach to evaluate its effectiveness and security hinders the ability to fully capitalize on its potential. The lack of standardized metrics and methodologies for assessing Matrix’s performance in real-world deployments further complicates efforts to optimize its usage. This problem underscores the urgent need for a systematic mapping study that consolidates existing knowledge, identifies the critical aspects of Matrix evaluation, and provides a clear direction for future research. By addressing these challenges, this study aims to establish a foundation that can support more effective and informed use of the Matrix protocol in a wide range of applications.

### Contribution

To address these challenges, this paper presents a comprehensive and focused systematic mapping study dedicated exclusively to the Matrix protocol—an area that has not yet been extensively explored in the literature. Rather than merely listing existing works, our study provides a structured classification and critical analysis of how the protocol has been adopted, evaluated, and discussed in academic contexts. The main contributions of this study include:

*   A thorough identification and categorization of 156 papers, leading to the selection of 21 primary studies that directly engage with the Matrix protocol;

*   A novel classification framework that maps each study in terms of its relationship with the protocol, research area, key contributions, and explored features;

*   A detailed cross-analysis that reveals prevailing research trends, underexplored areas, and critical challenges that hinder the broader adoption and evaluation of Matrix;

*   The provision of visual and tabular syntheses to support the understanding of the current research landscape and facilitate future comparative studies;

*   An evidence-based discussion that informs and guides researchers and practitioners about directions for future work on decentralized, secure communication systems.

## Overview of the Matrix protocol

Matrix protocol, an open standard for secure and decentralized communication, is gaining significant attention from academia, industry, and governments. Matrix builds upon existing components to establish an open protocol that empowers users to have full control over their data and communication. It is important to explore the historical context that influenced its development to understand its impact and usefulness. Additionally, we have examined the architecture that supports its distributed nature and the various use cases and applications that have emerged across different sectors. This section covers the evolution of the Matrix protocol and examines its technical framework. It also highlights the wide range of implementations demonstrating flexibility and adaptability in today’s communication landscape.

### Historical context

Matthew Hodgson and Amandine Le Pape are the co-founders of Matrix. Both initially worked at Amdocs, where they conceived Matrix to provide a more open and secure framework for real-time communication. The central idea was to create a decentralized communication infrastructure enabling interoperability between different messaging services. This approach aims to overcome the limitations of centralized protocols that create “communication silos” [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication.

### Architecture and functioning of the matrix protocol

The Matrix protocol facilitates decentralized, secure, and interoperable communication across various devices and platforms. Its architecture relies on several core components and mechanisms that ensure efficient network data synchronization, user discovery, and message integrity.

#### General structure of the Matrix protocol

The general structure of the Matrix protocol is based on a federated architecture, where multiple independent servers, known as **homeservers**, communicate with each other to synchronize data and messages across the network. Each homeserver is an independent entity that can be run by anyone—from individuals to large organizations—allowing for a truly decentralized communication infrastructure. This architecture contrasts with traditional centralized models, where a single server or a cluster of servers controlled by a central authority handles all data and message routing.

In the Matrix ecosystem, each user is associated with a specific homeserver, their primary point of presence in the network. This homeserver stores the user’s messages, profile data, contact lists, and other relevant information. When users register on the Matrix network, they choose a homeserver to register their accounts. All the user’s data, including messages, media, and room memberships, are stored on this homeserver. Importantly, users on different homeservers can still communicate seamlessly because homeservers are designed to federate with one another, synchronizing data to ensure a consistent global state across the Matrix network [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")]. Homeservers communicate using the **Server-Server API** (Federation API), which defines the protocols and methods for exchanging events, messages, and states between servers. This dual-API structure ensures a clear separation between client communication and server-to-server synchronization, contributing to the scalability and resilience of the network [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")].

#### End-to-end message lifecycle and server synchronization

When a user sends a message in Matrix, the process begins on the client device. The message is first encrypted using the Olm protocol (for one-to-one communication) or Megolm (for group communication). In both cases, encryption occurs locally, and only the intended recipients hold the necessary keys to decrypt the message [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")].

After encryption, the message is signed and sent to the sender’s homeserver. The homeserver serializes it as a JSON event and assigns it a unique identifier. This event is stored and then propagated to other homeservers participating in the same room using the Server-Server API, which handles federation across the network [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")].

Each homeserver that receives the event first verifies its digital signature, generated by the origin server using Ed25519 keys. If the signature is valid, the event is integrated into the server’s local copy of the room’s event history. Matrix structures this history as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), where each event references one or more previous events. This model allows for asynchronous message propagation and enables each server to build a consistent causal structure of the room’s evolution [[3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR3 "Jacob F, Becker L, Grashöfer J, Hartenstein H (2020) Matrix decomposition: analysis of an access control approach on transaction-based DAGs without finality. In Proceedings of the 25th ACM Symposium on Access Control Models and Technologies, SACMAT’20, Association for Computing Machinery. event-place, New York, NY, USA,: Barcelona, Spain., 81–92"), [11](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR11 "Jacob F, Beer C, Henze N, Hartenstein H (2021) Analysis of the Matrix event Graph Replicated data type. IEEE Access 9:28317–28333. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2021.3058576
                  
                "), [12](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR12 "Jacob F, Grashöfer J, Hartenstein H (2019 December) A glimpse of the Matrix: scalability issues of a new message-oriented data synchronization middleware. In Proceedings of the 20th International Middleware Conference Demos and Posters, Middleware’19, Association for Computing Machinery, New York, NY, USA, 5–6")].

Conflicts may occur when events arrive out of order or in parallel, particularly under network delays or partitioning. In these cases, Matrix applies a deterministic state resolution algorithm. The algorithm uses attributes such as event depth, timestamps, and signature validity to determine the authoritative state of the room. This process allows all non-malicious homeservers to eventually converge on the same room state, without requiring centralized consensus [[3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR3 "Jacob F, Becker L, Grashöfer J, Hartenstein H (2020) Matrix decomposition: analysis of an access control approach on transaction-based DAGs without finality. In Proceedings of the 25th ACM Symposium on Access Control Models and Technologies, SACMAT’20, Association for Computing Machinery. event-place, New York, NY, USA,: Barcelona, Spain., 81–92"), [11](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR11 "Jacob F, Beer C, Henze N, Hartenstein H (2021) Analysis of the Matrix event Graph Replicated data type. IEEE Access 9:28317–28333. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2021.3058576
                  
                ")].

This mechanism ensures that Matrix supports decentralized communication with strong consistency properties. It guarantees that all participants, regardless of which server they connect to, will see a coherent and verifiable conversation history [[13](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR13 "Albrecht MR, Celi S, Dowling B, Jones D (2023 May) Practically-exploitable cryptographic vulnerabilities in Matrix. In 2023 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (SP), pp 164–181.ISSN:2375-1207"), [14](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR14 "Li H, Wu Y, Huang R, Mi X, Hu C, Guo S (2023 December) Demystifying decentralized Matrix communication network: ecosystem and security. In 2023 IEEE 29th International Conference on Parallel and Distributed Systems (ICPADS), pp 260–267. ISSN:2690-5965")].

The Client-Server API manages the authentication process when a user logs in or registers on the Matrix network. It verifies the user’s credentials against the homeserver database, ensuring proper authentication and authorization for accessing the network. Additionally, users can send messages to individuals or groups through their client applications. The Client-Server API transmits these messages to the user’s homeserver, which then propagates them to other homeservers through federation. Incoming messages are similarly retrieved from the homeserver and displayed in real-time on the user’s client. Users can manage their participation in rooms by creating, joining, or leaving rooms, with the API offering tools for managing room memberships, permissions, and administrative tasks such as inviting or banning users. The Client-Server API lets users update profile information, such as display names, avatars, and status messages. It manages presence information, indicating whether a user is online, offline, or idle, facilitating real-time communication.

The Client-Server API supports E2EE to ensure secure communications. It encrypts messages on the sender’s device, allowing only the intended recipient to decrypt them, thereby guaranteeing privacy and security in all communications. Playing a key role in maintaining the integrity and efficiency of the Matrix network, the API standardizes how clients interact with homeservers and ensures compatibility across various client applications and devices. This compatibility enables users to select the client that best fits their needs without losing access to the network’s full capabilities. The API further enhances the user experience by supporting push notifications, media uploading, and content filtering features. These capabilities ensure that the Matrix ecosystem remains adaptable and can serve diverse user requirements, ranging from casual messaging to professional collaboration.

#### Servers and federation API

The synchronization process relies on the Server-Server API, also known as the Federation API. Servers, or homeservers, are the backbone of the Matrix protocol’s decentralized architecture. Each homeserver is an independent server that stores and manages user data, including messages, media, user profiles, and room information. Homeservers are responsible for maintaining the state of the rooms where their users participate and ensuring that this state is synchronized with other homeservers across the network.

From a technical standpoint, a room consists of a sequence of JSON objects. Figure [2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#Fig2) illustrates a room with simplified events. The clients are responsible for correctly reading, parsing, and displaying these events.

When a user sends a message, it is stored on the sender’s homeserver and then propagated to other homeservers via the Server-Server API. The process includes several key functions: Event Sending, Room Joining and Leaving, State Synchronization, and Event Authentication and Verification. In Event Sending, homeservers share events, such as messages or state updates, with other homeservers that have users participating in the same room, ensuring that all involved servers have the latest events. For Room Joining and Leaving, when a user joins or exits a room, their homeserver informs the other homeservers to ensure the room state is updated network-wide.

In the State Synchronization process, homeservers periodically synchronize room states to maintain consistency, comparing states, resolving conflicts, and agreeing on a common event history through a DAG model. Additionally, Event Authentication and Verification ensures the integrity and authenticity of events received from other homeservers. Each homeserver verifies events using cryptographic signatures, confirming that the data has not been altered during transmission and comes from legitimate sources. This combination of processes helps maintain a secure, synchronized, and consistent network across all homeservers.

The Server-Server API is essential for enabling federation, the core mechanism that allows different homeservers to communicate and collaborate in a decentralized manner. By facilitating seamless synchronization and communication between homeservers, the Server-Server API ensures that the Matrix network remains resilient, scalable, and consistent, even during network disruptions or server outages [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")].

Figure [3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#Fig3) demonstrates three homeservers maintaining synchronization of their local copies of a room. In this configuration, each homeserver keeps its own version of the room’s state, ensuring that all updates are propagated across the servers. When an event occurs in the room, it is shared among the homeservers, which then update their local states accordingly to remain in sync.

#### Event-based communication model

Matrix operates on an event-based communication model, where every action within the network is captured as an event. These events cover a wide range of actions, including sending messages, joining rooms, updating profiles, or modifying room settings. Each event is carefully timestamped and stored in chronological order, ensuring that all participants in a room observe the same sequence of messages and actions, regardless of the homeserver they are connected to. This structure guarantees a consistent and synchronized experience across the network, allowing users to follow the conversation’s flow seamlessly, no matter their location or server connection.

In Matrix, events are represented as immutable objects within a DAG. The DAG structure enables each event to reference one or more preceding events, creating a chain that establishes the order of occurrences. This model allows homeservers to maintain a coherent view of the room’s state without requiring a central authority to manage the sequence of actions. As a result, the Matrix network ensures data integrity and coherence across its distributed architecture, supporting a decentralized and resilient system for communication. The event-based DAG model enhances reliability and transparency, enabling efficient collaboration and secure interactions throughout the network.

#### Security

Security is one of the main pillars of the Matrix protocol, with E2EE being a critical feature. E2EE ensures that messages are encrypted locally before transmission. Only the recipient, holding the correct decryption key, can access the original content. Even if intercepted during transfer, the content of the communication remains secure and unreadable by unauthorized parties [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")]. Table [5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#Tab5) summarizes the cryptographic functions employed by the Matrix protocol, detailing its core mechanisms. Each key type – Ed25519, Curve25519, Olm, and Megolm – plays a role in securing and maintaining the integrity of communications within the Matrix network.

Matrix uses the Olm and Megolm cryptographic ratchets to implement E2EE. Olm is used for one-to-one communication, while Megolm is designed for group communication. These ratchets ensure forward secrecy, meaning that even if one encryption key is compromised, past and future communications remain secure [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")]. The Olm and Megolm protocols rely on cryptographic primitives like Ed25519 and Curve25519 to achieve their security properties. Ed25519 is used for digital signatures, providing a secure method to authenticate the sender of a message. Curve25519, on the other hand, is used for key exchange, allowing two parties to securely establish a shared secret over an insecure channel [[17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR17 "Bernstein DJ (2006) Curve25519: new diffie-hellman speed records. 
                  https://cr.yp.to/ecdh/curve25519-20060209.pdf
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-01")].

In addition to E2EE, Matrix employs other security measures, such as device verification and cross-signing. Device verification allows users to confirm the authenticity of each other’s devices by comparing unique device fingerprints. This process ensures that users communicate with the correct devices, preventing man-in-the-middle (MITM) attacks. Cross-signing enables users to verify a new device once and have that verification apply to all their other devices automatically, streamlining the security process across multiple devices [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")]. Matrix also prioritizes transparency and auditability. The protocol’s decentralized nature ensures that no single entity can alter or delete data without consensus, and all actions are recorded as events, providing a clear audit trail. This auditability is crucial for maintaining trust in a decentralized system, as it allows users to verify that their data has not been tampered with [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")].

#### Security boundaries in federated environments

Matrix allows users to operate their own homeservers, which increases autonomy over infrastructure and key management. However, this does not imply full data ownership. Due to federation, messages are often replicated to other homeservers participating in the same room. This replication reduces the level of control an individual user has over their data, particularly in public or multi-party conversations [[18](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR18 "Moerel L, Timmers P (2021) Digital sovereignty and the open strategic autonomy of europe. SSRN Electron J 10(2139/ssrn.3776179)")].

Forward secrecy in Matrix ensures that past messages remain confidential even if current or future encryption keys are compromised. However, this mechanism does not protect future communication. It prevents retrospective decryption but not prediction or interception of messages yet to be sent [[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR1 "Matrix F (2024) An open network for secure, decentralised communication. 
                  https://matrix.org/
                  
                . Accessed: 2024-08-25")].

Matrix does not offer strong metadata protection by default. Elements such as message timestamps, room membership, and homeserver domains can be inferred through traffic analysis [[19](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR19 "Brunila M, McConnell M, Grigg S, Appuhn M, Sumner B, Bohman M (2022) Drift: E2EE Spatial feature sharing & instant messaging. In Proceedings of the 6th ACM SIGSPATIAL International Workshop on Location-Based Recommendations, Geosocial Networks and Geoadvertising, LocalRec’22, New York, NY, USA.: Seattle, Washington. Association for Computing Machinery. event-place")]. While message contents remain encrypted, metadata exposure remains a challenge for privacy-sensitive use cases. On the client side, protection mechanisms vary across implementations. Typically, encrypted messages are stored locally, and key handling is done using secure enclaves or encrypted key stores when available.

## Methodology

### RQ02 - Main area of study

Figure [8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#Fig8) shows the papers according to their main area of study. The topic of **Security and Privacy** was addressed in twelve papers, which discuss a wide range of measures to protect user data and ensure secure communication channels. These measures include encryption, which involves encoding data to prevent unauthorized access, authentication processes to verify the identity of users, and access control mechanisms to manage and restrict user permissions. Most papers addresses **Security and Privacy** category. We believe that the Matrix communication model naturally raises security concerns. In the Matrix federated model, different homeservers host users who can communicate with each other even if they are not connected to the same server. While federation promotes decentralization, it also introduces significant challenges, especially when untrusted servers are involved. Malicious or compromised third-party servers can manipulate messages or metadata, compromising the security and privacy of users. To mitigate these risks, Matrix implements E2EE, ensuring that only end users can access message content. However, potentially malicious servers in the federation are a constant concern as they can harm data integrity or compromise cryptographic key verification. Additionally, Matrix allows users to connect with other communication networks using bridges to interact with external protocols like IRC or XMPP. Despite enhancing interoperability, these bridges introduce new attack vectors, where communication security may be weakened, especially if the target protocol offers a different level of protection than Matrix. Therefore, great interest is expected regarding **Security and Privacy** issues.

## Research challenges and directions

The adoption of the Matrix protocol in decentralized communication systems presents both opportunities and challenges, particularly in ensuring seamless integration and optimized performance across various domains. As the protocol continues to gain traction, several critical challenges emerge, demanding focused research and innovative solutions. These difficulties are a result of the decentralized architectures’ inherent complexity, the necessity of strong security and privacy safeguards, the high performance demands of real-time communication, and the need to scale efficiently while preserving interoperability across various platforms and applications. Understanding these challenges is essential for researchers and developers aiming to enhance the Matrix protocol’s implementation in real-world environments. In this section, we explore the key research areas that need attention, highlighting existing barriers and potential directions for future studies. By addressing the pressing concerns of security and privacy, optimizing real-time communication processes, tackling scalability and performance issues, and ensuring interoperability, this study aims to provide a foundation for further advancements in decentralized communication technologies. The following subsections present each of these critical areas, offering insights into current research gaps and the directions in which the Matrix protocol’s development can be steered to foster broader adoption and integration.

### Security and privacy

_Cryptographic vulnerabilities_: Cryptographic vulnerabilities present a significant challenge in the Matrix protocol, especially when dealing with weak authentication methods, susceptibility to MITM attacks, and protocol confusion issues. Weak authentication can lead to unauthorized access, allowing malicious actors to impersonate legitimate users or systems, thus compromising communication security. Albrecht et al. [[13](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR13 "Albrecht MR, Celi S, Dowling B, Jones D (2023 May) Practically-exploitable cryptographic vulnerabilities in Matrix. In 2023 IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (SP), pp 164–181.ISSN:2375-1207")] analyzed attacks like MITM. The susceptibility to MITM attacks is particularly concerning in decentralized networks like Matrix, where the flow of information between servers and clients can be intercepted if encryption is not properly implemented. In these cases, attackers can manipulate or eavesdrop on the communication without the knowledge of the communicating parties. Wichelmann et al. [[31](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13677-025-00829-7#ref-CR32 "Wichelmann J, Berndt S, Pott C, Eisenbarth T (2021) Help, my Signal has bad Device! Breaking the Signal Messenger’s Post-Compromise Security Through a Malicious device. In Detection of Intrusions and Malware, and Vulnerability Assessment: 18th International Conference, DIMVA 2021, Virtual Event, July 14–16, 2021, Proceedings, Springer-Verlag, Berlin, Heidelberg, 88–105")] discuss protocol confusion, where different implementations of a protocol handle messages inconsistently, also introduces risks, as attackers may exploit these discrepancies to create vulnerabilities or bypass security measures. These vulnerabilities highlight the need for stronger cryptographic mechanisms and standardized practices across the Matrix ecosystem to ensure robust security. Without addressing these issues, the protocol remains vulnerable to attacks that can undermine its decentralized and secure communication objectives.
