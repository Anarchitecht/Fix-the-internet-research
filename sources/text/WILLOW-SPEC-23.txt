Intersecting these Areas yields the sets of AuthorisedEntries for which they then need to bring each other up to speed. In this document, we present a strategy for doing so efficiently. Given the AuthorisedEntries that the peers have available, there can be two cases that necessitate data exchange. First, one peer might have an AuthorisedEntry that the other does not have, and second, the peers might hold nonequal parts of the Payload of some common AuthorisedEntry . As a first step to solving the problem, we simplify it. If AuthorisedEntries contained information about locally available Payload bytes, then both cases would merge into a single case: one peer might have a datum that the other lacks. Hence, we do not synchronise AuthorisedEntries directly, but LengthyAuthorisedEntries : 1 An AuthorisedEntry together with information about how much of its Payload a peer holds. 2 struct LengthyAuthorisedEntry { 3   The AuthorisedEntry in question. 4   entry : AuthorisedEntry , 5   The number of consecutive bytes from the start of the entry ’s Payload that the peer holds. 6   available : U64 , 7 } The task of the two peers then becomes conceptually simple: they each have a set of LengthyAuthorisedEntries , and they need to inform each other about all LengthyAuthorisedEntries the other party does not have, that is, they each need to compute the union of their two sets. In the scientific literature, this problem is known as set reconciliation   ( Minsky et al. , 2003) . Once the two peers have reconciled their sets, they can filter out AuthorisedEntries that overwrite each other, and they can separately request any missing (suffixes of) Payloads . Going forward, we thus concentrate on the set reconciliation part only. To perform set reconciliation, we adapt the approach of range-based set reconciliation   ( Meyer , 2023) . Range-based set reconciliation solves the problem recursively. To reconcile two sets, one peer first computes a hash over all items in its set, and sends this fingerprint to the other peer. That peer then computes the fingerprint over its items as well. If the fingerprints match, they are done reconciling. Alfie and Betty produce equal fingerprints for all their AuthorisedEntries in a given 3dRange . If the fingerprints do not match, there are two options. First, the peer can split its set in half and then initiate set reconciliation for each half concurrently (by transmitting its hashes for both halves). Second, if the set is sufficiently small, the peer can instead simply transmit its items in the set. The other peer responds to this with all other items that it held in the set, completing the process of reconciliation. Alfie and Betty produce non-equal fingerprints. Alfie splits the 3dRange in two, yielding a 3dRange including AuthorisedEntries A and B , and another 3dRange including C , and sends these 3dRanges and their fingerprints to Betty . Betty produces a matching fingerprint for the first 3dRange . As the other, mismatched 3dRange includes so few AuthorisedEntries , Betty sends her AuthorisedEntries Q and Y to Alfie . In response, Alfie sends AuthorisedEntry C to Betty . Overall, the peers collaboratively drill down to the differences between their two sets in a logarithmic number of communication rounds, spending only little bandwidth on those regions of the original sets where they hold the same items. Note that peers can actually split sets into arbitrarily many subsets in each step. Splitting into more subsets per step decreases the total number of communication rounds. Split apart non-equal ranges to hone in on the locations of any differences, while disregarding equal ranges . 3d range-based set reconciliation takes these ideas and applies them to Willow. The core design decision is to delimit sets of LengthyAuthorisedEntries via 3dRanges . When a peer splits its 3dRanges , it is crucial for overall efficiency to not split based on volume (for example, by splitting the times in half numerically), but to split into subranges in which the peer holds roughly the same number of AuthorisedEntries . Let Fingerprint denote the type of hashes of LengthyAuthorisedEntries that the peers exchange. Upon receiving a 3dRangeFingerprint , a peer computes the Fingerprint over its local LengthyAuthorisedEntries in the same range. If it does not match, the peer either sends a number of 3dRangeFingerprints whose 3dRanges cover the 3dRange for which it received the mismatching Fingerprint . Or it replies with its 3dRangeEntrySet for that 3dRange , with the want_response flag set to true . To any such 3dRangeEntrySet , a peer replies with its own 3dRangeEntrySet , setting the want_response flag to false , and omitting all LengthyAuthorisedEntries it had just received in the other peer’s 3dRangeEntrySet . When a peer receives a 3dRangeFingerprint that matches the Fingerprint over its local LengthyAuthorisedEntries in the same 3dRange , the peer should reply with an empty 3dRangeEntrySet for that 3dRange , setting the want_response flag to false . This notifies the sender of the 3dRangeFingerprint that reconciliation has successfully concluded for the 3dRange . The peers might be interested in when they have successfully completed reconciliation of a particular 3dRange . Unfortunately, tracking all the 3dRanges that you receive and determining whether their union covers the particular 3dRange you are interested in is a comparatively expensive (and annoying) algorithmic problem. To circumvent this problem, peers can attach small bits of metadata to their messages: whenever a peer splits a 3dRange into a set of covering 3dRanges , the peer can simply attach some metadata to the final such subrange that it sends that indicates which of the 3dRanges sent by the other peer has now been fully covered by subranges. As long as both peers are accurate in supplying this metadata, they can maintain perfect information about the progress of reconciliation without the need for any sophisticated data structures. Peers should be cautious that a malicious peer could provide wildly inadequate metadata, but in general this is tolerable: a malicious peer can sabotage reconciliation in all sorts of interesting ways regardless. Fingerprinting 3d range-based set reconciliation requires the ability to hash arbitrary sets of LengthyAuthorisedEntries into values of some type Fingerprint . We now describe the technique described in the paper for computing these efficiently. The key idea is to ensure that the Fingerprint for a 3dRange can be assembled from precomputed Fingerprints of other, smaller 3dRanges . What we describe now is not mandatory for Willow, but it probably is a good idea. Given these building blocks, we define the function fingerprint from sets of LengthyAuthorisedEntries to Fingerprint : applying fingerprint to the empty set yields fingerprint_finalise ( fingerprint_neutral ) , applying fingerprint to a set containing exactly one LengthyAuthorisedEntry yields the same result as applying fingerprint_singleton to that LengthyAuthorisedEntry and then applying fingerprint_finalise , and applying fingerprint to any other set of LengthyAuthorisedEntries yields the result of applying fingerprint_singleton to all members of the set individually, then combining the resulting Fingerprints with fingerprint_combine (grouping and ordering do not matter because of associativity and commutativity), and then applying fingerprint_finalise .
