Understanding User-Generated Content and Communities in
Decentralized Web3 Networks: An Empirical Study on Nostr
SHUTONG QU, University of Washington, USA
CHUNYANG LI, The University of British Columbia, Canada
HONGZHOU CHEN, Common Knowledge Base Association, China
WEI CAI∗, University of Washington, USA
Decentralized social protocols such as Nostr introduce a new paradigm for user-generated content (UGC) in the Web3
era, where content production, dissemination, and reward mechanisms operate without centralized governance. This paper
presents one of the first large-scale empirical analyses of Nostr, based on 22.3 million user events collected from four major
publicly accessible relays. Guided by three research questions, we examine (1) the temporal and spatial distribution of user
participation, (2) the structural characteristics of decentralized UGC networks, and (3) thematic and incentive patterns in
content creation and Zap-based rewards. Our analysis shows rapid growth followed by long-tail stabilization, while the
interaction network remains highly modular and loosely connected, indicating fragmented yet persistent communities.
Embedding-based clustering of textual posts identifies ten clusters on several topics: technical discussions, ideological debates,
personal expression, community coordination, and media sharing, highlighting a hybrid ecosystem of social and technical
discourse. We further find that knowledge-oriented content in Clusters 1 and 5 receives higher Zap engagement, suggesting
the socialization of a primarily technical infrastructure. These findings advance the understanding of decentralized multimedia
ecosystems by linking network decentralization with observed participation and engagement patterns in the absence of
centralized moderation.

1 Introduction
In recent years, decentralized social networking protocols have emerged as alternatives to conventional Web2
platforms. Unlike centralized services such as X (formerly Twitter) and Reddit, where data ownership and
governance are controlled by a small number of corporate entities, decentralized systems aim to provide users
with greater autonomy, transparency, and censorship resistance [8, 32]. Among these systems, Nostr (Notes and
Other Stuff Transmitted by Relays) has attracted increasing attention as a lightweight and open protocol that
enables users to publish and distribute user-generated content (UGC) without centralized servers. Its architecture,

As a social multimedia protocol, Nostr supports not only short text posts but also links, images, and other
media references through extensible event formats. More broadly, its open and relay-based design creates a
valuable setting for studying content creation and dissemination under limited platform-level moderation and
algorithmic curation [13, 33]. This context is particularly relevant to UGC research in the Web3 era, where
ownership, authenticity, and decentralization are reshaping online participation [22].
Despite its growth, academic understanding of Nostr remains limited. Web2 platforms have been extensively
studied in terms of user behavior, network structure, and content dynamics, but comparable evidence for Nostr is
stillscarce[ 28].Thisgapisdrivenbyseveralmethodologicalchallenges.First,Nostr’sdistributedrelayarchitecture
complicates large-scale collection because different relays may contain overlapping yet incomplete records [37].
Second, event heterogeneity (e.g., profiles, notes, follows, and interactions) requires careful preprocessing to
construct analyzable datasets. Third, the absence of centralized platform metadata places greater responsibility
on researchers to reconstruct activity and interaction patterns from raw events.
To address these gaps, this paper presents one of the first large-scale empirical studies of Nostr. We analyze
22.3 million events collected from four major publicly accessible relays, covering both user activity and textual
content at scale. Our objective is to characterize how UGC is produced, circulated, and socially rewarded in a
decentralized environment.
A central mechanism in this ecosystem is Zap, a Lightning Network–based micro-payment used as social
feedback. In this study, a Zap is defined as a small, user-initiated payment explicitly associated with a social
target (e.g., a post or a user), rather than a generic asset transfer. Because Lightning payments are low-cost and
near-instant, Zap activity provides an observable signal of economic engagement embedded in social interaction.
Guided by these motivations, we investigate the following research questions:
• RQ1: How are Nostr events and users distributed over time across the decentralized network?
• RQ2: What are the structural characteristics of decentralized UGC in terms of network scale and connec-
tivity?
• RQ3: Which types of user-generated content are most frequently created and most likely to receive Zap
rewards?
Methodologically, we combine descriptive statistics with embedding-based content analysis. On the descriptive
side, we quantify activity levels, participation patterns, and interaction structures. On the analytical side, we use
text embeddings and clustering to identify recurring discourse patterns and examine how thematic groups relate
to Zap engagement. Together, these analyses provide an empirical account of Nostr as an evolving decentralized
UGC ecosystem.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on decentralized plat-
forms, Web3 ecosystems, and UGC analysis. Section 3 describes the data collection process and methodological
framework. Section 4 reports descriptive findings on user activity and content characteristics. Section 5 presents
embedding and clustering results, including theme-level engagement differences. Section 6 discusses implica-
tions for decentralized social systems and multimedia research. Section 7 concludes with limitations and future
directions.

2.1 User-Generated Content in Web2 Platforms
User-generated content (UGC) is a defining feature of Web2 platforms, where users act not only as information
consumers but also as producers and distributors of digital content. Platforms such as X, Reddit, YouTube, and

Facebook have enabled large-scale participatory communication and have provided rich empirical settings for
studying interaction patterns, community formation, and content circulation [5].
Prior research on centralized platforms has examined multiple dimensions of UGC production and engagement.
A substantial line of work analyzes behavioral regularities, including posting frequency, topical diversity, and
participation inequality [15, 27]. Another line investigates networked diffusion and collective attention, showing
that grassroots signals such as hashtags or community tags can accelerate topic emergence and coordinate
large-scale discussion [35]. Together, these studies establish core analytical baselines for understanding how
users create and amplify content in platform-mediated environments.
Another major stream of research examines platform governance and moderation in Web2 systems. Centralized
platforms can influence UGC visibility through recommendation algorithms and community-rule enforcement
[32].Althoughmoderationisoftenintendedtoreduceharmfulcontent,priorworknotesthatitmayalsointroduce
concerns about bias, censorship, and uneven power relations between users and platforms. Accordingly, existing
studies have examined whether and to what extent moderation is associated with democratic participation,
freedom of expression, and the perceived authenticity of online discourse [11].
Methodologically, Web2 research also faces access-related constraints that stem directly from the centralized
control of platform data and interfaces. Many studies rely on platform APIs with limited or changing availability,
creating potential sampling bias and reducing reproducibility [6]. In addition, algorithmic ranking can distort the
relation between produced content and observed content. These limitations motivate research on alternative
infrastructureswhereplatform-levelcontrolisweakerandwheretheobservabledatalayerisorganizeddifferently
from centralized social media.
2.2 UGC in Web3 Platforms
Web3socialsystemshaveemergedasanalternativeparadigmthatemphasizesdecentralization,protocolopenness,
and user sovereignty [18]. In contrast to centralized Web2 services, these systems distribute storage, identity,
and interaction functions across nodes, relays, or chains, thereby reducing dependence on a single platform
operator. For UGC research, this shift is important because the technical architecture affects not only content
dissemination but also the boundaries of what can be observed and measured.
Existing work on decentralized social media highlights both opportunities and constraints. On the one hand,
open protocols can improve data portability and reduce gatekeeping in content publication. On the other hand,
distributed infrastructures often produce fragmented observability, because there is no authoritative global state
and no guaranteed complete view of all activity. As a result, empirical claims must be framed with explicit
attention to sampling scope, relay and node coverage, and temporal availability of infrastructure. For example,
Mastodon exemplifies a federated model in which independent servers (”instances”) interoperate through the
ActivityPub protocol, enabling community-driven governance [16]. Bluesky, initiated by X’s former leadership,
proposes an open social-media protocol with an emphasis on portability and interoperability [12]. Lens Protocol
and Farcaster represent blockchain-based approaches that combine decentralized identity (DID), token-based
governance, and NFT-enabled content ownership.
These platforms share several key characteristics:
• Censorship resistance: Users are less exposed to unilateral platform bans or centralized moderation
decisions.
• Open participation: Users can join or host instances/relays without requiring approval from a single
corporate authority.
• User sovereignty: Users retain greater control over identity and data, often supported by cryptographic
mechanisms.

2.3 Embedding and Clustering for UGC Content
Beyond platform-level analysis, UGC research has advanced substantially through computational content analysis.
Researchershavedevelopedandappliedabroadsetofmethodstoextractstructurefromlarge-scaleuser-generated
text. Early studies commonly relied on topic-modeling approaches, such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) [2],
to uncover latent themes and trace topic evolution in online discourse. These methods have been widely applied
to conversations on X, Reddit, and online forums [14].
More recently, progress in representation learning has expanded the analytical depth of UGC studies. Word
embeddings (e.g., Word2Vec, GloVe) and transformer-based sentence embeddings, such as BERT and Sentence-
BERT (SBERT), provide richer semantic representations of short social text [20]. These embeddings are often
combined with clustering algorithms (e.g., k-means, hierarchical clustering, and spectral clustering) to identify
discourse communities, detect emerging trends, and map the semantic structure of large corpora [25]. Beyond
topic discovery, clustering-based content organization also supports downstream tasks such as recommendation,
misinformation detection, and community analysis [24]. Applications of these methods to multimedia UGC
have also grown, including multimodal embeddings that jointly encode textual, visual, and social signals. Such
approaches show that UGC clusters not only around topical similarity but also across media forms, reflecting the
inherently multimedia character of online interaction.
Despite these methodological advances, most prior studies remain centered on centralized platforms. These
environments typically provide convenient APIs and rich metadata (e.g., likes, shares, and follower counts), which
facilitate model training, validation, and interpretation. By contrast, decentralized platforms introduce distinct
methodological constraints:
• Fragmented data: different relays or instances may contain overlapping yet incomplete records.
• Sparse metadata: no centralized authority consistently provides platform-wide indicators such as follower
counts or trending hashtags.
• Heterogeneous events: protocols such as Nostr include profiles, social links, and posts in multiple event
formats.
As a result, it remains unclear how well established text-analysis and clustering pipelines transfer to decentral-
ized settings. Although reduced platform-level control may preserve more ”authentic” UGC signals, it also makes
data collection and interpretation more complex. Addressing this gap requires adapting established methods to
decentralized infrastructures and evaluating their applicability in practice.

3 Methodology
3.1 Research Object: Nostr
Nostr is a decentralized social networking protocol introduced in 2020 [22], designed as a censorship-resistant
and minimal alternative to Web2 platforms such as X. The protocol specifies only a lightweight event format
and cryptographic-signature verification, while delegating data storage and dissemination to independent relay
servers [34]. Users access the ecosystem through multiple clients (e.g., Damus, Amethyst, and Primal), which
read events from relays and publish new events to them. This architecture removes single points of failure and
reduces dependence on a central operator.
Compared with X’s centralized model, where platform operators moderate content, enforce policies, and
shape visibility through recommendation systems, Nostr distributes governance across autonomous relays and
client-side filtering mechanisms. This design increases resilience and openness, but it also introduces practical
challenges, including weaker coordination, uneven moderation practices, and ecosystem fragmentation.
Inthisstudy,Nostrservesasameaningfulresearchobjectbecauseitrepresentsanextremecaseofdecentralized
governance in user-generated content communities. By examining how UGC emerges and circulates in Nostr,
we can better understand the opportunities and trade-offs of decentralized governance models for online social
platforms.
3.2 Data Collection and Analysis
We relied on original data collected from a set of major publicly accessible Nostr relays to examine observable user
interactions, relational structures, and discourse dynamics. Our dataset spans from August 30, 2024 to September
14, 2025, encompassing 22.3 million events generated by more than 91,000 distinct authors observed on these
relays during the collection period.
3.2.1 Data Collection. Data were obtained through continuous crawling of major publicly accessible Nostr relays
(wss://relay.damus.io, wss://relay.nostr.band, wss://relay.primal.net, wss://nos.lol). Because Nostr has no global
state and no authoritative relay registry, this dataset represents the publicly observable relay layer during the
collection period rather than an exhaustive census of the entire protocol ecosystem. We collected several core
event kinds that jointly capture both the social and content layers of Nostr:
• Kind-1: text notes and discussions that constitute user-generated content.
• Kind-3: contact lists, capturing follow and social-graph relationships.
• Kind-7: reactions (“likes”), indicating engagement activity.
• Kind-0 and Kind-9735: user metadata and Lightning “Zap” (tipping) events [36].
Although Kind-1 events are primarily textual and are treated as text in our embedding and clustering pipeline,
they can also contain external links, images, and other media references in the content field. To account for this
multimedia aspect of Nostr, we additionally conduct a complementary descriptive analysis of multimedia usage
based on URL occurrences in Kind-1 posts. Other event kinds (e.g., relay recommendations, direct messages,
deletions, and extended custom kinds) were excluded because they either do not provide analyzable public
text, are encrypted and inaccessible to public crawlers, or mainly serve auxiliary protocol functions without
substantive semantic content.

Data Coverage. As a point-in-time reference for relay-layer observability, we performed an event-ID overlap

relays was 79,037, corresponding to an estimated observable coverage of 72.26% for that day. We report this as a
relay-layer overlap estimate, not as a full-network coverage claim.
We use Kind-1 for this check because, in practical relay operation, Kind-1 content is typically retained more
consistently, whereas many auxiliary kinds are more likely to be pruned under storage/load constraints.
3.2.2 Data Preprocessing. To improve data reliability in a decentralized setting, we implemented preprocessing
procedures tailored to this study:
• Deduplication across relays: We removed identical events mirrored by multiple servers. Deduplication
was conducted in two stages: within each relay, we retained the latest instance of duplicate messages;
across relays, we retained the earliest instance to preserve approximate cross-relay propagation timing.
• Spam and bot handling: We excluded messages with empty content but did not apply aggressive
spam/bot filtering. Automated activity is treated as part of the observed Nostr ecosystem, allowing us to
characterize platform behavior in the wild rather than a human-only subset.
• Temporal normalization:We aligned all timestamps to UTC to support consistent longitudinal analysis.
• Event parsing: We extracted structured fields and filtered unsupported or non-target kinds.
After preprocessing, the dataset was stored as normalized JSONL files for scalable analysis of textual and
network properties. Because bot and spam-like activities are included by design (except empty-content messages),
our results reflect the operational ecosystem as observed, including automation-driven structural and temporal
signals.
3.2.3 Data Interpretation. We employed a multi-level computational analysis pipeline:
• Text embedding and clustering: We generated sentence-level embeddings for Kind-1 posts using
SBERT, and used MiniBatch K-means for cluster assignment. UMAP (with PCA initialization) was applied
only for low-dimensional visualization and qualitative inspection, not for cluster assignment.
• Network analysis: Follow and reaction relations were modeled as directed graphs. We applied Louvain
community detection to identify densely connected user groups with coherent social structure.
• Engagement and reward analysis: Lightning Zap events (Kind-9735) were mapped to clustered content
to quantify engagement distribution and reward concentration across discourse communities.
We adopted SBERT to represent each post as a semantic vector, so that semantically similar messages are
positioned closer in embedding space. This representation is suitable for large volumes of short and informal
UGC, which is characteristic of decentralized social platforms such as Nostr.

For network structure analysis, we used Louvain community detection on the follow graph. Louvain maximizes

collection, content analysis, network modeling, and engagement interpretation.
4 Descriptive Results
This section characterizes temporal, structural, and economic activity patterns in the observable Nostr ecosystem.
The analysis is descriptive in scope: we report how activity surges manifest in the data, without making causal
claims about external drivers.
4.1 Nostr Events on Timeline
From August 30, 2024, to September 14, 2025, we collected 22,317,426 distinct events from four major Nostr
relays (relay.damus.io, relay.primal.net, nos.lol, and relay.nostr.band). Specifically, the textual events(kind-1) were
authored by 91,311 distinct users identifiable by unique public keys. In addition, 378,599 distinct public keys were
observed during the over one-year span (i.e., keys appearing in posting or interaction records), representing
the observable active population in our dataset. Overall activity remained relatively stable at the monthly level,
suggesting that the ecosystem reached a baseline of sustained engagement (Fig. 1). At the same time, we observe
two pronounced surges around May 1 and August 20, 2025, both of which persist over an extended period rather
than appearing as short-lived spikes [23].
To further characterize these fluctuations, we examined event-type dynamics. Around May 20, 2025 and June
20, 2025, kind-0 events (user profile updates) increased sharply (Fig. 1 (b) ), consistent with waves of account
initialization or profile refresh behavior. By contrast, although the August 20, 2025 activity increase substantially
raised total event volume, it was not accompanied by a comparable increase in profile updates. This pattern
suggests that the August surge is more consistent with increased activity from existing users rather than by
large-scale onboarding.
In terms of social connectivity, a large share of users updated follow lists in September 2024, after which
follow-list changes remained comparatively limited until August 2025 (Fig. 2). Importantly, the May and August
2025 surges in overall event volume (Fig. 1) were not accompanied by corresponding expansion in the follow
network. This indicates that later growth in UGC production was more consistent with activity-side change than
with structural expansion. In other words, existing ties continued to serve as the primary channels for content
production and circulation. The lack of sustained follow-list expansion is consistent with stabilization, or partial
saturation, of the social graph, in which users rely more on established connections than on continuously forming
new ones. This pattern reflects a decoupling between network structure and activity intensity, which is plausible
in decentralized systems where information diffusion is less dependent on globally centralized connectivity.
Finally, we examine Zap events, which represent monetary interactions among users on Nostr. As shown in
Fig. 3a, the daily Zap-event pattern broadly tracks the overall activity trajectory (Fig. 1), including visible bursts
in May and August 2025. However, Zap value exhibits strong skewness: a small number of very large transfers
disproportionately affect total transaction volume. Consequently, while total daily Zap volume occasionally

4.2 Nostr Events on Spatial Distribution
To characterize the spatial dispersion of user-generated events across Nostr’s infrastructure, we examined daily
message volumes on four major public relays from August 30, 2024 to September 14, 2025. Because the same
event may be mirrored across multiple relays, we counted only one deduplicated instance per event, following
the automatic selection of the default Python Nostr relay manager, to avoid double counting. The resulting
unique-event totals are 6.48M on wss://relay.nostr.band, 5.98M on wss://nos.lol, 5.44M on wss://relay.primal.net,
and 4.42M on wss://relay.damus.io.
As shown in Fig. 3b, the temporal distribution reveals both persistent inequality and dynamic variation across
relays. The relay wss://nos.lol maintains a relatively stable but moderate message volume, indicating continuous
yet limited usage. In contrast, wss://relay.nostr.band and wss://relay.primal.net show multiple surges after May
2025 and become the most active relay nodes in the observed period. The relay wss://relay.damus.io remains
consistently lower than the others before May 2025 (roughly an order-of-magnitude gap), but then exhibits rapid
growth and gradually approaches the other relays. This pattern may indicate later-stage adoption and increasing
popularity among Nostr users.
Overall, these trends suggest that Nostr traffic is concentrated in a small set of relays [34], implying a semi-
centralized spatial structure within an otherwise decentralized protocol. The uneven distribution further indicates
that user activity and content propagation depend strongly on the operational stability and adoption level of key
relay nodes, rather than being evenly distributed across the network.
4.3 UGC Characteristics
Previous analyses indicate that the Nostr activity surges in May and August 2025 temporally coincided with
periods of intensified public discourse around platform governance. Within this context, the Nostr corpus shows
a relatively higher concentration of terms related to decentralization, autonomy, and technical infrastructure,
suggesting a communicative orientation that differs from centralized platforms [4].
To examine how this broader shift is reflected in UGC, we compare linguistic, emotional, and identity-related
characteristics of UGC on Nostr and X in Fig. 4. This comparison provides a descriptive view of how platform
affordances and community orientation may be associated with different discourse patterns in decentralized
versus centralized environments.

4.3.1 Topical Orientation of UGC. On Nostr, the most frequent words (e.g., bitcoin, btc, mempool, block, sats)
indicate a strong focus on cryptocurrency and protocol-related discussions. Other high-frequency terms, such as
files, image, and build, suggest frequent sharing of technical details and self-developed tools, consistent with
participation by technically oriented users. Terms such as nostr, primal, and blossom further point to discussion
of clients and relay infrastructure, indicating that many users also engage with maintenance and improvement of
the protocol itself. Although words such as sports, trump, and world appear, they are far less frequently and not
dominant themes. Overall, the lexical profile suggests that Nostr functions as a specialized technical community
in which most UGC centers on infrastructure and software-related discourse.
In contrast, X exhibits a broader topical spectrum. Frequent temporal words (today, time, week, year) and
emotional terms (thank, love, happy, please, support) reflect everyday communication and social interaction.
Terms such as team, game, and show point to entertainment, public events, and shared experiences. This broader
lexical spread suggests that X functions as a general-purpose social space, where UGC is more strongly tied to
daily life and social exchange than to technical collaboration.
4.3.2 Interactional Style and Emotional Expression. The Nostr vocabulary is primarily practical and information-
oriented. Common verbs such as build, get, make, and use indicate that communication is often used to exchange
knowledge or discuss technical implementation. Explicit affective expressions (e.g., good, thanks, like) appear
less frequently, suggesting comparatively limited emphasis on interpersonal affective signaling. This pattern is

5 Embedding and Clustering Results
Whereas Section 4 characterizes platform-level activity patterns and community structure, this section examines
the semantic composition of user-generated content (UGC) on Nostr. Our goal is to identify which message
categories are most prevalent and which categories are more likely to receive Zap engagement. To do so,
we convert textual content into high-dimensional semantic embeddings, apply unsupervised clustering with
MiniBatch K-means, and then compare cluster-level prevalence and engagement patterns [19, 20, 25].
5.1 Embedding Generation
We use the Sentence-Transformers library in Python to encode each post as a dense semantic vector. Specifically,
we adopt the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model, a Transformer-based encoder distilled from BERT, which produces 384-
dimensional embeddings. This representation places semantically similar texts closer in vector space and provides
a quantitative basis for measuring thematic similarity. In total,𝑁 = 6, 696, 205 Kind-1 posts are embedded,
yielding an embedding matrix of shape(6696205, 384).
To verify that the representation captures meaningful variation in topic and expression, we conducted a
preliminarycosine-similaritycheckonrandomlysampledtexts.Semanticallyrelatedmessages,includingtechnical
updates and discussions of decentralization, consistently showed higher similarity scores (typically> 0.8). This
result supports the semantic validity of the embedding space used in subsequent clustering analysis.
5.2 Clustering Method
Given that the embedding space contains over 6 million samples, we applied MiniBatch K-means to identify
latent topic groups in the semantic space. The number of clusters (𝑘 = 10) was selected using the elbow method
together with silhouette analysis, balancing interpretability and internal coherence. Each resulting cluster is
therefore treated as a distinct thematic type of UGC.
To support intuitive interpretation of the clustered semantics, we visualized the embedding geometry through
a two-stage dimensionality-reduction pipeline. We first applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce
the original 384-dimensional sentence embeddings to 50 principal components, preserving most global variance
while reducing computational cost. We then applied Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to
map these representations into two dimensions for visualization. UMAP was used only for visualization (not for
cluster assignment). The parameters were set to𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑏𝑜𝑟𝑠 = 15, 𝑚𝑖𝑛𝑑𝑖𝑠𝑡 = 0.1, and𝑚𝑒𝑡𝑟𝑖𝑐 = 𝑐𝑜𝑠𝑖𝑛𝑒 , which provide
a practical balance between local neighborhood preservation and global separability.
For clearer plotting, we randomly sampled 100,000 representative points from the full dataset and colored them
by cluster ID (0–9). Each color corresponds to one semantic cluster produced by the unsupervised model.
To evaluate clustering quality, we computed the Silhouette Coefficient and used it as a quantitative indicator of
separation structure. In addition, manual inspection of representative samples from each cluster showed that
semantically related content was grouped together, supporting the interpretability of the clustering results.

The ten clusters described in subsection 5.3 reveal not only topical diversity but also structural properties of Nostr
as a decentralized user-generated content (UGC) ecosystem. Considering their linguistic composition, content

At the affective-social layer, Clusters 0, 4, and 8 form the emotional core of the network. These clusters are
dominated by Japanese sentences and emoji-heavy short messages, accounting for roughly one third of the text

a phatic function similar to “likes” or “reactions” on centralized platforms, but without algorithmic prioritization.

where distribution is strongly shaped by centralized recommendation systems, Nostr relies on distributed relays
and voluntary reposting. This design supports many-to-many diffusion, in which users simultaneously act as
producers, distributors, and validators of content. The resulting structure is a horizontally organized knowledge
network that supports identity performance, thematic affiliation, and multilingual interaction without explicit
centralized moderation.

6 Improving Nostr Networks and Future Work
This section discusses potential directions for improving the design and governance of Nostr-style decentralized
social systems, with a focus on UGC production, distribution, and moderation. Building on Sections 4 and 5,
we derive exploratory design implications from three recurring empirical patterns: (1) activity surges without
corresponding follow-network expansion (Section 4.1), (2) relay and community-level structural fragmentation
(Sections 4.2 and 4.4), and (3) semantic layering and uneven Zap allocation across content categories (Sections
5.3-5.5). These implications are intended as data-grounded directions rather than prescriptive rules.
6.1 Enhancing the Production and Distribution of UGC
As shown in Sections 4.1 and 4.4, increases in content activity are not matched by comparable expansion of follow
ties, and interactions remain concentrated within modular communities. Section 4.2 further shows relay-level
concentration and uneven traffic allocation. Taken together, these patterns suggest that content visibility on
Nostr is dominated by local relay and community exposure, while cross-network diffusion remains limited.

centralized ranking.
6.2 Improving UGC Quality
Section 5.5 shows that Zap engagement is sparse overall and concentrated in clusters associated with affective
and identity-oriented content, while technical or automated clusters receive comparatively little reward. This
pattern indicates that economic feedback, by itself, is not a reliable proxy for informational quality or factual
validity. It also suggests that quality support on Nostr should not depend on top-down moderation or single-score
reputation systems.
Because Nostr lacks centralized verification and moderation, credibility support must remain consistent with
decentralized design constraints. In this context, credibility mechanisms are better treated as optional, client-side,
and interpretive tools that assist user judgment, rather than as global enforcement infrastructure.
A feasible approach is local trust inference at the client level. Instead of imposing a platform-wide trust graph,
clients can offer optional ranking or filtering layers based on user-controlled signals, such as interaction history,
repeated co-engagement patterns, or local endorsement relationships. These layers affect interface presentation
only;theydonotalterprotocol-leveleventvalidityoraccessrights.Trustisthereforemodeledasalocalviewpoint,
not a system-wide rule.
Additional soft authenticity signals can complement this approach. For example, clients can expose provenance
metadata and publication patterns to help users distinguish original content from mass-generated or synthetic
posts. Client-side AI-assisted indicators may also flag likely duplicates or suspicious dissemination patterns while
preserving privacy through on-device processing [9, 26].
Together, these directions suggest a trustless authenticity layer that strengthens content interpretation without
reintroducing centralized gatekeeping. Rather than importing an external Web-of-Trust authority, Nostr can sup-
port emergent and user-defined credibility models that remain dynamic, interpretable, and reversible, consistent
with the protocol ethos of “verify locally, cooperate voluntarily.”
6.3 Community Governance and Incentive Design
Section 4.4 reports a highly modular follow structure with strong intra-community cohesion, limited inter-
community connectivity, and bridge effects concentrated in a small set of hubs. This indicates that coordination
in Nostr is primarily local and polycentric, not global and hierarchical. Under this structure, governance is more
likely to emerge from repeated local interaction than from platform-wide rule enforcement.
Given these observed constraints, sustainable UGC participation may depend on lightweight coordination
mechanisms that improve interaction quality without introducing centralized authority. One practical path is
local self-regulation: users form interest-based clusters through voluntary following and tagging, while relay

Incentive design should follow the same principle. Micro-rewards such as Lightning tips and peer recognition

constructive participation and collective accountability while avoiding centralized control over visibility.
From this perspective, governance in Nostr is better interpreted as emergent coordination than rule imposition.
Social norms and quality expectations are produced through repeated interaction, local filtering, and voluntary
incentives. This framing aligns with the empirical structure observed in Section 4.4 and with the decentralized

6.4 Semantic Analytics and UGC-Driven Research Opportunities
Section 5 shows that embedding-based analysis can recover interpretable semantic structure from large-scale
decentralizedUGC,evenwhenglobalseparabilityislimited.Subsections5.4and5.5furthershowthatsemanticcat-

systems provide a productive setting for advancing semantic analytics beyond centralized-platform assumptions.
First, embedding-based community discovery can reveal latent thematic organization not directly visible
in explicit follow graphs [10]. Mapping high-dimensional representations of posts may clarify how semantic
similarity relates to diffusion, boundary formation, and local polarization across relay-layer subnetworks.
Second, longitudinal semantic analysis can track cycles of convergence and divergence in collective attention.
This can help explain when decentralized discourse stabilizes around shared frames and when it fragments into
parallel topical streams.
Third, integrating behavioral traces (e.g., relay activity patterns and micropayment flows) with off-chain
semantic representations can support end-to-end modeling of UGC lifecycles in Web3 environments, from
production and circulation to recognition and reward.
Finally, AI-assisted co-creation introduces an additional research frontier. Transparent participation of genera-
tive agents in decentralized communities may reshape authorship, creativity, and accountability, and therefore
requires new methodological frameworks for attribution and governance analysis.
Overall, these directions provide a rigorous and extensible research agenda for decentralized UGC ecosys-
tems. They connect computational social science with distributed protocol design and open a broad space for
interdisciplinary work in Web3 communication systems.
7 Limitations and Conclusion
7.1 Methodological and Data Limitations
Although this study uses a large-scale dataset collected from multiple major public relays, several methodological
limitations remain. First, because Nostr is decentralized and relay-distributed, no single collection can fully
represent the entire global ecosystem; our data therefore reflect the historically observable relay layer during
the collection window and may contain sampling bias. Second, while the dataset covers over one year, it is
still bounded to a fixed observation period and cannot fully capture longer-term structural and behavioral

7.2 Conceptual and Theoretical Boundaries
From a theoretical perspective, our analytical framework necessarily introduces measurement abstractions (e.g.,
connectivity, modularity, relay concentration) that can be interpreted as a mild re-centralization of observation.
This may appear in tension with Nostr’s ideological emphasis on decentralization and autonomy. At the same
time, the empirical results also reveal a persistent governance trade-off in Web3 social systems: how to sustain
coordination and content quality without restoring centralized authority. We do not treat this tension as a design
failure; rather, we view it as a structural condition of decentralized social ecosystems.
7.3 Conclusion and Implications
This study provides a systematic empirical analysis of UGC dynamics and community structure in Nostr-based
decentralized social networks. Across temporal activity patterns, relay-layer distribution, semantic clustering, and
Zap engagement, the results show that decentralized communities are shaped jointly by local network structure,
communication layers, and user-driven feedback mechanisms. Compared with Web2 platforms, the absence of
centralized moderation and recommendation does not eliminate coordination; instead, coordination emerges
through relay-level organization, community norms, and lightweight social-economic signals. These findings
contribute to a bridge between social-computing analysis and decentralized protocol design, and they support the
need for minimal, voluntary coordination mechanisms that preserve user autonomy. Overall, this work extends
current understanding of Web3 UGC ecosystems and offers evidence-based implications for future system design,
governance practice, and interdisciplinary research.
